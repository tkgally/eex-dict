A **review cycle** gives existing entries a second reading by the two outside reviewer models (reviewer-a and reviewer-b), to catch what their first reading missed. First I read each entry myself, looking for missing senses, idioms, and learner errors. Three changes came from that reading:

- *governor* gained a fifth sense: the person in charge of a country's central bank, or one of the group who control it. The entry's opening summary (the "core idea") now mentions it.

- *grandfather* listed *grandmother* both in its word family and under "see also"; the second was a repeat and was removed.

Then the two reviewer models read every field of all twelve entries again. *Grandfather* and *grass* came back clean.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

They raised 27 issues, 17 of them blocking (must be settled before publication). I logged 27 decisions in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`: 12 applied and 15 rejected. None went to the owner.

Applied:

- *goal*: the entry said *in goal* (playing as the player who guards the goal) is mainly British. It is used in American sport too, so that claim is gone, and the meaning is now a short note on the example *Who's playing in goal today?*
- *girl*: the sense *the girls* (a woman's female friends) said it is always used with **the**. It is now "usually with **the**, sometimes with a possessive (*my girls*)". A repeated remark about *girl* sounding disrespectful was removed from sense 3, since the usage note already says it.
- *goat*: the usage note now says a male goat is *often* called a *billy goat* and a female a *nanny goat* in informal use, and adds that farmers usually say *buck* and *doe*. The weak "see also" link to *farm* was removed.
- *goods*: the note on British *goods train* now says the British use the word for things carried in large amounts by train or truck, and that American English says *a freight train*. The idiom *damaged goods*, said of a person, is now marked "disapproving".
- *government*: *in government*, meaning "being the party in power", is now labelled British; American English usually says *in power*.
- *grain*: the idiom *go against the grain* now puts the main meaning first (be against your nature or beliefs) and keeps the second (be different from what is usual at a time). The core idea now names stone along with wood and cloth, to match sense 4.
- *grammar*: sense 2 is now *how well a person follows the rules of a language when they speak or write*. The old wording had an unclear *they*.

Rejected:

- reviewer-b asked for *GOAT* (greatest of all time) as a sense of *goat*. It is an abbreviation and would need its own entry, not a sense here.
- reviewer-a wanted a "historical" label on governors sent to rule distant areas. Such governors still exist in some territories.
- reviewer-a wanted *an own goal* labelled British. The sports meaning is used everywhere; only the figurative meaning is mainly British, and the phrase's explanation already says so.
- reviewer-a called the young-woman sense of *girl* neutral, not informal. In formal writing an adult is called a *woman*, so the label stays.
- reviewer-a objected three times to the *grave* note that the noun and the adjective *grave* (very serious) share a spelling but not a meaning. The note describes present meaning, not origin, and translators do need different words for the two.
- Smaller ones: two learner errors on *grain* stay (both are corrected with the headword); *God willing* keeps a definition that can replace it in a sentence.
