## What this cycle did

The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose a **build** cycle: new entries from the queue of words still to be written, most frequent words ("band 1") first. The queue is working through band-1 adverbs in alphabetical order, and this cycle took the next twelve: *hard*, *hardly*, *here*, *highly*, *honestly*, *how*, *immediately*, *in*, *indeed*, *inside*, *instead*, and *just*.

Several of these are among the most used words in English (*how*, *just*, *here*, *in*), and each needed several senses: *just*, for example, has six (*a moment ago*, *exactly*, *only*, *almost not*, *now*, and the use that makes a statement stronger), plus the phrases *just about* and *just now*. Each entry was written from my own knowledge under the house style guide, then sent through the full checking pipeline. The dictionary now has 2,423 entries, all published.

All 24 pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel on the first try. The comparative forms of *hard* (*harder*, *hardest*) come from the dictionary's table of exceptions.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewers ("reviewer-a" and "reviewer-b", two different language models) raised 55 issues, 41 of them blocking. I accepted 38 and rejected 17. Reviewer-b's reply on *instead* could not be read and was asked again; the second reply was complete and raised nothing. The main changes:

- **instead**: the entry taught *instead of* (*tea instead of coffee*) in its collocations and two learner errors. Under the house rule of one part of speech per entry, *instead of* is a preposition with its own entry; this adverb entry now only points to it in one sentence of the usage note.
- **in** (adverb): two collocations, *hand something in* and *move in*, are phrasal verbs, which get entries of their own; they were replaced. A learner error said *come in the house* is wrong, but it is normal in American speech; it was replaced by a real error (*She isn't in home*, corrected to *She isn't in* or *at home*). The sense *arrived* now covers *the results are in*.
- **highly**: the explanation said *highly* goes only with words that judge (*highly successful*). Reviewer-a showed that *highly likely* and *highly recommend* do not fit that rule. The explanation and the learner-error note now say only that *highly* is not used with simple feelings (*very happy*, not *highly happy*).
- **just**: *just now* meaning *at this moment* is mainly British, and the entry now says so. Two "informal" labels were removed where the use is ordinary.
- **here**: the phrase *here you are* repeated sense 2 (giving something to someone); it was folded into that sense.
- **hardly**, **honestly**, **indeed**: an unneeded "formal" label was removed from *hardly*, and two learner errors that were not clearly errors (*Honestly speaking*; *Indeed, I like it*) were dropped.

Main rejections: four times reviewer-a asked for *highly paid* and *immediately affected* to be filed as "adverb + past participle", a collocation type that does not exist in the dictionary's closed list; reviewer-b again asked for a core idea that is not a sentence, which the house rules do not allow.

After the review, the cross-reference tool added eleven links back to the new entries. I checked each: two landed on the wrong sense (the *just* link in *exactly* was on its "reply to agree" sense, and the *immediately* link in *directly* was on its "straight to a place" sense) and were moved by hand. A family link between *in* and *inside* was removed in both directions, because reviewer-b was right that *in* is not a form of *inside*.
