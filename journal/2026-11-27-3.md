# Run report, 2026-11-27 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: closure

**What this cycle did.** The tool that picks each cycle's job (the selector) chose *closure*: writing entries for words the dictionary already uses or points to but has no entry for yet. Closure takes rows in a fixed order of sources. The first source, words used inside definitions, is empty, so the rows came from the second, *family*: words that an existing entry lists as a related form (for example, **afford** lists **affordable**). Twelve were claimed, the cycle's limit, all band-2 adjectives (band 2 is the second-most-important frequency group).

The twelve new entries:

- **affordable**: not too expensive, so that most people can pay for it. A note says how it differs from **cheap**, which can suggest poor quality.
- **aged**: three senses: having a stated age (*children aged six to ten*), very old (formal, *her aged parents*), and food and drink kept for a long time to improve it (*aged cheese*). The "very old" sense has two syllables (*AY-jid*), which is recorded as a second pronunciation.
- **amazed**, **annoyed**, **annoying**: each with its prepositions (*amazed at*, *annoyed with someone*, *annoyed about something*) and, for **amazed** and **annoyed**, the common learner mistake of using the *-ing* form for the person who feels the emotion.
- **associated**: two senses, both used only before a noun (*the associated costs*; *an associated company*). A usage note explains that *the costs associated with moving* is the verb **associate** used in the passive and sends the reader there.
- **athletic**: strong and good at sports, and connected with sports (*athletic shoes*), with notes on the British **athletics** and on the moving stress (*ATH-leet*, *ath-LET-ik*).
- **balanced**: having parts in the right amounts (*a balanced diet*), fair to all sides (*a balanced report*), and calm and not easily upset (of a person).
- **beneficial**: having a good effect, with a stress note.
- **bloody**: covered in blood, violent (*a bloody battle*), and the British and Australian informal word that adds force (*I've lost my bloody keys*), with an explanation that many people hear it as a mild swear word. The same word before an adjective (*bloody good*) is an adverb; it has been added to the queue as its own entry.
- **boiling**: of water at the point of turning to steam, and, informally, very hot (*It's boiling in here*).
- **bottom**: in the lowest position (*the bottom shelf*) and lowest in a list or level (*the bottom group*).

**Checks.** The word-form tool produced the forms from its rules; only **bloody** has its own comparative (*bloodier*, *bloodiest*). The pronunciation panel (three models plus an open pronouncing dictionary, consulted only to check) agreed with every transcription except **aged**: the panel preferred the two-syllable form, while the open dictionary agrees with the one-syllable form we use as the main one. The entry keeps both, the second as a variant, and carries a flag saying the pronunciation was disputed. One line in the curator file records this for the owner.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 64 issues, 2 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). Both came from reviewer B on **associated**: my first draft treated *associated with* (*the risks associated with sport*) as the adjective, but it is the verb in the passive, and the dictionary keeps each part of speech in its own entry. I agreed and rebuilt the first sense around the uses before a noun. Of the logged decisions on minor issues, I applied 31 and rejected 10. Applied: clearer definitions for **affordable** and **aged**, *a balanced diet* moved first, more natural examples, house marks in translator notes, better signposts, and a repetitive note in **bloody** removed. Rejected: reviewer A's repeated claim that *comparative with more* is not a grammar code (it is one of the house codes, a settled question), a request for a "rude" attitude label (no such label exists; the explanation says it), and a request to cover *associated with a charity* (the passive verb again).

The cross-reference tool left seven back-links whose sense it could not choose; I placed them by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.34 for this cycle, almost all reviewer B and the pronunciation panel; US$1.48 of US$15 for the day.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. The draft of **associated** shows a recurring risk with past-participle adjectives: it is easy to write the passive verb into the adjective entry.

**What is next.** The next cycle, if the clock allows, takes whatever the selector picks; more family rows remain for closure.

**What needs the owner.** Only the **aged** pronunciation line in the curator file, which can wait for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: rereading existing entries. Its order of work puts entries still in draft first (there were none), then open entry reports from the public (none), then the *coverage pass*. That pass rereads the most important band-1 words, one batch at a time, and checks that every common sense and idiom is there. This cycle took the next four words on that list: **learn** (verb), **let** (verb), **lie** (verb, in the sense of lying down), and **light** (noun).

**What I added before the reviewers read them.**

- **let**: five idioms that earlier runs had noted as missing: *let's face it* (something unpleasant has to be accepted), *let's roll* (time to start or leave), *let rip* (do something with full energy, or shout angrily), *let something slip* (say a secret by mistake), and *let yourself go* (relax freely, or stop taking care of how you look).
- **lie**: a subsense for the future-time use, *lie ahead* and *lie in store* (*Who knows what lies in store for us?*).
- **light**: three phrases, *make light of something*, *a leading light*, and *at first light*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 46 issues, 2 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). Both came from reviewer A and made one point: in **lie**, the sense for things lying on a surface repeats the sense for a person lying down, so the two should be merged. I disagreed with the merge. A person lying on a bed and a letter lying on a table are said with different verbs in many languages, and the style guide treats that as a reason to keep senses apart. The reviewer was right that the two definitions read almost the same, though. Sense 1 now reads *have your body flat on a bed, the ground, or another surface*, and the sense for things reads *be on the ground or another surface and stay there*. I logged the definition change as applied and the merge as rejected.

Of the minor issues, I applied 30 and rejected 10. Many concerned the house rule "one fact, one field": the same fact should not be stated in two notes.

- **let**: the usage note repeated the word forms and a learner mistake, and a translator's note repeated the grammar. Both were removed.
- **lie** and **light**: a translator's note in each that repeated the usage note was removed.
- **learn**: one learner mistake looked invented, and I removed it. The British past form *learnt*, which the word-forms note already gives, was taken out of the usage note.

Reviewer A also found real gaps. I added *let someone go*, meaning to end someone's job, and *learn to live with it*, meaning to come to accept something over time. I also widened the definition of *let something slide*, made the two meanings of *let yourself go* part of its definition, and took the headword out of two **light** definitions where it had defined itself. A rejected example: reviewer A asked for *lie in wait*, but the dictionary keys an idiom to its first noun, here **wait**, so it belongs in that entry.

**Spend.** US$0.25 this cycle; US$1.73 of US$15 for the day.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with **like** (verb), **little**, **long**, and **matter**.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: new entries taken in order from the queue, the ordered list of words still waiting for an entry. The limit was twelve, but I took six so that each one could be written in full and the cycle could still finish safely. All six are band-2 adjectives (band 2 is the second-most-important frequency group).

- **aggressive**: angry and threatening (*aggressive behavior*), very determined to win (*an aggressive sales campaign*), and, in medicine, an illness that spreads fast, with a subsense for strong, fast treatment (*aggressive treatment*). A note for translators warns that in business and sport the word can be neutral or approving, so a word meaning only "hostile" may give the wrong tone.
- **alternative**: another choice (*an alternative route*), and different from the usual way (*alternative medicine*, *an alternative lifestyle*), with a subsense for energy that comes from the sun, the wind, and so on, rather than from coal, oil, or gas. A usage note explains that American English also uses **alternate** for the first sense.
- **ambitious**: of a person who wants very much to succeed, including wanting success for someone else (*my parents were ambitious for me*), and of a plan that is hard to achieve. A note for translators says the English word is often approving.
- **ancient**: from thousands of years ago (*ancient history*), very old (*an ancient oak tree*), and the informal, humorous use (*I feel ancient next to these students*).
- **appropriate**: suitable for a situation (*not appropriate for a job interview*), with the phrase *where appropriate* and a note that the related verb is said differently at the end (*-pree-uht* for the adjective, *-pree-ayt* for the verb).
- **artificial**: made by people, often as a copy of something natural (*artificial flowers*). It has a subsense for limits or deadlines set by a rule rather than by need (*an artificial deadline*). A second sense covers what is pretended rather than sincere (*an artificial smile*).

**Checks.** The word-form tool and the pronunciation panel agreed with every form and transcription.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 24 issues, 1 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). Reviewer A was right that my *alternative energy* subsense was wrong. It said "not made by burning coal, oil, or gas", but some alternative fuels are burned. It now reads "coming from the sun, the wind, or other things instead of coal, oil, or gas". Of the minor issues, I applied 18 and rejected 6. I applied:

- a subsense for *aggressive treatment*, which no sense covered;
- a wider definition of **ambitious**, so that it fits *ambitious for someone*;
- *where appropriate*, which was missing;
- American *in college* in place of *at university*;
- *truly ancient* in place of the unnatural *very ancient*;
- a corrected usage note on **alternate**, whose meaning *every second one* is shared by both varieties.

The rejections were the settled house questions: *comparative with more* is a grammar code; *humorous* is a register label in the dictionary's closed list; the rule for a subsense is to state its restriction (*used of energy and fuel*) in its explanation.

**Spend.** US$0.16 this cycle; US$1.89 of US$15 for the day.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. The *alternative* error is worth remembering. A short definition can rule out too much: "not made by burning" also excluded fuels that are burned.

**What is next.** The queue continues after **artificial**; the coverage pass continues with **like**, **little**, **long**, and **matter**.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new beyond the **aged** pronunciation line from cycle 1.
