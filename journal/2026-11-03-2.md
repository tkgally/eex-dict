# Run report, 2026-11-03 (second run)

## Cycle 1: originality

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks each cycle's kind of work) forced an originality check, because this is the 660th run and the check comes every twentieth. An originality check takes ten definitions at random from the dictionary and asks whether any of them reads as if it were copied from a published dictionary. The dictionary must be original work: its definitions are written from the model's own knowledge, never from another dictionary. Each sampled definition gets an exact-phrase web search, and the same ten go to one reviewer model (reviewer B, a Google model) with the question "copied, original, or cannot tell?". The reviewer may only answer "copied" if it can quote the published wording.

Before the check, the usual start-of-run housekeeping found nothing to do: no pull requests left open by an earlier run, no stray branches, nothing in the owner's inbox, and no reader reports about entries.

**What the check found.** No exact-phrase search found any of the ten definitions word for word anywhere. Reviewer B answered "cannot tell" for all ten and named no source, so the verdicts rest on the searches. When a search showed a dictionary with similar wording, I searched again with the headword and the opening words to see the published text. That turned up two real problems, both with one well-known learner's dictionary:

- **closet** (noun): our definition opened *a small room or a space in a wall, with a door*, which is that dictionary's opening almost word for word. The rest differed, but the distinctive opening was the same.
- **temper** (noun): our second meaning, *a short period when someone is very angry*, was a close paraphrase of that dictionary's *a short period of feeling very angry*. Our third meaning, *the way someone is feeling at a particular time*, followed the same dictionary's wording for its mood meaning.

The other eight were judged fine: five original, three "generic overlap" (short, plain definitions that any dictionary would word much the same way, such as *the part of a pair of pants that covers your leg*). The full table is in `reviews/originality/2026-11-03.md`.

**What I rewrote.** The rewrites were written from scratch, not by swapping words in the published text:

- closet: *a space built into a wall and closed off by a door, used for storing clothes and other things*.
- temper, meaning 2: *a state of strong anger that comes on suddenly and does not last long*.
- temper, meaning 3: *the mood someone is in at a given moment, especially how angry or calm they are*.

Both entries then went through the full checking pipeline: format validation, the inflection and pronunciation checks (all verified), and the two-reviewer panel. The panel raised 18 issues, none of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). I applied 8 and rejected 10, each with a logged reason.

Applied: *walk-in* in *a walk-in closet* is an adjective, so that collocation moved to the "adjective + noun" group; a fact about the British words *wardrobe* and *cupboard* appeared twice in the closet entry (once in the word-comparison box and again in a culture note), so the culture note now says only what is new; the idiom *in the closet* is now defined as a state (*not telling other people that ...*), and its explanation says "gay or bisexual" to match the definition; *rage* was removed as a synonym of a short burst of temper, since rage is stronger and need not be brief.

Rejected: requests to hand-mark the headword in examples (the website does that automatically); a request to add *a skeleton in the closet* here (by the dictionary's rule an idiom lives under its first noun, so it belongs under *skeleton*); a claim that *in a good temper* is dated (it is still in use, especially in British English); and a request to change the marking of *lose your temper* in a note (the style guide marks a phrase named as a phrase that way).

**Spend.** About US$0.07 (the reviewer call and the panel), leaving US$14.31 of today's US$15 budget.

**What did not work.** The exact-phrase search, which the checklist asks for, found neither problem on its own: both were found only by a second, looser search. I have added that second search to the instructions in `NEXT.md` for the next check. Reviewer B's ten "cannot tell" answers added nothing this time.

**What is next.** The next originality check is due in twenty runs. The scheduler will choose the next cycle's work.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle. The open items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" are unchanged.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build: twelve new entries from the queue of words still to write (`headwords/queue.tsv`), in the order set by the build-order decision. All are in band 2, the second of the dictionary's five frequency groups. Two are verbs that existing entries already pointed to, **deposit** and **insult**. The other ten are nouns: **agent**, **anniversary**, **announcement**, **ant**, **anxiety**, **appeal**, **approach**, **arms** (weapons), **arrival**, and **aspect**. Each was written from scratch. Each then went through the full pipeline: a format check; generated word forms (plurals, past tenses); a pronunciation check, in which three other models and an open pronouncing dictionary all agreed on all 24 transcriptions; and the two-reviewer panel (two models from different companies read every field). Afterwards came my adjudication, meaning I read each reviewer issue and decided to apply, reject, or escalate it.

**What the reviewers found.** They raised 58 issues. Eight were blocking, which means they must be settled before an entry can be published. I applied 50 and rejected 8. Nothing was escalated. The blocking issues were all fair:
- The usage note in *arms* said that "an arm" is always a part of the body. That is false: a chair, the sea, and an organization can all have an arm. The note now says only the useful fact: in the weapons meaning there is no singular, and one gun is *a weapon*.
- *arrival* had an example about spring under a meaning defined as "something new". Spring comes every year, so the example went, and the definition is now *the coming or first appearance of something new*.
- *announcement* was marked "countable or uncountable" only because of one example about the act of announcing. That example was removed, and the meaning is now countable.
- The second meaning of *deposit* (*he deposited his bags on the floor*) was labelled formal, yet its explanation called it humorous. Both the label and the explanation went.
- The idiom *a free agent* hid its sports use (a player free to join any team) in an explanation field, which the style guide keeps for function words. The definition now covers it.

**Added because a reviewer noticed a gap:**
- a meaning of *deposit*: pay part of the price first, or pay money you get back later (*deposit a month's rent*)
- a sub-meaning of *agent* for staff who deal with customers (*a ticket agent*)
- the plural *arrivals* for the part of an airport where travelers come out
- *a new arrival* meaning a newborn baby, now its own informal sub-meaning

**What I rejected, with reasons logged.** I turned down requests to hand-mark the headword in examples (the website does it). I kept the "informal" label on *be up in arms*: people say it in conversation, but it would sound out of place in a formal report. I kept the "British" label on *aspect* meaning the direction a building faces, because American English usually says *exposure*. I also kept the wedding meaning of *anniversary* as its own meaning, because a bare *our anniversary* always means the wedding day.

**Cross-references.** The cross-reference tool links related entries in both directions, but it could not decide which meaning of three existing entries (*worry*, *stress*, *feature*) should link back to the new ones, so I placed those three links by hand. Two link-type mismatches between new and existing entries were fixed on the new side. One is left for the planned audit of cross-references: the existing entry *birthday* calls *anniversary* a synonym, but it should be a "compare" link.

**Spend.** About US$0.39 for the panel, leaving about US$13.90 of today's US$15.

**What is next.** The scheduler chooses. The build queue continues with band-2 nouns after *aspect*.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose another build. I took eight words instead of the twelve allowed, to leave enough room in this run for checking and merging. All eight are band-2 nouns, next in queue order: **assault**, **assessment**, **asset**, **assignment**, **assistance**, **association**, **assumption**, and **athlete**. Each went through the same pipeline as in cycle 2: format check, generated word forms, the pronunciation check (all 16 transcriptions verified), the two-reviewer panel, my adjudication, the defining-vocabulary check, and cross-references.

**What the reviewers found.** Fourteen issues, none blocking. All fourteen were right, and I applied them:
- **Missing meanings.** *assessment* gains a third meaning, an official decision about how much something is worth or how much tax is owed (*a tax assessment*). *assignment* gains the act of giving people jobs, places, or things (*a seat assignment*). Both entries now open with a one-sentence core idea, which the style guide requires for three or more meanings.
- **Labels.** The "law" label came off the first meaning of *assault*, which also covers everyday attacks, not only the crime.
- **Grammar.** The "job" meaning of *assignment* is countable; *on assignment* is a fixed phrase, not a sign that the noun is uncountable. *assumption* in the sense of taking power (*his assumption of power*) is uncountable.
- **Smaller fixes.** Two definitions of *assessment* now also cover the process and a single test. *association* (connection) now covers things as well as people. A British-only collocation, *a housing association*, was removed. The signpost for *asset*'s first meaning (the short label that helps a reader pick a meaning) is now "advantage", because the old one, "useful quality", did not fit a person.

**A problem I caught myself.** My first wording for the new tax meaning of *assessment* used *calculation*. That word is outside the list of about 2,400 words that definitions may use, and the vocabulary check flagged it. The definition was rewritten with plain words.

**Cross-references.** I placed back-links by hand in *judgment*, *homework*, and *organization*, where the tool could not tell which meaning should carry the link. The tool placed one in *task* itself.

**Notes worth having.** *athlete* has a short usage note: in British English the word often means someone who runs, jumps, or throws in track and field events, while in American English it covers any sport. A pronunciation note warns against adding a sound in the middle (*ATH-uh-leet*). *assistance* has a false-friend note: in some languages a similar word means the people present at an event.

**Spend.** About US$0.22, leaving about US$13.70 of today's US$15.

**What is next.** The scheduler chooses. The build queue goes on with band-2 nouns after *athlete*.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a review. There were no unreviewed drafts and no reader reports, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That pass is a careful reading of the most important everyday words (band 1, the most frequent of the dictionary's five frequency groups), asking whether each entry is missing a meaning a learner will meet, and whether its notes follow the stricter note rules of 2026-10-24. I read only two entries this time, the verb **report** and the adjective **real**, both with six meanings. The block was small on purpose: this was the run's fourth cycle, and the earlier cycles had used much of the session's working memory. A short, careful reading is better than a long, thin one. The pass now stands at 165 entries.

**What changed in *report*.**
- The meaning "tell someone officially what you have found" now covers companies announcing their results (*the company reported higher profits*, *report a loss*), a very common use the entry lacked.
- A translator's note on grammar was removed: it repeated, nearly word for word, a learner-error box in the same entry, and the note rules allow one fact in one field.
- One example of the "learner errors" box now spells *canceled* the American way.
- A grammar-pattern tag was removed from a passive example that did not show the pattern.

**What changed in *real*.**
- One example was ungrammatical (*the house cost less than ten years ago*). A reviewer marked it blocking, meaning it must be fixed before publication. It now shows the meaning plainly: *In real terms, our pay has gone down, because prices have risen faster than wages.*
- The "true" meaning (*the real reason*) no longer claims to mean "most important".
- The intensifying meaning (*a real shame*, *a real success*) is now defined simply as *great or serious*, and *a real chance* and *real progress* moved there from the "proper" meaning, where they did not fit.
- A made-up learner error (*I real like this song*) was removed. Learners do not really make it, and the real problem, *real good* for *really good*, is already in the usage note.
- That usage note now says only that *real* before an adjective is an informal American adverb with its own entry. I added *real* as an adverb to the queue of words still to write, so that entry will be written later.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** 17 issues, 2 blocking (both about the same bad example). I applied 10 and rejected 7, each with a logged reason. The rejections:
- Two requests to add *get real* and *the real thing* here. By the dictionary's keyword rule an idiom lives under its first noun, or else its first verb, so they belong under *get* and *thing*.
- A request to merge two meanings of *report*. They differ in who reports and what they report.
- Two objections to an automatic grammar note on *real*. Word forms are written by a script, not by hand, and the first meaning of *real* does have comparative forms.

**Spend.** About US$0.10. The run's total was US$1.40 of today's US$15.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *reach* (verb) and the other unread six-meaning entries.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
