# Run report, 2026-10-30

## Cycle 1: closure

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose *closure*: building entries for words the dictionary already uses but has no entry for. The plain `closure` list is empty, so the twelve words came from the next list, `family` rows: verbs whose noun or adjective already has an entry. All twelve are in band 2 (the second most important of five frequency bands): **replace**, **round**, **screen**, **secure**, **ship**, **slow**, **sort**, **specify**, **speed**, **spot**, **spring**, and **star**. Each verb got its senses, signposts, grammar, examples, collocations, and cross-references, and every one passed the full pipeline and is now published as `reviewed`.

Before the work, the pre-flight checks found no open pull requests, no reports from readers, and nothing in the inbox. Four old `claude/` branches have nothing left in them that is not already on `main`, and each already has a line asking the owner to delete it.

**Pronunciation and forms.** All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each verb) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel. Eleven of the twelve American ones also matched the open CMU pronouncing dictionary; it disagreed only on **replace**, where three of four votes agreed with ours. The inflected forms came from the rules, and for **speed** (*sped*) and **spring** (*sprang*, *sprung*) from lines already in the exceptions table.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model, and reviewer B, a Google model) raised 61 issues, 5 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). I applied 34 and rejected 27, each with a logged reason.

- **Blocking, applied.** Two definitions used the headword to define itself: **slow** was "become slower" and the shape sense of **round** was "make something round". Both now say the thing in other words. In **sort**, I had given the British use (*get the heating sorted*) a grammar pattern that really belongs to *get*. The pattern is removed and the example carries a short note instead.
- **Blocking, rejected.** Reviewer A wanted the idiom *spring a leak* added to **spring**. Under the house keyword rule, an idiom lives under its first noun, here **leak**, which is in the queue (band 3). The same applies to *spring to mind*, which belongs beside *come to mind* in **mind**. Both are listed in `NEXT.md` for a later review.
- **Real gaps the reviewers caught, added:** the loan sense of **secure** (*the loan is secured against their home*); *spring for* (informal American, "pay for", as in *spring for dinner*) and *spring someone from jail*; and the transitive use of **ship** for software (*the team shipped the update*).
- **Smaller fixes:** clearer core ideas (the one-sentence summary at the top of an entry with three or more senses) for **screen**, **spring**, **star**, and **sort**; a dated example (*screened her eyes*) replaced; an example making a science claim about plant growth replaced, because examples must not state checkable facts; a vague translation note on **screen** removed.
- **Rejected:** reviewer B's requests to hand-mark *sped* and *sprang* in examples (the site marks every form listed in the inflections table on its own), several requests to drop the explanation that says a sense is used only in one pattern (*sort through something*; the style guide asks for exactly that), and a request to mark **spot** as never used in continuous tenses (*I keep spotting mistakes* is ordinary English).

Two back-links (the reverse half of a cross-reference) could not be placed by the script because it could not tell which sense should get them. I placed them by hand: **secure** is now listed as a synonym under the "receive or obtain" sense of **get** and the "attach" sense of **fasten**.

**Spend.** US$0.39 of the day's US$15 budget, all on the review panel and the pronunciation panel. Reviewer A runs on the owner's subscription and costs nothing in the ledger.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. One small slip: I logged two of reviewer B's identical *sped* objections with the same index number, so the decision log has two identical lines for them. Both are rejections with the same reason, so nothing is lost.

**What is next.** More `family` and `crossref` verbs in queue order, the coverage pass in review cycles, and a lint pass (the periodic whole-dictionary check) in four runs.

**For the owner.** Nothing new. The open items in `NEXT.md` stand: the list-name question (*Ms. Kim*), the charter line about "two non-Anthropic reviewers", and the four branches waiting to be deleted.

## Cycle 2: originality

**What the cycle did.** The selector forced an *originality check*, which runs once every twenty runs. This was run 600. The check tests whether our wording matches any published dictionary. A script picked ten fields at random from across the dictionary: definitions of **gamble**, **demonstrate**, **ought**, **doubt**, **hint**, and **do** (the auxiliary verb), two phrase definitions (*up to date* under **date** and *be all ears* under **ear**), and the usage notes of **video** and **bicycle**. I searched the web for each text as an exact phrase and then asked reviewer B, the Google model, whether each one reads as copied.

**What was found.** No search turned up our wording in a published dictionary. Five fields matched nothing at all. The other five are what the house rules call *generic overlap*: short, plain wording that any dictionary would likely write in a similar way. Oxford's learner's dictionary defines *bet* with "risk money on". Its *prove* definition says "use facts, evidence ... to show that something is true", close in idea to our **demonstrate**. Its *clue* uses "a piece of information that helps you find the answers", which has the same shape as our **hint**. Britannica's *modern* uses "the newest information", close to our *up to date*. One grammar teaching page, not a dictionary, uses the same plain clause we use for **doubt** ("probably not true or will probably not happen"). In none of these cases is the wording distinctive enough to call it copying. Reviewer B answered "original" on all ten. Earlier records show that this reviewer names likely sources but cannot quote them, so the web searches decide.

**Verdicts:** 5 original, 5 generic overlap, 0 to rewrite. The record is in `reviews/originality/2026-10-30.md`.

**Spend.** Less than one US cent for the reviewer call. The web searches are not billed to the project.

**What did not work.** Nothing. Several searches returned spam pages that copy dictionary text, and I ignored them.

**What is next.** The next originality check is due in twenty runs. The selector will pick the next cycle's work.

**For the owner.** Nothing new.
