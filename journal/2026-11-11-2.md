**What was added.**

- *pick*: two subsenses under the "remove" sense, one for picking your nose or teeth and one for *pick at* (*don't pick at that cut*); a new sense for playing a stringed instrument with the fingers (*pick the banjo*); the "eat slowly" sense now says people do it when worried or unwell, not only when not hungry.
- *picture*: a subsense for the general state of things (*the overall picture*, *the jobs picture*); the idea sense now names a picture in your mind; *a picture frame*, *a picture book*; the saying *a picture is worth a thousand words*; and a learner error that is very common: *the man on the picture* for *in the picture*.
- *play*: a new sense for the small free movement of a part (*too much play in the steering wheel*), a new sense for the button that starts a video (*press play*), and the phrases *foul play* (a crime, or unfair actions in sport) and *bring something into play*.
- *pot*: a British sense for a small storing container (*a pot of paint*, *a yogurt pot*), the sayings *a watched pot never boils* and *pots of money* (British, informal), and a pointer to *melting pot*, which has been queued as a future entry.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** Two reviewer models read each changed entry field by field. They raised 47 issues, 5 of them "blocking" (must be settled before the entry stays published). 40 were accepted and fixed, 7 rejected.

- Two blocking issues on *pick* asked for *pick someone's pocket* and *pick a fight*. Both were rejected: the dictionary files an idiom under its first noun, and both are already in the entries for *pocket* and *fight*.
- Three blocking issues on *play* said the new *foul play* phrase was defined only as a crime while one example showed its sports meaning. They were right: the definition now covers both meanings.
- Among the accepted minor points: the *play* entry's translator note was generic and was replaced by a specific trap (the stage sense is countable, the fun sense is not); the *picture* translator note repeated a fact from the box comparing *picture*, *photo*, and *image*, and now says only what is new; the British phrase *put someone in the picture* got its British label; the new *pot* sense was labeled British, which both reviewers asked for.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The cap for a cycle is twelve; this one wrote eight, so that each could be full: *lighting*, *lion*, *listener*, *litter*, *living*, *lizard*, *local*, and *location*, all nouns. The dictionary now has 4,176 entries, all marked reviewed.

**The entries.** A few points worth knowing:

- *living* has three senses: the money you earn (*what do you do for a living?*, with the common learner error *for living*), a way of life (*healthy living*, *city living*), and *the living*, people who are alive. *The cost of living* and *the standard of living* are not in this entry: the dictionary files a phrase under its first noun, and they are already in the entries for *cost* and *standard*.
- *local* covers a person who lives in an area (*ask one of the locals*), the British informal *my local* (the bar near your home), and the American *local* train that stops at every station.
- *location* has a note for translators: in some languages a word that looks like it means renting, not a place. It also has a business-branch use (*the coffee shop has three locations*), a formal use for finding where something is, and the filming sense (*on location*).
- *litter* covers waste on the ground (uncountable, with the learner error *a litter*), the babies an animal has at one time, and cat litter.
- *lion* has the animal, a sense for an admired, famous person (*a literary lion*), and three phrases: *the lion's share*, *the lion's den*, and *throw someone to the lions*.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** Both reviewer models read every field of every entry. They raised 47 issues, 16 of them blocking (must be settled before an entry is published). 43 were accepted and fixed; 4 were rejected.

- Most blocking issues were about examples that did not show the sense they sat under. One example under *the living* actually used the adjective (*the living members of the band*); it was replaced. Under *lion*, the person sense had the simile *fought like a lion*, which is about the animal; it was replaced. *The lighting is too dark* is not natural English (a room is dark, lighting is dim) and was fixed. The translator note on *location* had lost its verb and was rewritten.
- Rejected: a request to add *cost of living* and *standard of living* to *living* (they belong under *cost* and *standard*, which have them); two complaints that *listener* is defined with *listens* (defining an agent noun through its verb is normal practice here); and a quibble about the order of nouns in one collocation type.
- Two words in definitions, *podcast* and *studio*, are outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions must use) and have no entries, so those definitions were reworded.
- Two cross-references that the linking tool could not place were placed by hand: *garbage* now compares itself with *litter*, and *place* lists *location* as a synonym.

## Cycle 3: closure

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *closure*: writing entries for words the dictionary already uses but does not yet define, so that a reader who meets a word in a definition can look it up. Words come first from definitions (the "closure" source), then from word families (*locker* from *lock*), then from cross-references. Six entries were written: *senior*, *locker*, *lover*, *lunchtime*, *majority*, and *recording*, all nouns. The dictionary now has 4,182 entries, all reviewed.

**A queue fix.** The closure source had queued *recordings* (from a definition of *label*), a plural form rather than a dictionary word. It was marked as a duplicate and the singular *recording* was written instead.

**The entries.**

- *senior* has two American senses, an older person (*discounts for seniors*) and a student in the last year of high school or college, and a general one, someone older or higher in rank (*she is three years my senior*).
- *lover* has a note for translators: in some languages the usual translation means a steady partner or even a spouse, while English *lover* stresses a sexual relationship and can suggest a secret one. Its second sense is an enthusiast (*a music lover*).
- *majority* has most of a group (with the common error *the majority of the students was late*), a voting sense with a subsense for the winning margin (*won by a majority of 300 votes*), and the formal legal sense (*the age of majority*), plus *be in the majority* and *the silent majority*.
- *locker*, *lunchtime*, and *recording* are single-sense or two-sense entries.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** 20 issues, 5 blocking; 19 accepted, 1 rejected.

- The main blocking point was on *majority*: one example used the word for the winning margin, which the voting definition did not cover. Both reviewers saw it. The margin now has its own subsense.
- Another example stated a checkable fact (the legal adult age in many countries), which house style forbids; it was replaced.
- The *recording* definition was plural (*sounds or pictures*) for a countable sense; it is now *a sound or a set of pictures*.
- Rejected: a suggestion to split *senior*'s "older or higher in rank" sense in two; both uses share the same grammar (*my senior*), so one sense serves the learner.
- Two definition words outside the plain defining vocabulary, *sexual* and *further*, were reworded. *Lover* now points to the "enthusiast" sense of *fan*, and *fan* points back.

**Spend.** US$0.17 for this cycle; US$1.38 of the day's US$15 so far.

**What did not work.** The closure source queued a plural form (*recordings*) as if it were a headword. This is a known weakness of the tool that reads definition words; it was handled by hand and needs no new machinery yet.
