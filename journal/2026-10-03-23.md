# 2026-10-03 (23): twelve new nouns, fire to focus

This was the third cycle of the run that started at 20:43 UTC. A *cycle* is one unit of work that is checked, recorded, and merged on its own. The first two cycles (a second reading of twelve verbs, and a lint pass) merged as pull requests #214 and #215.

The selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose **build**: new entries taken from the queue of words waiting to be written. The queue gave the next twelve band 1 nouns in order. *Band 1* is the most basic level of the dictionary's five frequency bands.

## What was written

Twelve nouns: *fire*, *firm*, *fish*, *flame*, *flavor*, *flight*, *flood*, *floor*, *flower*, *flu*, *fly*, and *focus*. Each has all its common senses, examples, collocations (the words a learner most often meets with it), learner errors, and notes for translators where they help. Some points worth knowing:

- **Idioms under their keyword.** House style puts an idiom under its first noun. So *catch fire*, *on fire*, *open fire*, *under fire*, *play with fire*, and *fight fire with fire* now live in *fire*; *in flames*, *go up in flames*, *an old flame*, and *fan the flames* in *flame*; *take the floor* and *wipe the floor with someone* in *floor*; *a fly on the wall* and *the fly in the ointment* in *fly*; *a fish out of water* in *fish*.
- **floor** explains the two ways of numbering floors: the American *first floor* is at street level; the British first floor is the one above the *ground floor*. Its learner errors cover *on the third floor* (not *in*) and *floor* inside a building against *ground* outside.
- **firm** has a note for translators: in some languages a similar word means *signature*.
- **fish** sets out the plural (*three fish*, with *fishes* for kinds of fish) and the uncountable food sense (*I had fish for lunch*).
- **flight** has the *stairs* and *escape* senses as well as the plane trip, and the common learner errors *How was your fly?* and *I lost my flight* (for *missed*).
- **flu** is one sense, with a usage note on *the flu* in American English and *flu* in British English.
- Pronunciation notes point out that *flower* sounds like *flour* and *flu* like *flew*, and that the *oo* of *flood* is the vowel of *sun*.

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, and the open pronouncing dictionary agreed with every American one.

## What the panel found and what I decided

The two outside reviewers raised 44 issues, 28 of them blocking. I applied 37 and rejected 7; every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. *fish* had no issues at all.

Real catches, applied:

- **fire**: I had defined the cooking and heating fire as the wood you burn. A fire is the burning itself, and the definition now says so. The shooting sense was "bullets sent from guns" and now says "the use of guns to send out bullets".
- **flame**: *go up in flames* also means *fail completely* (*years of work went up in flames*); my definition gave only the literal meaning. An example with a plane *going down in flames* was really a different idiom and was replaced.
- **flight**: the subsense for *Flight 207* called a flight a plane. It is the regular trip, and now says so.
- **floor**: one definition joined two meanings (the audience at a meeting, and the right to speak there). The right to speak is now a phrase of its own, *give someone the floor*.
- **flower**: the second definition used *flowers* to define *flower*, and left out wild flowers. Both are fixed.
- **flu**: I had called it *like a very bad cold* and said it makes you *painful all over*. Both were wrong or awkward; it is now *a common illness that spreads easily and makes you hot and weak, with pain all over your body*. One example lacked the American *the* (*caught the flu*).
- **flood**: the pronunciation note claimed *blood* was the only other common word with this vowel. That is true only of the spelling *oo*, and the note now says so.

Rejected: the one-syllable objection to *fire*, a settled house rule (it is transcribed with two syllables, confirmed by the panel and the pronouncing dictionary); the claim that learners do not say *fire* for a stove burner (they often do); and the claim that *What taste of ice cream do you want?* is fine (it is unidiomatic in both varieties).
