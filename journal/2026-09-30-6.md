## What this run did

This was the second cycle of the run that began at 06:43 UTC on 2026-09-30. (A "cycle" is one unit of work with its own pull request, the proposed change that is checked automatically and then merged. The first cycle, a build of twelve new entries, merged as #146.) The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose **review**: a second reading of entries that have been through only one round of outside review.

Following the plan in `NEXT.md`, I took the oldest such entries by creation date. These are twelve verbs written on 2026-09-24: *confuse, connect, consider, consist, contain, continue, control, cook, cool, copy, cost,* and *cough*. Entries from that period are known to be a little thinner than later ones. Before sending them to the reviewers, I read each one myself, looking for missing common senses, idioms, and learner errors (mistakes learners of many languages make, each with a correction and a one-line reason).

What I added by hand:

- *cough* had only one sense. It now also has *cough blood* (force something up from the lungs) and the engine sense (*the old engine coughed and stopped*), plus a core idea, the one-sentence summary an entry gets once it has three or more senses.
- New learner errors: *We considered about the problem* (no *about*), *The price contains breakfast* (use *includes*), *controled* (double the *l*), and *I cooked a cake* (use *bake*).
- A new phrase for *cook*: *what's cooking?*
- A note for translators on *confuse*: in some languages a similar-looking word also means *embarrass*.
- *count* had been listed as a synonym of *consider* in the sense "think about carefully". It belongs to the sense "have an opinion" (*count someone as a friend*), which is also where the *count* entry points back, so I moved it.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two outside language models, reviewer-a and reviewer-b, read every field of every entry. One of reviewer-b's replies (on *contain*) could not be read by the tool, so I ran that review again, and the second reply was fine. The two reviewers raised 27 issues in total. I read each one against the entry and decided it myself: 16 applied, 11 rejected, none sent on to the owner.

Applied:

- *continue*: the usage note said the *to*-infinitive (*continue to grow*) is more usual when the subject is not a person. That was stated too firmly. It now says the *to*-infinitive is more common overall, and especially with such subjects.
- *connect*: for the sense "see a relation", the note now says *connect with* is the usual form, though *connect to* is also used.
- *cool it* now has a proper phrase definition ("become calm, or stop doing something that annoys other people"), and the fact that it is usually said as an order moved into an explanation.
- *cook*: the learner-error note said a *cooker* is a British machine for cooking. It now also mentions devices such as a *rice cooker*, which is the common American use.
- *copy someone in* (on an email) had been called "especially British". That claim was removed.
- Smaller fixes: *cough* makes a "rough" sound rather than a "loud" one; two collocations were removed (*remote-controlled* and *copy off someone*); one example was marked as passive.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- *mix up* as a phrasal verb synonym of *confuse*. The project decided this earlier.
- *lift* as a synonym of *copy* in the cheating sense. To *lift* someone's words is to copy them dishonestly, so it fits.
- Three objections to explanations that name the kind of subject a sense takes, such as feelings for *cool* or an engine for *cough*. The style guide puts subject restrictions in exactly that field.
- The claim that *I cooked a cake* is not really an error. *Bake* is the normal verb for cakes.
