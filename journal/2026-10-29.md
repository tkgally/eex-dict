# Run report, 2026-10-29

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from what is most overdue) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words the dictionary still needs. Before that, the start-of-run checks found nothing waiting: no open pull requests left by an earlier run, no new files from you in `inbox/`, and no reader reports ("Entry report" issues) on GitHub. Three old branches hold no unmerged work and are already listed for deletion in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`.

Twelve entries were written, all in band 1 (the most frequent of the five frequency levels):

- **as long as** (conjunction): *only if*, *during the whole time that*, and an informal American use meaning *since*.
- **America** (noun): the US, and *the Americas*, the two continents together.
- **dollar** (noun): the unit, a coin or note, and the currency's value; the phrases *top dollar* and *bet your bottom dollar*.
- **Europe** (noun): the continent; the British use for the continent without Britain; and the European Union in news and politics.
- **October** (noun), shaped like the other month entries.
- **American** (adjective): of the US, typical of the US (*very American*), and of the two continents as a whole.
- **crazy** (adjective): six senses from *foolish* to *mentally ill* (labeled offensive), *like crazy*, and a note on **crazy** versus **mad**.
- **north** and **west** (adverbs): direction (*drive north*) and position (*north of the town*), with the phrase *up north*.
- **pretty** (adverb): *fairly* or *quite* (*pretty good*), with the phrase *pretty much*.
- **since** (adverb): *hasn't called since* and *has since become*.
- **all right** (interjection): agreeing, checking, starting, impatience, pleasure, and the British greeting *All right?*

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Each entry went through the pronunciation panel (all 24 American and British transcriptions agreed and were verified) and then to the two automated reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, a Google model). They raised 109 points, 12 of them blocking. I logged 99 decisions: 81 applied the reviewer's point, 18 rejected it with a written reason, none needed you.

The most useful catches:

- In **as long as**, sentences like *stay as long as you like* and *as long as possible* are really comparisons with the adverb **long**, not the conjunction. They were taken out, and the entry now points to **long** for them.
- In **America**, examples about *South America* alone did not show the "two continents" sense. That sense is now presented as *the Americas*, with new examples.
- **north** and **west** were first defined as *toward the north* and *toward the west*, which uses the word to define itself. They are now defined by the sun (*toward the direction where the sun goes down*).
- **pretty** had a separate *very* sense that differed only in tone of voice. It was merged into one sense, and the tone point now lives in the usage note.
- An example in **American** stated a geographic fact, which the example rules forbid. It was replaced.

The rejections: reviewer B wanted *the US* and *Oct.* removed as abbreviations, but neither is on the house list of banned abbreviations, and existing entries use both. Both reviewers called "offensive" an attitude label, but in this dictionary it is a register value. Reviewer B wanted *bet your bottom dollar* moved under **bet**, but the phrase rule takes the first noun (*dollar*) before the first verb.

Fourteen cross-references that the script could not match to a sense were placed by hand: for example, **crazy** is now listed as a synonym in **foolish** and **strange**, and **all right** in **okay**, **yes**, and **hello**. The cycle also queued six words the new entries point to: *cent*, *September*, *European* (adjective and noun), *crazily*, and *craziness*.

**Spend.** US$0.40 of the US$15 daily cap, all for pronunciation checks and reviewer B.

**What did not work.** I started three review processes at once to save time. Then I saw that the spending ledger has no lock against two processes writing at the same moment, so I stopped two of them before they recorded anything and ran the batches one after another. Nothing was lost, and `NEXT.md` now says to run one process at a time. A known tool gap showed again: the inflection script cannot record a plural (*Americas*) for a noun it treats as having none, so that form is marked by hand in the examples.

**What is next.** More cycles may follow in this run. The queue still has the other compass words (*south* and *east* as adverbs, *north* and *west* as adjectives), which should be defined the same way as **north** and **west**.

**What needs you.** Nothing new. The open questions in `NEXT.md` are unchanged, including whether continent names may appear in definitions. **Europe** follows the working assumption: no names in the definition.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector chose *review*: a second reading of entries that already exist. There were no unreviewed drafts and no reader reports, so the cycle continued the coverage pass you asked for on 2026-10-24. That pass rereads the most important everyday words (band 1), starting with those that have the most senses, and asks of each: does the entry cover every meaning and fixed phrase a learner will actually meet? Four entries were read: **head** (noun), **blow** (verb), **say** (verb), and **thing** (noun). Eighty-one entries have now had this reading.

**What was added.**

- **head**: six idioms (*bury your head in the sand*, *go over someone's head*, *rear its ugly head*, *a head of steam*, the British informal *off your head*, and *scratch your head*), plus *a full head of hair*, *no head for heights*, and *head of state* as collocations.
- **blow**: a sub-sense for *blow a tire*; the money use (*blew all her savings*) separated from the "waste a chance" use; *blow hot and cold*; collocations such as *blow something to pieces*.
- **say**: *say to do something* (*Mom said to call her*); the phrases *not to say*, *I wouldn't say no*, *says who?*, *that is to say*, *enough said*, and *what someone says goes*.
- **thing**: a new sense, *an activity someone likes* (*Dancing isn't really my thing*), and the phrases *first things first*, *too much of a good thing*, *be seeing things*, *among other things*, and the informal *and things*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two automated reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, a Google model) raised 101 points, 10 blocking. I logged 101 decisions: 88 applied, 13 rejected with a reason. Many of the applied points were about older text in these entries, not this cycle's additions:

- **say** had a sub-sense for *meet me at, say, six*, where **say** works as an adverb, not a verb. It was removed, the usage note now points to a **say** adverb entry, and that entry has been added to the queue.
- **head** claimed that *heads*, the side of a coin, takes a singular verb (*Heads is my choice*). Nobody talks like that, so the claim was removed from both places it appeared. The idiom *go to someone's head* now defines its alcohol meaning instead of leaving it in a side note.
- **blow** had an invented learner error (*The wind blowed*); the style guide forbids regularized forms made up to fill the field, so it was removed. An unnatural example (*blow the trumpet*) became *blew a few notes on his trumpet*. A sub-sense was coded transitive when its examples (*blow on your tea*) take no object.
- Notes that repeated another field were cut in all four entries, under the house rule of one fact, one field.

The rejections mostly applied the phrase rule (an idiom goes under its first noun, else its first verb). *Blow someone a kiss* belongs in **kiss** (noun), which is in the queue but not yet built. *Blow someone's mind* belongs in **mind**, which lacks it. *It goes without saying* and *when all is said and done* are already in **go**. One choice goes the other way: *blow the whistle on someone* should go under **whistle**, but that entry does not exist yet, so it stays in **blow** for now. `NEXT.md` lists these moves for later.

**Spend.** US$0.43 this cycle; US$0.83 so far today, of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The reviewers flagged a lot of older material in these large entries, so this cycle covered four entries rather than more.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with the next band-1 entries by number of senses and sub-senses.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. The queue handed out twelve band-1 rows. One of them, the phrase *you are welcome*, turned out to exist already as the phrase *you're welcome* inside the **welcome** (adjective) entry, so it was marked as a duplicate and not written. The other eleven were written:

- **help** (interjection): the shout for help in danger, and the informal *Help! I have three exams tomorrow*.
- Seven phrasal verbs, each with all its common senses:
  - **come up**: a subject mentioned, a problem that suddenly needs time, *her birthday is coming up*, a job that becomes available, walking up to someone, moving to a higher floor, the sun coming up, plants coming up, something appearing on a screen, and visiting.
  - **go ahead**: *Sure, go ahead*, a plan going ahead, going first.
  - **go down**: prices, moving lower, the sun, falling (and a ship sinking), a computer system stopping, how something is received (*went down badly*), and losing a game.
  - **go off**: leaving, an alarm ringing, a bomb exploding, lights stopping, food going bad (British), an event going well, *I've gone off coffee*, and the American *go off on someone*.
  - **go up**: prices, climbing, buildings, fire, going to a place, going up to someone, a cheer going up, and moving up a league.
  - **hurry up**: *Hurry up!* and *hurry the kids up*.
  - **turn out**: how things turn out, *it turned out that...*, switching off a light, a crowd turning out, a factory turning out products, emptying a pocket, and making someone leave their home.
- **Mr.** and **Mrs.** (abbreviations), with how they are said, *Mr. President*, *Mr. Right*, and the common error of using them without a name.
- **double** (determiner): *double the price*, *double my salary*, *double what we paid*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 99 points, 12 blocking. I logged 99 decisions: 93 applied and 6 rejected. The blocking points were sound: examples that showed a different meaning from their sense (*go down to the end of the street* means *along*, not *lower*; *a cold drink would go down nicely* is about swallowing), a definition of the sun "coming up in the morning" that left out the moon, and a contradiction in **Mr.**, which said the title is never used without a name while also giving *Mr. President*. Reviewer A also found real gaps, and five senses were added during review (listed above with each verb). Rejected points: reviewer B wanted *went ahead* marked by hand in examples, but the site already marks every form listed in the inflections; and both reviewers again called "humorous" an attitude label, which it is not in this dictionary.

**Pronunciation.** Twenty-one of twenty-two transcriptions were verified. The British form of *hurry up* got only one vote in three, twice. The entry carries a flag saying so, and a line in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` records the working assumption (keep it, matching **hurry** itself).

**Spend.** US$0.40 this cycle; US$1.23 today.

**What did not work.** My first check of the pronunciation results filtered out lines containing "verified", which also hid the one line saying "unverified". The final gate caught it before the commit.

**What is next.** Possibly one more cycle; then the queue continues with the remaining band-1 rows.

**What needs you.** Only the new pronunciation line above, when you next look at the curator file.
