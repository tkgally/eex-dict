**Why this cycle was a lint.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) forces a lint, the housekeeping pass over the whole dictionary and its knowledge base, once ten runs have gone by since the last one. Ten had, so this cycle was the ninety-eighth lint pass. No entries were written or reviewed.

**Before starting.** There were no open pull requests left by an earlier run, no stray working branches on GitHub, nothing in the owner's inbox folder, and no open "Entry report" issues from readers. Nothing needed rescuing.

**Mechanical checks.** The size caps on the project's rule and wiki files, the check that every link inside the wiki resolves, the entry validator, and the unit tests all passed. The wiki as a whole is within 77 words of its 30,000-word cap, so the one note this pass extended (the reviewer-precision record) was condensed at the same time to stay under it.

**Cross-references.** The cross-reference tool (`tools/crossref.py`) adds a matching link on the other side whenever one entry names another as a synonym, an opposite, or a word to compare. This pass it added one such back-link automatically: `background-n` now lists `ground-n` in its word family, mirroring the link from `ground-n`. When the tool cannot tell which sense of the target entry the link belongs to, it writes nothing and lists the pair for a person (a "NO-SENSE" pair). There were nine. Five are the long-standing ones that wait for the cross-reference audit the owner asked for in October. The other four came from verbs built this morning, and I placed them by hand:

- *corner* (trap a person or animal) now appears as a synonym in sense 1 of *trap*, which is the same meaning.
- *revise* (change a plan or number because of new information) now appears as a synonym in sense 1 of *update*.
- *crop* listed *trim* as a synonym, and *enter* listed *type* as a synonym. Neither pair truly means the same thing: to crop hair is to cut it very short, to trim it is to cut off a little; to enter information can be done by speaking or scanning, not only typing. So in both pairs the link was changed to "compare" on both sides, rather than writing a doubtful synonym back-link.

**Band vote.** Each word waiting to be written has a frequency band from 1 (most important) to 5. Words added to the queue by tools since the last lint have no band until the next vote, in which three word-list models each give a band and the lower middle vote is taken. Forty-eight such rows were voted on this pass, for under one US cent. No word had votes more than one band apart, so none needed my judgment. Two came out in band 1, nine in band 2, twenty-eight in band 3, and nine in band 4. The record is `experiments/band-vote-2026-11-02/README.md`.

**Reviewer precision.** Every reviewer objection that I accept or reject is logged, and a reviewer's "precision" in a kind of objection is the share accepted. A kind under 30 percent over twenty or more decisions gets switched off for that reviewer. Nothing new fell under the line. The nearest is reviewer B's pronunciation objections, down from 38 to 35 percent over 23 decisions; it will be watched at the next lint. Reviewer B's spelling-and-format objections remain under the line at 27 percent but stay on by the owner's earlier ruling.

**Reports.** The lint reports listed 330 definitions that use their own headword (most harmless, judged at each entry's review), 911 sense pairs where a link may name the wrong sense (for the cross-reference audit), and 8 words that an entry promises has its own entry but that has neither an entry nor a queue row. ## Cycle 2: build

**Why a build.** After the lint merged, the mode selector chose a build, because building new entries had gone longest without its share of runs. It allowed up to twelve new entries and US$3 of paid calls. I claimed six from the top of the queue, so that each could be full; the limit is a ceiling, not a target.

**What was built.** Six new entries, all now published-ready (`reviewed`):

- **review** (noun): a careful look at something to decide whether to change it, with a new subsense for the academic *literature review*; a written opinion of a book, film, or product; a job performance review; and the American school sense of studying again before a test (British *revision*). The phrase *under review* is included.
- **sweater**: one sense, with a usage note on a real trap: the British word is *jumper*, but in American English a *jumper* is a sleeveless dress. A pronunciation note for translators says the *ea* sounds like *bed*, not *eat*.
- **flip** (verb): seven senses, from turning a pancake over, flipping a coin, and flipping a switch, to flipping through a magazine, informal *flip* (suddenly get very angry), and two informal American uses: buying and quickly reselling a house, and a district or a voter changing sides in politics.
- **offend**: making someone upset; seeming unpleasant or wrong to someone (with the formal *offend against a rule*); and the formal legal sense, breaking the law. It carries one learner error that learners really make: *I felt very offensive* for *I felt very offended*.
- **trace** (verb): finding something lost; finding where something came from; describing how something developed; copying a picture through thin paper; and following a line with your finger.
- **urge** (verb): pressing someone to do something, calling publicly for something (*urge caution*), and making a person or animal move on.

**Checks.** The pronunciation panel (three outside models plus an open pronouncing dictionary used only at run time) agreed with all twelve transcriptions, and the inflection tool generated every form (*flipped*, *flipping*) from its rules.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (one Claude model, one Google model) raised 36 issues. One was blocking: an *offend* example, *Most young people who offend once never do it again*, reads like a crime statistic, which the house rules forbid in examples. I replaced it with a judge's warning. Of the 35 minor issues I applied 33 and rejected 2. The applied ones improved real things: the finger-tracing use of *trace* was wrongly placed under copying and is now its own sense; a *traced the call* example was moved to the "find where it came from" sense; a *that*-clause pattern was removed from the first sense of *urge*, where a person is the object; the *offend* senses were rearranged so that one meaning is not defined twice; the British word *timetable* became *schedule*; and two reviewers both caught that *sweater* stated the *jumper* fact twice. I also added the political sense of *flip* that one reviewer noticed was missing. I rejected a request to put the opinion sense of *review* first (the "look again" sense is the core that the others build on) and a claim that the study sense of *review* is only uncountable (*a quick review before the test* is normal).

**Cross-references.** Eleven links to other entries needed a person to choose which sense they belong to. I placed ten by hand (for example, *offend* now appears as a synonym in the first sense of *upset*, and *urge* is a word to compare in *persuade*, *encourage*, and *recommend*). The eleventh, *switch* as a synonym of flipping a switch, was dropped: the *switch* entry has no on-or-off sense, because that use belongs to the phrasal verbs *switch on* and *switch off*. *Flip* and *turn* were changed to "compare" on both sides, since they are not true synonyms. Two new phrasal verbs, *flip off* and *flip out*, were added to the queue.

**Spend.** US$0.22 this cycle; US$1.78 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**Next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order, the cross-reference audit, and the band-1 coverage review.

## Cycle 3: review

**Why a review.** The selector chose a review cycle. There were no draft entries and no reader reports, so the work was the next step of the coverage pass the owner asked for in October (ruling B1): reading the most important (band 1) entries again to add whatever a complete learner's dictionary would have but ours lacks. The pass is working through entries with six senses, newest-unread first. I took four: *thin*, *taste*, *take off*, and *system*. That brings the pass to 151 entries, with 56 six-sense entries still to read.

**What I added.**

- **thin**: a subsense for *thin air* at the top of a mountain, and a new sense for a thin voice or sound (high and weak).
- **take off**: a subsense for removing a lid or a cover; a new sense for taking a player out of a game, a show off the air, or someone off a medicine; and the British informal sense of copying someone to make people laugh. Its senses with an object now carry the code `separable` (the object can go between *take* and *off*), as the owner's ruling B4 asks for every phrasal verb when it is next reviewed.
- **system**: a subsense for *the solar system* and star systems, and one for sets of units and signs (*the metric system*, *a writing system*).
- **taste** was already complete; it got smaller fixes only.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 48 issues, 9 of them blocking, and I applied all 9:

- *thin*'s summary line said thinness is the distance "from one side to the other", which is what *narrow* means. It now says "distance through" something. A translator's note that restated the learner error about *thin*/*narrow* was removed.
- *taste* had a garbled definition (*a small amount of food that you have to find out what it is like*), now *that you try in order to find out*.
- My new *take off* sense was badly defined. Its definition, about a list or a plan, did not fit its own examples (a coach taking a player off, a doctor taking someone off pills). I redefined it to cover them.
- *system* had an example stating a science fact (*the heart is part of the circulatory system*), which the house rules forbid. It also listed *model* as a synonym, which it is not. Both are fixed: *model* and *network* are now "compare" on both sides.

Of the minor issues I applied 33 and rejected 6. The applied ones included the British *transport system* (now American *transportation*), an example that belonged to the computer sense, an invented learner error and two generic translators' notes (removed), and repeated facts. I also replaced two examples of my own that stated checkable facts before the panel saw them. I rejected three requests to drop opposites and synonyms from *thin*: the linked senses really match (*a heavy coat*/*a thin coat*, *a fat book*/*a thin book*, *fine* meaning "very thin"). I also rejected two calls to move *humorous* to a different label set (it belongs to the register set) and a request to hand-mark a phrase example.

## Cycle 4: build

**Why a build.** The selector chose a build again, allowing up to twelve entries. Because this was the run's fourth and last cycle, I claimed five, so that they would be as full as the first cycle's.

**What was built.** Five band-2 verbs, all now `reviewed`:

- **value**: thinking that someone or something is important (*I really value your advice*), and judging how much money something is worth (*The house was valued at twice what they paid for it*).
- **vary**: being different from each other (*Prices vary from store to store*), changing over time (*My work hours vary from week to week*), and making something different (*vary your diet*). A note for translators says that in much American speech *vary* and *very* sound the same.
- **view**: thinking of someone in a certain way (*She views him as a friend*), looking at something carefully, looking at a page or file on a screen, watching a movie or a show, and visiting a house you might buy or rent.
- **volunteer**: offering to do something, working without pay (*He volunteers at an animal shelter*), saying something without being asked, and the informal *volunteer someone* (offer another person for a job without asking them).
- **wander**: walking without a clear purpose, straying from where you should be, a mind or attention wandering (with *wander from the point* when speaking), and a path or river winding. A pronunciation note separates *wander* from *wonder*.

**Checks.** The pronunciation panel and the open pronouncing dictionary agreed with all ten transcriptions; the inflection tool produced every form.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 32 issues, one blocking, and they were right: I had written the sound of *wander* as *WON-der*, but *won* has the vowel of *sun*, the very vowel the note gives to *wonder*. It now reads *WAHN-der*. I applied 29 of the 31 minor issues. Among them: *wander* had a purpose clause in an example (*to look for a snack*) under a sense defined as "without a clear purpose"; its third sense mentioned wandering eyes, which its definition did not cover; *wander off* was missing; an example of *value* gave a price in dollars, which the house rules avoid; *film* became *movie*; and three labels that did not fit were removed (*formal* on *volunteer information*, *literary* on a winding path, *British* on viewing a house). I rejected two: *range* is a true synonym of *vary* in *vary from ten to fifty dollars*, and the screen and movie senses of *view* stay separate, because they take different collocations.

**Cross-references.** Six links needed a person to pick the sense; I placed all six (for example, *view* is now a synonym in the right senses of *regard* and *consider*, and *value* is a word to compare in *respect*). *Range* and *wander* became "compare" on both sides. *Vary* and *differ* had named each other both as synonyms and under "see also", so the second link was removed on both sides.
