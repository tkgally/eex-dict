# 2026-10-22, cycle 2 of the 00:44 UTC run: eleven phrasal verbs, *turn down* to *work out*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose it and allowed up to twelve. The queue is still in the most common phrasal verbs (a verb plus a small word such as *up* or *off*, with a meaning of its own). I claimed twelve and wrote eleven:

- *turn down* (make a sound or heat lower; refuse an offer; fold back the covers of a bed)
- *turn into* (become something different; make something become different)
- *turn off* (stop a machine or light; leave a road; make someone lose interest)
- *turn on* (start a machine or light; suddenly attack someone; excite someone; depend on something, formal)
- *turn over* (move something so the other side faces up; give someone to the police or give control to someone; think about something carefully)
- *turn up* (make a sound or heat higher; arrive; be found after being lost; find by searching)
- *use up*, *wake up* (stop sleeping; start to notice a problem), *watch out* (be careful; look out for someone)
- *wash up*, whose meaning depends on the variety: wash the dishes in British English, wash your hands and face in American English, and be carried onto a beach by the sea in both. The entry labels each sense and explains the difference.
- *work out* (find an answer; exercise; end well; plan; come to an amount)

*Wait for* was not written: it is a verb + preposition pattern already covered by sense 1 of *wait*, and a settled house rule says such patterns do not get their own entries. It is marked as a duplicate in the queue.

Two learner errors that I think are especially useful: many languages say *open* and *close* the light or the TV, so *turn on* and *turn off* both carry *Please close the light* → *Please turn off the light*. And *turn on* shows that word order changes the meaning: *the dog turned on him* (attacked him) is not *the dog turned him on*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two outside reviewer models raised 28 issues; four entries drew none. I made 28 decisions: 15 applied and 13 rejected.

Applied, among others:

- *turn down*: both reviewers objected that my summary sentence spoke of *moving an offer down*, which makes no sense. It now lists the three uses plainly.
- *turn off*: reviewer-a found my road examples unnatural (*turn off at the next exit*). They were replaced with clearer ones (*turn off just after the bridge*; *turn off the main road*).
- *wake up*: I had listed *I waked up* as an error, but the inflection table (generated from rules and the exceptions list) itself allows *waked* as a rare past form, so the two contradicted each other. The learner error was removed.
- *work out*: the claim that Americans say *works out to* and British speakers *works out at* is now hedged to "more common".

Rejected, among others: reviewer-b's repeated objection that the pattern *verb + adverb or preposition* is not on the list (it is; this is a settled house rule, listed in `NEXT.md` as a fence); reviewer-a's claim that defining *turn on* (formal sense) as *depend on something* is circular (it uses a different word); and reviewer-a's view that *look out* is not a synonym of *watch out*.

## Pronunciation

Ten of the eleven came back "disputed": only one of the three pronunciation models agreed with each transcription. This is a known problem, recorded in `wiki/notes/phrasal-verb-stress-recheck.md`: all three models put the main stress on the small word (*turn DOWN*), but one of them writes the stress mark differently, and the checking tool counts that as disagreement. The entries carry the `pronunciation-disputed` flag and one line in the curator file with the working assumption. *Turn into*, where the stress is on *turn*, was verified. The note proposes a small tool fix; it needs a logged decision in a later run.
