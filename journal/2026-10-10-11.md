## What this cycle did

A **build cycle** writes new entries from the queue, the project's ordered list of words still to be written. Until this morning the queue had been handing out band-1 nouns (band 1 is the most common words), and the last of those, *youth*, was written at 05:38 today. The claim tool now hands out band-1 adjectives in alphabetical order. The dictionary had only 42 adjectives before this cycle.

I drafted twelve: *annual, ashamed, automatic, average, basic, beautiful, bitter, black, blind, blue, bold, bored*. Each has two to five meanings, with examples, typical word partners (collocations), plain-English pronunciation notes, word-family links, and common learner errors where they exist. Some highlights:

- *ashamed* and *beautiful* each have a short note on choosing between near-synonyms: *ashamed*, *embarrassed*, and *guilty*; and *beautiful*, *pretty*, *handsome*, and *good-looking*.
- *black* has five meanings, three idioms (*black and blue*, *black and white*, *in black and white*), and a usage note. The note says that the word for people is often written with a capital, **Black**, and that using *a black* as a noun for a person is offensive.
- *bored* has two learner errors: *I am boring* when the speaker means *I am bored*, and *bored about* for *bored with*. It also notes that the informal *bored of* is common in speech, though some people object to it.
- Idioms were placed by the house rule that an idiom goes under its first noun. So *out of the blue* is under *blue*, but *turn a blind eye* stays under *eye*.

Every entry then went through the full pipeline: format validation; the inflection rules, which generate forms such as *blacker* (*average* needed one more grammar code first); the pronunciation panel and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary, which confirmed all 24 transcriptions; and both outside reviewers, reading every field.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 35 issues, 22 of them marked blocking. I applied 19 and rejected 16. *bold* and *bored* drew no issues.

Applied, among others:

- *black*, the meaning for people: I had defined it as belonging to "a group of people who have dark skin". The first reviewer rightly said that *Black* is a racial identity, not a description of skin color alone. It now reads "belonging to a race of people who have dark skin, or connected with them". A short explanation says it is used especially of people whose families came from Africa. *Africa* cannot appear in the definition: it is not on your approved list of proper names.
- *black coffee* is now "without milk or cream". A tea example no longer mentions sugar. The awkward *a black sense of fun* became *a black sense of humor*; both reviewers flagged it.
- *blind*: one meaning mixed *blind panic* (a feeling too strong to think through) with *blind faith* (belief not based on reason). These are now a meaning and a sub-meaning.
- *ashamed to do something* was redefined. It means not wanting to do something because it would make you feel bad about yourself in front of others. I also dropped a learner error that was too absolute: you can truly be ashamed of forgetting a name.
- *average*: the summary line and signpost no longer suggest "the middle number", which is a different calculation.
- *beautiful*: the note no longer claims it is always the strongest word of its group.
- *blue* meaning sad is now defined as "feeling sad and without energy", not just "sad". *annual*'s usage note now says "usually" before a noun, not always.

Rejected:

- The second reviewer said five times that *comparative with more* is not a grammar code. It is one of the dictionary's fixed codes.
- The first reviewer objected five times to short notes such as "Used of coffee and tea". A standing house rule puts such restrictions on who or what a word describes in exactly that field.

The cross-reference tool added one return link: *attractive* now lists *beautiful* as a synonym. I checked it; it is on the right meaning.
