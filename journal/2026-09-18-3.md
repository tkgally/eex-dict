## What was drafted

Fourteen new entries, all determiners (the small function words that go directly before a noun, like *the* or *some*): **least**, **less**, **little**, **many**, **more**, **most**, **much**, **my**, **neither**, **no**, **other**, **our**, **several**, and **such**. This closes out most of the dictionary's core set of quantity words (*much/many/more/most*, *little/less/least*) and possessives (*my*, *our*). Every entry went through the full pipeline: draft, check related word forms and pronunciation (against a panel of AI models plus, for American English, a freely consulted pronunciation reference), send to two independent AI reviewers for a field-by-field critique, read every objection myself, and fix or reject each one with a one-line reason recorded permanently.

## What the reviewers caught

The dominant pattern, flagged by both reviewers across several entries, was one this dictionary has hit before: folding a different grammatical use of the same word into the wrong entry. *Several*, *many*, and *most* can all be followed by "of" (*several of the guests*) — but in that form the word stands in for a noun (a "pronoun" use) rather than directly describing one, and house rule reserves that for a separate entry. I had written full example sentences demonstrating the "of" pattern inside the determiner entries; both reviewers caught it on *several*, one on *many*. Removed throughout, with the pointer to the future pronoun entry kept as a one-line mention rather than a worked example.

A second catch: two idioms were filed under the wrong headword. The dictionary's rule is that an idiom lives under its first meaningful word. *Once more* has no noun or verb, so its first meaningful word is "once", not "more" — I dropped it rather than misfile it further. *More or less* belongs under "more", not "less" where I'd first put it; moved.

Several collocation lists needed trimming: *no way* is really an interjection, not an example of "no" before a noun; *no small* and *no ordinary* were incomplete fragments needing a noun (*no small task*); *such as* is already a separate, queued word in its own right.

Two usage notes describing "double negatives" (*I don't have no time*) were softened from calling them merely "avoided in careful English" to the more accurate "not standard English" — a fair correction from one reviewer.

I did not accept every objection. A cluster of single-reviewer claims would have broken patterns this dictionary already settled on sibling entries: that *little*/*a little* can't share one entry with two senses (they can — *few*/*a few* already do this, reviewed and accepted); that the "two" versus "three or more" distinction between *less/least* and *more/most* is wrong (a deliberate teaching simplification used consistently across the whole family); and that *at least*/*at most* don't belong under their determiner entries (they do, by the same first-word rule that already placed *either...or* under *either*). Rejected, with the matching precedent cited each time.

One pronunciation stayed genuinely unsettled: for *several*, the model panel favored a fuller, three-syllable pronunciation, but the CMU dictionary (the open reference) backed my two-syllable version for American English, so I kept it and flagged the entry. British English has no equivalent open reference to settle the same disagreement, so that half of the question goes to the owner's queue with my version kept meanwhile.
