# Run report, 2026-10-26

## Cycle 1: site

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a site check. A site check rebuilds the public website from the dictionary's entry files and then opens the pages in a real browser, at a phone width and a desktop width, to make sure nothing is broken. The scheduler gave this reason: of all the kinds of work that were allowed today, the site check had gone longest without being done. Writing new entries from the words the dictionary already uses ("closure") is still blocked, because your ruling of 2026-10-24 says milestone 1, the list of about 2,400 basic defining words, must be finished first; 120 of those words are still unwritten.

Before the site check, the cycle did its usual safety checks. There were no open pull requests left behind by an earlier run, and no "Entry report" issues from readers on GitHub. One leftover branch, `claude/gallant-carson-ax7wls`, belongs to yesterday's run; its work is all on `main` already, so nothing was lost. This run's tools cannot delete branches, so I added a line to `reviews/needs_curator.txt` asking for it to be pruned.

**The result.** The build tool (`tools/build_site.py`) turned 2,924 entries into 2,538 headword pages. A headword page can hold more than one entry, for example a noun and a verb with the same spelling, which is why there are fewer pages than entries. No entries were held back as unfinished drafts, and there were no renamed entries needing redirect pages.

The browser check (`tools/site_check.py`) ran 79 tests, and all 79 passed. Among other things, it confirmed that:

- no page scrolls sideways on a 360-pixel phone screen, across all 2,544 pages;
- searching for an inflected form such as *ran* finds **run** first, and pressing Enter opens it;
- a phrase can be found by its words (*a cut above* finds *a cut above someone or something*), and a misspelling such as *abiilty* offers "Did you mean **ability**";
- hovering on a linked word shows a short preview of its entry;
- the translator's view can be turned on and off, and the setting survives a reload;
- dark mode works, the menu behaves at both widths, and the sense menu on long entries such as **act** jumps to the right sense;
- a cross-reference to a word with no entry yet is shown in plain grey, as your ruling C2 asks;
- an address for a page that does not exist gives a "not found" page that searches for the word.

The twelve adjectives written in yesterday's last cycle (**bent** to **lucky**) appear and render normally.

**What changed.** Nothing in the build tool or its page templates needed fixing, so no code changed. The website files themselves are never stored in the repository; GitHub builds and publishes them after each merge.

**Spend.** Nothing. A site check uses no paid model calls.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** If the run clock allows, the next cycle will take whatever the scheduler picks: most likely building more milestone-1 words, or continuing the coverage review of the most important common words (next on that list are **fall**, **come**, **catch**, **ground**, **fresh**, and **over**). ## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build: writing new entries from the queue (`headwords/queue.tsv`, the ordered list of words still to be written). Under your ruling A2 the queue serves milestone 1 first, the basic defining words, so this cycle took the next twelve: the adjectives **ordinary**, **ripe**, **salty**, **selfish**, **sour**, **spoken**, **steep**, **sticky**, **temporary**, **unfriendly**, and **worn**, and the adverb **greatly**. Milestone 1 now has 108 words left.

Each entry went through the full pipeline: the format check, the inflection tool (which writes forms such as *riper* and *stickier* from rules), the pronunciation panel (several models transcribe each word and their votes are compared, along with the open CMU pronouncing dictionary for American English), both outside reviewers, my decision on every point they raised, and the checks again.

A few things worth knowing about the entries:

- **ordinary** has a second, slightly negative sense (*the food was very ordinary*), the phrase *out of the ordinary*, and a translator's note warning that in some languages a look-alike word means *rude*.
- **salty** covers taste, lively but slightly rude language (*salty language*), and the informal *annoyed* (*he's still salty about losing*).
- **sour** covers taste, milk that has gone bad, a bad mood, and things that *go sour*.
- **steep** covers a slope, a sudden large change (*a steep rise*), and, informally, a price that is too high.
- **temporary** has a learner error for *a temporal job*, a real mistake for speakers of languages where the look-alike word means *temporary*.
- **greatly** has a learner error for using it before plain adjectives (*greatly interesting*).

**Pronunciation.** All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) ended up verified. The panel did not accept my first British form of **salty** (ˈsɔːl.ti). I changed it to ˈsɒl.ti, the form the dictionary's entry for **salt** already uses, and kept the other as a variant. It then passed.

**What the reviewers found.** They raised 45 issues; 8 were blocking (meaning the entry should not stand as it is). I accepted 34 and rejected 11.

The blocking ones were real. In **worn**, one example (*the steps were worn smooth by many feet*) and two collocations were really the verb **wear** in the passive, not the adjective. I replaced them (*my favorite jeans are worn at the knees*). In **sour**, one sense was coded "comparative with more" while the entry gives *sourer*; I removed the code. In **unfriendly**, an example about plastic bags made a claim about the world, which the house rules forbid; I replaced it with a neutral sentence, and I reworded a confusing explanation.

Among the minor points, I accepted that *unfriendlier* is standard, so the entry now shows it. **steep**'s summary sentence (the "core idea") now covers the price sense. **greatly** lost a claim that it is "a little formal", which no label backed up. **sticky** gained *a sticky note*.

The rejections were mostly reviewer A calling a gradability code a duplicate of the inflection note. That note is written by a script from the code, so there is no real duplication; the house rules already settle this. Two more asked for idioms whose keyword is another word (*a ripe old age* belongs under **age**, *a steep learning curve* under **curve**).

**Cross-references.** The linking tool adds a matching link in the other entry when it can tell which sense is meant. Seven times it could not tell. For three I placed the link by hand: **fresh** now lists **sour** as an opposite, **sharp** lists **steep** as a synonym, and **sour** points to **salty**. The other four synonyms were loose, so I removed them from the new entries instead.

**Spend.** About US$0.33, for pronunciation and reviewer B. Reviewer A runs on your subscription at no charge to the budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** More milestone-1 words, the coverage review of the most important words (next: **fall**, **come**, **catch**), and lint in about two runs.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a review: rereading existing entries and improving them, rather than writing new ones. Under your ruling B1 the review mode is working through a "coverage pass" over the most important common words, those with the most senses first. It asks of each entry: is any common use missing, and does every example fit the sense it sits under? This cycle read six entries: **fall**, **come**, **catch**, **ground**, **fresh**, and **over** (the adverb). Each went through both outside reviewers and my decision on every point they raised.

**What was missing, and is now there.**

- **fall**: *fall into a group* (*most students fall into this age group*); *fall to someone*, meaning to become their duty; *fall in battle* (labeled literary); a face falling in disappointment; and eyes, blame or stress *falling on* something.
- **catch**: being *caught in the rain* or *in traffic*; hitting someone (*the ball caught her on the head*); a fire starting to burn; and the informal goodbye *catch you later*.
- **come**: one reviewer pointed out that a single sense mixed two meanings, happening at a time and having a place in an order. I split it. The new "order" sense holds *B comes before C*, *family comes first*, and race positions (*our team came in second*), which had been filed under the wrong sense. Also new: *come to an end* or *a decision*; *come as a surprise*; *come into view* or *into effect*; *music comes naturally to her*; the phrase *to come* (*for years to come*); and the sexual sense, labeled informal, which the taboo-vocabulary rules say to include and define plainly.
- **ground**: the phrases *middle ground*, *give ground*, *run something into the ground*, *break ground* (American, starting to build), and *burn to the ground*.
- **fresh**: the old-fashioned American *don't get fresh with me*.
- **over**: bending (*lean over*, *bend over*), handing something over, staying the night (*stay over*), and liquid boiling over.

**What the reviewers found.** They raised 149 issues, 11 of them blocking (meaning the entry should not stand as it is). I accepted 120 and rejected 29.

The blocking ones were real:

- In **over**, *lean over so I can see* was filed under "through the air above a place". It is bending, so it went to the new bending sense. *We painted over the old color* was the preposition, not the adverb, so I replaced it with *the cut soon healed over*.
- In **catch**, *you won't catch me eating there again* is an idiom meaning "I would never". It did not fit the sense "see someone doing something wrong", so I replaced it.
- Two senses of **fresh** had muddled definitions: air *from outside*, and a "clean, cool" sense that did not fit clean sheets. I rewrote both.

Many minor points were also right:

- Several "learner errors" were regularized forms (*falled*, *catched*). Your notes rules ask for those to be removed, so they are gone.
- Several entries said the same fact in three or four places, such as "fall never takes an object" and "catch, caught, caught". Each fact is now in one place.
- Some examples made claims about the world (*fresh vegetables are better for you*, *milk comes from cows*). I replaced them.
- Two translator's notes were generic. They now name a specific trap: many languages call fresh water *sweet* water, and the "reason" sense of **ground** is unrelated to soil.

I rejected three kinds of point:

- Idioms whose keyword is another word: *fall into place*, *catch your breath*, *come to mind*.
- Requests to mark *broke* and *stood* in the **ground** phrases. The site marks the headword, and here that is *ground*, which appears unchanged.
- Claims that a verb pattern such as *usually passive* is not on the closed list. It is.

I also turned down moving the "reason" sense of **ground** ahead of the land senses.

**Other changes.** When a review removes a cross-reference, its mirror in the other entry goes too. **drop** lost a "compare" link to **fall**, and **jump** lost an "opposite" link to **fall**. *Ground rules* was added to the queue as a compound noun of its own.

**Spend.** About US$0.51.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with **hand** (one of the three model entries in the style guide), then **wild**, **weak**, **turn**, **start**, **side**, and **hold**.
