# 2026-10-04 (18): the seventeenth originality check, two rewrites

*Second cycle of the run that started at 16:44 UTC on 4 October 2026.*

## What this cycle did

The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose **originality**, which it forces every tenth run. The originality check asks one question: does any of our wording match a published dictionary too closely? Every entry must be written from the drafter's own knowledge, never copied, and this check is how slips are caught.

A script picked ten pieces of text at random from the dictionary: eight definitions and two usage notes. For each one I ran an exact-phrase web search, and I asked one of the outside reviewing models (reviewer-a) whether each piece reads as copied. Each piece then got one of three verdicts: **original** (no match), **generic overlap** (a short, plain definition that any dictionary would write the same way, such as *a female child* for *girl*), or **rewrite** (it follows a published dictionary's distinctive wording and must be rewritten).

## What was found

Five pieces were original: the definitions in *axe*, *above*, *set an example* (under *example*), and *fun*, and the usage note on *other*. Three were generic overlap: *girl* (*a female child*), the *share* sense *have the same feeling, opinion, or interest as someone else*, and the usage note on *yourself*; they say the obvious thing in the obvious way. Two needed rewriting:

- *close* (verb), sense 2, read *stop being open to the public for a time, or make a shop or office do this*. The Longman dictionary has *if a shop or building closes, or you close it, it stops being open to the public for a period of time*. The ideas are simple, but the frame is the same. It now reads *finish business for the day or for a short period so that customers cannot come in, or make a shop or office do this*.
- *wave* (verb), sense 3, read *hold something in your hand and move it in the air*. The Oxford Learner's dictionary has *to hold something in your hand and move it from side to side*. It now reads *move something you are holding through the air several times, for example so that people notice it or to threaten someone*.

Neither new wording turns up in an exact-phrase search. Because a rewrite is a change to an entry, both entries went back to the reviewers. They raised six points; I accepted one (an awkward example, *He waved the waiter to come over*, is now *The teacher waved the children to sit down*) and rejected five with reasons logged (for example, *open* really is the opposite of *close* a meeting).

## About the reviewer's answers

reviewer-a said "copied" for nine of the ten and gave quotations it said came from the Cambridge dictionary. Search could not confirm any of those quotations word for word, and several of them look like our own wording given back with *to* in front. Yet its two firmest claims did point at the two real problems, which other dictionaries (Longman and Oxford Learner's) showed. As in recent checks, the verdicts here come from search, with the reviewer's answers recorded beside them. In the previous check, earlier today, the same model answered "cannot tell" on all ten; its behavior on this task swings widely.
