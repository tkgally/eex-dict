# Report for 2026-11-20 (second run)

## Cycle 1: closure

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a closure cycle. "Closure" means writing entries for words the dictionary already relies on, so that a reader who follows a link finds an entry rather than a grey, unlinked word. The first source of such words, words used inside definitions, is empty for now, so the cycle took the next source: "family" words, which are related forms that existing entries list in their word-family box (for example, *swimmer* is listed by *swim*). Twelve nouns were written, all in frequency band 2 (common but not core): *sunlight*, *supporter*, *surroundings*, *survivor*, *suspect*, *sweatshirt*, *swim*, *swimmer*, *tag*, *takeout*, *teaching*, and *teammate*.

**How they were written.** I wrote every definition, example, and note myself in this session. To save typing the long, repetitive JSON layout, I put my wording into a short throwaway helper in my scratch folder (outside the repository) that only arranged it into the entry file format; later fixes after review went in the same way, one entry and one field at a time, each chosen by me. No script generated or changed any wording. The helper is not committed.

Some points of interest in the new entries:

- *surroundings* is always plural, and the entry carries a learner error for the common mistake *the surrounding is very quiet*.
- *suspect* (the noun) has a note for translators that the noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second, plus the phrase *the usual suspects*.
- *tag* has five senses: a label, a word added to something online, the children's chasing game, a name painted on a wall, and the short question at the end of a sentence (*isn't it?*).
- *takeout* is labeled American, with the British form *takeaway* recorded beside it.
- *supporter* has a second sense for a sports-team follower, labeled British because Americans usually say *fan*.

**Pronunciation.** All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, three or four models voting, with the open CMU pronouncing dictionary as a further check where it has the word. For *teammate* the CMU dictionary disagreed with the American form (it likely runs the two m sounds together), but three of four models agreed, which meets the project's rule.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** Two reviewer models from different companies read every field of every entry. They raised 30 issues, 4 of them "blocking" (serious enough that the entry cannot be published until settled). I applied 27 and rejected 3, each decision logged with a reason.

The real catches:

- *swim* (noun) and *swimmer* were defined with the verb *swim* itself, which the style guide forbids because a reader who does not know the word learns nothing. Both were rewritten in other words.
- The one-sentence summary at the top of *tag* described the children's game as "a touch", but the entry defines it as the game itself. The summary was rewritten.
- *suspect* had an example about software causing a computer fault under a sense about people the police suspect. That use now has its own sub-sense (*a thing that people think may be the cause of a problem*).
- An example in *teaching* named a religious figure; it was replaced with a neutral one.
- *takeout* was marked as countable; Americans do not say *a takeout* for a meal, so it is now uncountable and has no plural.
- Smaller fixes: *teammate* now says *on the same team* (the American form), *supporter* covers supporting a person as well as an idea, *survivor* now covers violence from another person, and *tag* covers metal tags such as those on a pet's collar.

Rejected: a British label for the grammar sense of *tag* (the word is used everywhere; only the compounds *question tag* and *tag question* differ, and both are shown); a separate sense for a takeout restaurant (Americans say *a takeout place*, shown among the typical word combinations); and a claim that *get used to your surroundings* is not a verb + noun combination.

**Links fixed by hand.** The cross-reference tool links an entry to the matching sense of the word it points at, and it could not decide three. I placed them myself: *supporter* now points to *fan* the person (a separate entry from *fan* the machine, which it had wrongly pointed to), and *environment* and *label* now point back to *surroundings* and *tag*.

**Spend.** US$0.30 this cycle (reviews and pronunciation checks); US$1.06 of the US$15 daily budget used so far today.

**What did not work.** Two small slips, both caught before review: my first draft of *suspect* left out two required parts of the phrase record, and I mistyped the American transcription of *surroundings*. The validator caught the first; I caught the second on rereading. Neither reached the reviewers.

**What is next.** The scheduler will pick the next cycle. Closure continues with family words after *teammate*; the next maintenance (lint) pass is due in three cycles; the weekly digest is due tomorrow.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. The older items (pronunciation disputes and the ten-entry samples in the weekly digests) still wait for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build cycle: new entries taken in order from the main word queue (the list of headwords waiting to be written, ordered by importance). I claimed eight rather than the allowed twelve, to keep each entry full. All eight are band-2 nouns (common but not core): *summary*, *sunglasses*, *sunrise*, *sunset*, *sunshine*, *supervisor*, *supplier*, and *surgeon*. As in cycle 1, I wrote all the wording myself and used a throwaway helper outside the repository only to lay it out in the file format.

Points of interest:

- *summary* has the phrase *in summary*.
- *sunglasses* is plural only, with a learner error for *a new sunglasses* (the fix is *a pair of sunglasses*).
- *sunset* has the phrase *ride off into the sunset*.
- *supervisor* has a second, British sense for the university teacher who guides a student's research.
- *surgeon* has a note for translators: in Britain a surgeon is usually called *Mr.*, *Ms.*, or *Mrs.*, not *Dr.*, which surprises many readers.

**Pronunciation.** All 16 transcriptions were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, and the open CMU dictionary agreed with every American form.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** The two reviewers raised 26 issues, 3 of them blocking. I applied 21 and rejected 5.

The real catches:

- I had defined the second sense of *sunrise* and *sunset* as "the colors in the sky". Both reviewers pointed out that when people *watch the sunset* they watch the sun itself going down, not just the colors. Both senses now say "the sight of the sun going down (or coming up), and the colors it makes in the sky".
- The phrase *ride off into the sunset* had an example about a film's happy ending, but my definition only covered leaving a job at the end of a career. I widened the definition to cover both, since both uses are common.
- *sunshine* was missing its common meaning of happiness, or a person who makes you happy (*the new baby brought sunshine into their lives*). It now has that sense. The well-known phrase *a ray of sunshine* belongs under *ray* by the house rule that a phrase is filed under its first noun, so I noted it for when *ray* is next written or reviewed.
- The time-of-day senses of *sunrise* and *sunset* (*at sunset*) are now marked uncountable.
- Both reviewers said *resume* is a confusing synonym for *summary*, because in American English a *resume* is usually a document listing your work history. The existing *resume* entry already has a "summary" sense that points here, so I kept the link but added a short warning note.
- In the *surgeon* note, the titles were marked as quoted examples rather than as words being talked about; corrected.

Rejected:

- Two requests to hand-mark *rode* in *he rode off into the sunset*. The house marking rule applies to forms of the headword (*sunset*); *rode* is not one.
- A complaint that the address sense of *sunshine* (*Morning, sunshine!*) should not be defined with "used when speaking to someone". The dictionary already uses that form for words of address such as *darling*.
- Two smaller points about the grammar and labels of that address sense.

**Other changes.** The *glasses* and *sunglasses* entries pointed to each other twice, once as related words and once as "see also". I removed the duplicate. The *adviser* entry turns out to lack the university sense (*a student's academic adviser*); noted for its next review.

**Spend.** About US$0.20 this cycle; US$1.26 of the US$15 daily budget used so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The decision-logging tool asked me to say which of two issues on the same field I meant in four cases; I did.

**What is next.** The scheduler will choose the next cycle. The maintenance (lint) pass is due in two cycles, and the weekly digest tomorrow.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: review

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose a review cycle: re-reading published entries rather than writing new ones. With no drafts waiting and no reader reports open, the cycle continued the "coverage pass", the owner's instruction of 2026-10-24 that every common (band 1) word be read again to check that no current meaning or common phrase is missing, and that the notes follow the style guide's "one fact, one field" rule (each fact is stated in one place in an entry, not repeated in several boxes). Four entries were read: *effect* (noun), *empty* (adjective), *end* (verb), and *eye* (noun). This brings the pass to 393 words.

**What I added before the reviewers saw the entries.**

- *effect*: the phrases *to good effect* and *to the effect that*, and the common combinations *ripple effect* and *domino effect*. Its usage note had repeated the *effect*/*affect* warning already given as a learner error; it now keeps only the separate point about the rare formal verb *effect a change*.
- *empty*: the pattern *empty of something* (*the room was empty of furniture*).
- *end*: the phrases *end it all* (a gentle way of saying "kill yourself", labeled euphemistic), *end your days*, and *to end all* (*a party to end all parties*).
- *eye*: a new sense for looking and attention (*all eyes were on the bride*, *keep your eyes on the road*), and five phrases: *not bat an eye*, *in the public eye*, *have your eye on something*, *more than meets the eye*, and *open someone's eyes to something*. A usage note and a translator's note that both repeated the learner errors were removed.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** 47 issues, 5 blocking; I applied 43 and rejected 4.

The real catches:

- *end* told learners that **in** is for a result and **at** is for a time, but its own first sense has *the school year ends in June*. Both are right: **at** goes with a clock time, **in** with a month or year. The learner-error note now says so, and a translator's note that repeated the same rule was removed.
- *effect*'s sense for special effects was defined for films, plays, and shows, but one example was about a game. Sound effects in games are common, so the definition now includes games.
- I had coded the new looking-and-attention sense of *eye* as "usually plural", but most of its examples (*under the watchful eye of*) are singular. The code is gone, and the sense now comes second, before the less common "ability to judge" sense.
- *up to your eyes in work* is mainly British (Americans tend to say *up to my ears*); it now carries a British label.
- Further additions the reviewers asked for: *before your eyes*, *with an eye to something*, *a black eye*, *roll your eyes* (*eye*) and *put something into effect* (*effect*).
- Smaller fixes: *see eye to eye* means simply "agree", not "agree completely"; *catch someone's eye* is now defined so that it fits both a person and a thing (*a red dress caught my eye*); *crowded* moved to the "no people" sense of *empty*; a translator's note on *eye* that only said "idioms are not translated word for word" was removed as too general.

Rejected: *on an empty stomach* (it is already in the *stomach* entry, filed under its noun by the house rule); a claim that *run out* is not a synonym of *end* (the linked sense is *my passport runs out next month*, which does mean "ends"); a doubt about the *informal* label on *to end all*; and a claim that *humorous* is not a register label (it is in the dictionary's closed list of register labels).

**Spend.** About US$0.22 this cycle; US$1.48 of the US$15 daily budget used so far today.

**What did not work.** One new definition used *extreme*, a word outside the defining vocabulary (the limited word list that definitions are written in); the vocabulary check caught it and I changed it to *worst*. My decision log for that item still says "most extreme"; the entry is right.

**What is next.** The maintenance (lint) pass is due next cycle. The coverage pass continues with *far* (adjective and adverb), *feed*, and *film*.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build cycle again: new entries in order from the main word queue. This was the run's fourth and last allowed cycle, so I kept it to six entries. All six are band-2 nouns: *surname*, *survey*, *swear word*, *sweet*, *swimsuit*, and *sympathy*. As before, I wrote all the wording myself; a throwaway helper outside the repository only arranged it into the file format.

Points of interest:

- *surname* has a note for translators: in some languages the family name comes first, so the English form labels *first name* and *last name* can confuse; *surname* or *family name* is clearer.
- *survey* has four senses (asking people questions, a general study of a subject, measuring land, and the British check of a house before buying it) and a note that the noun is stressed on the first part, the verb on the second.
- *sweet* (noun) separates *sweets* meaning sugary foods in general (used everywhere: *cut down on sweets*), the British word for a single piece of candy, and an old-fashioned British word for dessert.
- *sympathy* has three senses (feeling sorry for someone, what you say to someone after a death, and support for someone's side) and a "false friends" note: in several European languages a similar-looking word means "liking", which English *sympathy* does not.

**Pronunciation.** All 12 transcriptions were confirmed by the panel.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** 27 issues, 1 blocking; I applied 24 and rejected 3.

The real catches:

- The one blocking issue: my example *What's for sweet?* sounds dated even in Britain, where people say *pudding* or *dessert*. The dessert sense now carries an "old-fashioned" label and a less awkward example.
- Two senses of *sweet* were defined with the word *sweet* itself; both were rewritten.
- My learner error for *sympathy* said *sympathy with* is only for ideas, but the entry's own third sense has *in sympathy with the protesters*. I replaced it with a clearer error that learners really make: *sympathy to her* instead of *sympathy for her*.
- The note on *surname* first repeated the definition's point that the surname comes last in English; that sentence was removed.
- I had given *swimsuit* the "clothing" subject label, but the dictionary keeps such labels for specialist words, and everyday garments such as *shirt* and *sweater* have none. Removed. The same label sits on *sweatshirt* and *sunglasses* from earlier today; they are noted for their next review, because changing a published entry outside the review process is not allowed.
- Smaller fixes: *sympathy* no longer says it includes "wanting to help"; its comparison with *pity* now says what the difference is (*pity* can sound as if you feel better than the other person); the British spelling *swearword* is no longer marked British, since it is found in both varieties.

Rejected: a claim that the British *costume* (as in *swimming costume*) is not a synonym of *swimsuit*; a claim that two senses of *sweet* overlap (they differ in region and in whether you can say *a sweet*); and a small point about how the third sense of *sympathy* is counted.

**Spend.** About US$0.15 this cycle. The whole run spent about US$0.87 across four cycles; US$1.63 of the US$15 daily budget is used so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. A definition first used *English*, which is outside the defining vocabulary; the check caught it and I reworded it.

**What is next.** The next run's scheduler is likely to choose the maintenance (lint) pass, which is due. The coverage pass continues with *far*, *feed*, and *film*; the weekly digest is due tomorrow.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this run.
