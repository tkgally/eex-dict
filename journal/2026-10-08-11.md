## What the reviewers found

Two reviewer models from different companies, called reviewer-a and reviewer-b, read every field. Reviewer-b's first reply on *station* came back empty, so it was run again, and the second reply was complete. Together they raised 34 issues, 27 of them "blocking", meaning the entry cannot be published until each is settled. I decided each one myself: 22 applied and 12 rejected, all logged with a reason.

Reviewer-a was right on these points, among others:

- *sport* said that *practice sport* is always wrong. You can practice a sport, meaning train at it. The learner error now targets *make sport*, which is a real mistake.
- *star* limited a famous star to actors, singers, and sports players. The definition now covers any well-known person and the main performer in a show, and the rating sense now covers anything rated, not only hotels, restaurants, and films.
- *state of the art* was defined as a noun phrase with *the*. It is normally used without *the* (*this camera is state of the art*), so the definition and examples changed.
- *stairs* had a learner error that was really about *upstairs*. It was removed, and the usage note keeps the point.
- Smaller fixes: *move a square* is not English (now *move two squares*), and *have spring* needs *a lot of*.

I rejected reviewer-a where the house rules decide otherwise. It wanted *a state visit* and *a state dinner* treated as adjectives. In this dictionary a noun used before another noun is still the noun, and such pairs are listed as collocations. It also wanted *state of the art* moved out of the *state* entry, but idioms stay under their keyword noun.
