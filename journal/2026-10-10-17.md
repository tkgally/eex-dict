## What this cycle did

A **review cycle** gives existing entries a second reading by the two outside AI reviewers (the **panel**). Every entry has been through the panel once before it was published; about 850 have been through it only once. The project's mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job) chose review. I took the twelve oldest of these one-round entries, all created on 3 October: *practical* (adjective), *thread*, *fee*, *feeling*, *fence*, *festival*, *fever*, *field*, *fight*, *figure*, *file*, *film* (all nouns).

I first read each entry for gaps: missing common senses, idioms, learner errors, and claims that are no longer true. What I changed by hand:

- *thread*: the idiom *lose the thread* (stop following what someone is saying, or forget what you were going to say), with two examples. It had been listed only as a collocation; that listing was removed so the idiom is in one place.
- *fight*: the idiom *pick a fight* (start a fight or an argument on purpose).
- *feeling*: the phrase *have mixed feelings about something* (feel both pleased and unhappy about it).

The note now says it is a different word with the same spelling, not covered here. The tool word joins the list of same-spelling words (**homographs**) that need their own entries when the tooling for them is fixed.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 20 issues on the eleven entries, 16 marked blocking. I applied 9 and rejected 11. *fee*, *festival*, and *fight* drew no issues.

Applied:

- *fever*: the entry said *have a temperature* was British. It is used in American English too, though less often, so it now says "especially common in British English", in both the usage note and the note for translators. The learner-error note said to always use *a* (*I have a fever*); in fact bare *fever* is normal in lists of symptoms (*fever and a headache*), and one of the entry's own examples shows that. The note now says *a* is needed after *have*, and names the exception.
- *field*: the physics meaning was "an area in which a force, such as electricity, has an effect". Electricity is not itself a force. It is now "an area around something in which it pulls or pushes other things without touching them", in plain words. The idiom *have a field day* is now "enjoy a chance to do something very much, especially a chance to say bad things about someone", and has a second example.
- *film*: the comparison with *movie* said *film* is the usual British word. *Movie* is also common in Britain, and the note now says so.
- *thread*: a second example for *pick up the threads*.

Rejected:

- One reviewer objected five times on *file* that the metal tool should be part of this entry, and that a file is not a folder. The house rule gives same-spelling words with unrelated meanings their own entries, and a box or folder for papers is a real, common meaning of *file*.
- The other reviewer said that the two idiom definitions in *fence* (*sit on the fence*, *mend fences*) were not base-form verb phrases. They are.
- An objection that the summary sentence of *thread* was not grammatical; and one that the note on *practically* ("also means *almost*") misleads. The word *also* already marks *almost* as the extra meaning.
- A formatting objection on *feeling* that goes against the house rule for marking words in notes.
