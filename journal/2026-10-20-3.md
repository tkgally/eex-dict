# 2026-10-20, cycle 3 of the 00:43 UTC run: twelve phrasal verbs from *get away* to *go on*

## What this cycle did

This was a second **build** cycle in a row. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) again chose to write new entries, and the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written) continued through the most basic phrasal verbs, the two-word verbs such as *give up* that get their own entries.

I claimed the next twelve and wrote all of them: *get away, get back, get in, get off, get on, get out, get over, get up, give back, go away, go back, go on*. These are some of the most used verbs in English, with many senses each: *get out* has six (leave, escape, take something out, become known, avoid a duty, go out more), *go on* has six (continue, happen, do next, encourage, of time, start working).

A few points a learner needs got particular attention:

- **Which vehicle takes which particle.** You *get in* (and *get out of*) a car or a taxi but *get on* (and *get off*) a bus, a train, or a plane. Many languages use one verb for both, so this is in the learner-error boxes and the translator's notes of all four entries.
- ***Go on doing* and *go on to do*.** The first means continue the same thing; the second means do something new next (*She went on to become a doctor*).
- ***Wake up* and *get up*.** *Wake up* is to stop sleeping; *get up* is to leave your bed.
- **British and American choices.** The friendly sense of *get on* (*I get on well with my sister*) and *How are you getting on?* are labeled British, because Americans usually say *get along*; the label means *mainly used there*, not *only*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two outside reviewers raised 60 issues between them; I applied 39 and rejected 21.

Applied, the main ones:

- ***get over***: I had included *get something over with* (*Let's get this meeting over with*) as a fourth sense. Both reviewers said it is a different phrasal verb, and they are right: it has its own word, *with*, and its own meaning. The sense is gone, and *get over with* is queued as its own entry, with *get away with* and *go back on*, which the new entries point to.
- ***get back***: the *start again* sense did not cover two of its own examples, *get back to sleep* and *get back to normal*; it now reads *return to an activity, a subject, or a state that you were in before*.
- ***get off***: one definition mixed *have no punishment* with *help someone else avoid punishment*. The second use (*Her lawyer got her off*) is now explained separately.
- ***go on***: the learner-error example *She went on to talk for an hour* could be correct (she spoke next, and for an hour), so it was replaced with a sentence that is clearly wrong.
- Four opening summaries (the *core idea* sentences) were reworded so that they cover every sense, and a handful of definitions and collocations were widened or replaced (*get out* now includes taking something out of a pocket or drawer; *get up* sense 2 is *rise to your feet*).

Rejected:

- reviewer-a asked to drop the British labels on *get on*; house practice is that a region label means *mainly used there*, which is true here.
- reviewer-a objected to the *To go away is to ...* form of the core idea, which is house style.
- Nine of reviewer-b's issues asked for a grammar pattern label that is not in the dictionary's fixed list (a settled objection), three more asked for another pattern that is not in the list, and one asked to call *go back to sleep* intransitive, against house practice for a verb followed by its preposition.

The cross-reference script added links back from related entries (*escape*, *return*, *arrive*, *recover*, *leave*, *disappear*, *happen*); all landed on the right sense this time.
