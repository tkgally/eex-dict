I wrote each entry from my own knowledge, following the house style guide. Some points of interest:

- *disabled* has three senses: of a person, made for disabled people (*a disabled toilet*), and not working (*location services are disabled*). Its usage note says that *disabled people* and *people with disabilities* are both widely accepted, and that older words such as *handicapped* are now considered offensive by many people.
- *disappointed* has one sense, a note on the prepositions (*disappointed in* a person, *with* or *by* a thing), and the learner error *The film was very disappointed* (it should be *disappointing*).
- *distant* has five senses, from "far away" to a distant relative and a cold, distant manner, plus a short guide to choosing between *distant*, *far*, and *remote*.
- *dry* has six senses (not wet, weather, boring, humor, wine, no alcohol), with sub-senses for dry skin and a river that has run dry.
- *dumb* puts the everyday informal sense, "stupid", first. The old sense "cannot speak" is labelled offensive and old-fashioned, and the usage note tells learners what to say instead.
- *easy* has a guide to *easy* versus *simple* and two learner errors: repeating the object (*easy to read it*) and using *easy* where the adverb *easily* is needed.
- *early* has the phrase *at the earliest*. Its use as an adverb (*I got up early*) belongs in a separate entry, which is already in the queue.

## What the checks and reviewers found

The pronunciation check verified all 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word), and the inflection tool produced the comparative forms (*drier*, *earlier*, *easier*, and so on) or recorded that a word is not compared with *-er*.

Two reviewer models from different companies read every field. They raised 42 issues across nine entries; *disappointed*, *divorced*, and *early* came back with none. I read each one and accepted 32 and rejected 10. The main fixes:

- Both reviewers said that *the disabled* (meaning disabled people as a group) is a noun use and does not belong inside the adjective entry. I agreed and removed it; the usage note still mentions it.
- Both reviewers objected that the *dumb* summary sentence said what the word "first meant". A claim about a word's history belongs only in the etymology field, so I rewrote the sentence to describe today's uses.
- *dull*: three definitions were tightened (a dull sound is low and soft, not "unclear"; a dull pain need not last a long time; a dull day is cloudy rather than dark).
- *easy*: a fourth sense, *not strict* (*an easy teacher*), was added after a reviewer pointed out it was missing, and an example with *feel easy about* was replaced because it is not natural in American English.
- *disabled*: the British label on "made for disabled people" was dropped, because *disabled parking* is used in American English too.

The rejections, each logged with a reason: *foreign* stays as an opposite of *domestic* (*domestic and foreign policy* is a standard pair); *double the price* stays pointed to a *double* determiner entry, matching how this dictionary already treats *half the price*; and a reviewer's objection to the house style of the summary sentence ("Domestic describes...") goes against a settled rule.

After review, the cross-reference tool added back-links to existing entries. Two landed on the wrong sense and I moved them by hand: *dumb* now sits under the "intelligent" sense of *clever*, not its "rude" sense, and *distant* sits under the "a long way" sense of *far*.
