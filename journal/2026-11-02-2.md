# Run of 2026-11-02 (second run of the day)

## Cycle 1: closure

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work by weighing what is most overdue) chose *closure*: writing entries for words the dictionary already uses but does not yet define. Ten new entries were written and taken through the full review pipeline, and all ten are now published:

- Three words used in existing definitions: **missile** (the weapon, and the formal sense of an object thrown at someone), **rocket** (into space, as a weapon, and as a firework, with the idiom *it's not rocket science*), and **offensive** as an adjective (rude, unpleasant, attacking, and the American sports sense, which is often said with the stress on the first part).
- Three second entries for words with the same spelling but a different origin, which earlier runs had asked to be written by hand: **bat** the flying animal (with *as blind as a bat* and *like a bat out of hell*), **pound** the place where lost dogs or towed cars are kept, and **bit** the computing unit.
- Four words named in other entries' word-family boxes: **average** as a noun (with *on average* and a learner error, *in average*), **background** (five senses, from a person's life to a phone screen), **backyard** (the American and British senses differ), and **baker** (with the British *the baker's* for the shop, and *a baker's dozen*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Each entry goes to two independent reviewer models, which mark every field as fine or as an issue. They raised 32 issues, none of them *blocking* (serious enough to stop publication). I applied 27 and rejected 5, each with a logged reason. The useful catches: an example for the thrown-object sense of *missile* that did not actually show something thrown at people; *keen baker*, which is mainly British; *police pound*, which is not a typical phrase; a plural form of the fixed idiom *like a bat out of hell*; and a missing phrase, *against a background of*. Both reviewers objected to the translator notes on the two homograph entries (*bat*, *pound*), which only repeated that the two words are different; I removed them. The rejections were house-style points: a phrase named as a word takes bold marks, and a restriction on a sense's form belongs in its explanation.

Both reviewers also noted that the British salad leaf called *rocket* (American *arugula*) is missing. It has a different origin, so it needs its own second entry, `rocket-n-2`. The work queue has no way to list a second entry with the same spelling, so it is recorded in `NEXT.md` for a later run to claim by hand.

After review, the vocabulary check found five words in definitions that are outside the defining vocabulary (the limited word list definitions are written in): *furry*, *upside*, *insulting*, and *firework* twice. I rewrote those definitions in plainer words. Three cross-references needed a matching link placed by hand: *offensive* is now listed as a synonym in **rude**, and **garden** now points to **backyard** as the usual American word. The third, a synonym link from *pound* to **shelter**, was removed, because the **shelter** entry has no sense for an animal shelter.

**Spend.** US$0.32 for this cycle (pronunciation panel and reviewer B; reviewer A costs nothing). US$1.17 for the day, of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. All twenty pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel; *backyard* differed from the open pronouncing dictionary on stress but had a 3-of-4 model majority, which the house rule accepts.

**What is next.** More closure and family words in queue order; the hand claim of `rocket-n-2`; links from the original **bat** and **bit** entries to their new second entries when those are next reviewed.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: a second reading of entries that are already published. With no drafts and no open entry reports, the work was the coverage pass that the owner asked for on 2026-10-24: re-reading the most important everyday entries, the ones with the most senses first, to check that nothing a learner needs is missing. This cycle read four six-sense entries that had not been re-read since that date: **touch** (verb), **title**, **ticket**, and **think**.

What was added:

- **touch**: a new sense, *affect or be connected with* (*a problem that touches all of us*).
- **title**: a new sense for the words on screen at the start or end of a film (*the opening titles*); a subsense for the titles of old, powerful families (*he inherited the title*); and a subsense, labeled American, for the document that shows who owns a car. That last use had been hidden inside an explanation field, where the reviewers rightly said a learner would not find it.
- **ticket**: the idiom *write your own ticket*.
- **think**: a new sense, *consider someone to be something*, covering the common *be thought to be* and *think of someone as*; a subsense for *think of others*; and the polite reply *think nothing of it*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 49 issues, 5 of them *blocking* (serious enough to stop publication). All 5 were right and applied: four were about the car-title use in **title**, and one asked for *think of someone as*, which was missing. Of the 49, I applied 44 and rejected 5 with reasons (for example, *just the ticket* is not only British, and *touch and go* is fairly called informal).

Several fixes were about the house rule that one fact lives in one field. **think** said three times, in three places, that English puts *not* with *think* (*I don't think so*); it now says it once. **title** listed the common titles twice and stated the family-name rule twice. **ticket** defined a ticket as something you have paid for and then listed *a free ticket*; the definition now says *usually* paid. A synonym link between **touch** and **handle** was removed on both sides, since handling something is more than touching it. Two matching links were placed by hand: **affect** now lists **touch** as a synonym, and **credit** (the list of people who made a film) now points to the new screen sense of **title**.

**Spend.** US$0.21 for this cycle; US$1.38 for the day, of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with 55 unread six-sense entries, starting from **thin**, then the five-sense ones.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. The open question on names after titles (*Ms. Kim*) in **title** and three other entries still waits for your ruling; the working assumption is unchanged.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: new entries from the main work queue, which is now working through band-2 verbs (band 2 is the second of five frequency bands, "common": words most intermediate learners know or need soon). Six verbs were written, reviewed, and published:

- **trick**: one sense, with the patterns *trick someone into doing something* and *trick someone out of something*, and a common learner error (*he tricked me to sign* for *he tricked me into signing*).
- **trim**: cut a little off to make neater (hair, a beard, the fat off meat); make a little smaller (costs, a budget); and decorate (a dress trimmed with lace; the American *trim the tree*).
- **trouble**: worry someone; the polite request *Could I trouble you for the salt?*; cause pain or problems (*his knee has been troubling him*); and the formal *he didn't even trouble to say goodbye*.
- **type**: write with a keyboard; and, as a technical medical sense, test blood to find its group.
- **unite**: join together to work for the same aim, and join things or groups so that they form one.
- **update**: make something up to date, or become up to date (*the app updates automatically*); and give someone the latest news (*I'll update you tomorrow*). The pronunciation note explains that the verb is usually stressed on the second part and the noun on the first.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 26 issues, 3 of them *blocking*. One was right: I had tagged *I need to get my hair trimmed* with a grammar pattern that belongs to **get**, not to **trim**; it is removed. The other two were from reviewer B, which said that *usually passive* is a grammar code rather than a verb pattern. The dictionary's closed list of values puts it among the verb patterns, so both were rejected with that reason, and `NEXT.md` now tells later runs to reject this objection. Of the 26, I applied 22 and rejected 4.

Several applied fixes widened definitions so that every example fits: **trim** now covers cutting off an unwanted part (*trim the fat off the meat*) and removing a small part (*trimmed two pages from her report*); **update** now covers software that updates itself. *Modernize* was dropped as a synonym of **update**, since updating a file does not make it modern.

Six cross-references needed a matching link placed by hand in older entries, because the linking script could not tell which sense to attach them to: **reduce**, **disturb**, **write**, **combine**, and **inform** now point back to the new verbs. A loose synonym link from **unite** to **join** was removed instead, because no sense of **join** means quite the same thing.

**Spend.** US$0.17 for this cycle; US$1.55 for the day, of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order, after **update**.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
