A few points about the content:
- **slide** has six senses: a page in a talk shown on a screen (with the older photographic slide as a sub-sense), the playground slide, a fall in value (*stop the slide in sales*), a sliding movement, the glass slide used under a microscope, and the British hair clip.
- **slip** has four senses (a small mistake, a small piece of paper such as a *permission slip*, a fall, and the piece of clothing) and two idioms: *a slip of the tongue* and *give someone the slip*. The idiom *let something slip* stays on the list for the **let** entry, because idioms are filed under their first noun, else their first verb.
- **snap** covers the sound, the fastener on clothes, the informal British word for a photograph, and the informal American "something very easy" (*the test was a snap*), plus the phrases *a cold snap* and *in a snap*.
- **snowflake** includes the recent informal insult for a person thought to be too easily upset, labeled disapproving.
- **spam** has a learner error that learners really make: *I got a spam* instead of *a spam email*, because the word is uncountable.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** Each entry went to the two-reviewer panel: reviewer A (a Claude model, no charge) and reviewer B (a Google model, paid per call). They read every field and raise issues, some marked *blocking* (must be settled before the entry is published). They raised 28 issues, 3 blocking. I applied 26 and rejected 2.

The blocking ones were all right:

- The note for translators on **slide** only listed the senses and said they are different words in other languages. That is the generic kind of note the style guide forbids. I removed it, and the same kind of note on **slip**.
- **snap** was missing the common American phrase *in a snap* ("very quickly"). Added.

Useful minor catches: *pay slip* is British (Americans say *pay stub*), so it came out of the list of common word combinations; **snowflake** was defined using *snow*; one **slide** example described a car skidding out of control, which is not a smooth movement; the summary sentences (*core idea*) of **slide**, **slip**, and **snap** did not fit all their senses and were rewritten.

Rejected: reviewer B wanted *a snap decision* added as a noun sense. In that use **snap** works as an adjective, so I queued a separate **snap** adjective entry instead. Reviewer B also said *a snap of his fingers* does not fit the "sound" sense; the gesture is named for its sound, so the example stays.

After review, the basic-vocabulary check (definitions should use only the dictionary's list of about 2,400 basic words) caught *slope*, *microscope*, *photographic*, *everyday*, and *alcoholic* in definitions. ## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *review*: rereading entries that are already published, to fix and complete them. There were no draft entries and no reader reports, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. Under your rulings of 2026-10-24, the most important words (band 1) are read again one at a time to make sure every current meaning, common phrase, and grammar pattern is there. This cycle took the next four in the pass: **dance** (noun), **deal** (verb), **degree**, and **design** (noun). The pass now stands at 377 entries.

**What was added before the reviewers saw the entries.**
- **dance**: a piece of music for dancing (*the band played a slow dance*), and the informal British idiom *lead someone a merry dance*. The definition of sense 2 started with *the act of*, wording the style guide forbids; it now reads "a short time spent dancing, often with a partner".
- **deal**: working mainly with a kind of thing, such as facts or gossip (*That newspaper deals mostly in gossip*).
- **degree**: the level of seriousness of an injury or a crime (*second-degree burns*, *murder in the first degree*). The explanation points out that the scale runs in opposite directions: a first-degree burn is the least serious, but first-degree murder is the most serious.
- **design**: the combinations *a design fault* and *a design flaw*.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The same two-reviewer panel as in cycle 1 (reviewer A, a Claude model at no cost; reviewer B, a Google model paid per call) read all four entries. They raised 44 issues, 1 of them blocking. I applied 40 and rejected 4.

- The blocking issue was right. In **deal**, the example *The storm dealt serious damage to the coast* uses video-game language; ordinary English says a storm *causes* damage. It is now *The injury dealt the team a serious blow*, which also shows the two-object pattern the entry lists. The definition of that sense now says it is used mainly with **blow**.
- Reviewer B pointed out the new informal use *I can't deal* / *learn to deal* ("cope"). I added it as sense 5.
- The summary sentence (*core idea*) of **deal** spoke only of handing things out, which fits cards but not drugs or trade. It was rewritten. Two labels that did not fit were removed: *informal* on dealing drugs and *formal* on *deal a blow*.
- In **degree**, an example with an oven at "two hundred degrees" means something different in American and British kitchens, so it was replaced. The British phrases *first degree* (meaning a bachelor's degree) and *do a degree* were dropped. The angle sense moved below the university and amount senses, which learners meet more often. The usage note had repeated two learner-error notes; it now keeps only the degree sign (*30°C*).
- In **dance**, *party* was listed as a synonym of *a dance* (the event). A party is broader, so I removed that link and its mirror in the **party** entry. The usage note only repeated the grammar fields and was removed.
- **design**: the idiom *have designs on something* now also covers a person (*he has designs on his best friend's sister*).

What I rejected: a call to drop the plain-words pronunciation note on **dance** (the style guide asks for plain-words notes of this kind); single asterisks for a phrase named as a phrase (house style uses double); the claim that *first-degree* is only an adjective, since *murder in the first degree* shows the noun; and a grammar code ("often before another noun") that still fits.

**Checks.** All definitions use the basic vocabulary, and all gates pass. One new back-link was placed automatically: **cope** now points to **deal**.

**Spend.** US$0.18 this cycle; US$0.51 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *build* again, with room for 12 new entries. I took the next eleven nouns in queue order, all in frequency band 2: **specialist**, **species**, **spell** (magic), **spending**, **spider**, **spin**, **spite**, **split**, **spray**, **spy**, and **stack**. The twelfth slot went to a second **spell** entry. *Spell* meaning magic and *spell* meaning a period of time (*a dry spell*, *a dizzy spell*) are unrelated words that happen to share a spelling, and the dictionary's rule is that such words get separate entries, never senses of one entry. **Content highlights.**

There is also the informal British phrase *in a spin* ("very confused or worried").
- **split** covers a disagreement in a group, the end of a relationship, a sharing of money or work, and a crack, plus *do the splits*.
- **spray** covers the liquid in a can (*hair spray*), small drops in the air (*sea spray*), a mass of small things such as stones or sparks, and a small bunch of flowers.
- **stack** has the informal American idiom *blow your stack* ("suddenly become very angry").
- **species** has a learner error that is real: *this specie* for *this species*.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two-reviewer panel (reviewer A, a Claude model at no cost; reviewer B, a Google model paid per call) raised 43 issues, 9 of them blocking (must be settled before the entry is published). I applied 38 and rejected 5.

Minor catches I applied: *in the shops* is British (now *in stores*); the **spell** (period) definition said "short" although an example spoke of a long spell; *from spite* is dated (now *purely out of spite*); the *do the splits* phrase had an example that did not use the phrase; the news sense of **spin** moved above the informal ones.

What I rejected:

**Content highlights.**
- **stand** has six senses: a position you take on a question (*take a stand against the new road*), a piece of furniture that holds things (*a music stand*), a small place that sells things (*a lemonade stand*), the seats at a sports ground (*the stands*), the witness's place in an American court (*take the stand*), and a taxi stand. **What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model at no cost; reviewer B, a Google model paid per call) raised 34 issues, 10 of them blocking. I applied 28, rejected 3, and escalated 3 (passed to you, see below).

What I applied:
- In **stand**, I had used *make a stand* for stating an opinion. Both reviewers pointed out that *make a stand* means to resist or fight back, while the opinion sense uses *take a stand*. Example and collocation list now use *take a stand*.
- In **stair**, the example *He took the stairs two at a time* uses the plural meaning "a whole set of steps", which has its own entry (**stairs**). I replaced the example and added a pointer from **stair** to **stairs** for learners who land on the wrong entry.
- In **standing**, my explanation said *standings* always comes with *the*. That is false (*current standings*, *league standings*), so it was removed.
- Coverage: *taxi stand*, *a one-night stand*, *in good standing*, *sticker price*, and the messaging sticker were added at the reviewers' suggestion.

**Escalated.** The inflection tool (`tools/inflect.py`, which writes plural forms by rule) records **standing** as having no plural. Its rule is that a noun whose every sense is uncountable or plural-only gets no plural. But sense 2 is the plural-only word *standings*, so the entry seems to contradict itself. I did not change the tool in this run, because rules come in a later run with a logged reason. I marked the entry `inflection-disputed` and added a line to the curator list. My working assumption is that hand-marking *standings* in its examples, which the style guide asks for, is enough until someone decides.

**Rejected.** Both reviewers objected to the hand marks on *standings*, but the style guide asks for exactly those marks on a form that the inflection table does not list. I also kept the *literary* label on *stair* meaning a whole set of steps.

**Checks.** The basic-vocabulary check caught *sexual*, and that definition was reworded. Three back-links were placed by hand: **step**, **visit**, and **label**. All gates pass.
