**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks the kind of work each cycle does) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words waiting for one. It allowed up to 12 entries and US$3 of paid model calls. I took the next twelve words in queue order, all nouns in frequency band 2 (the second most common of five bands): **slide**, **slip**, **smoker**, **smoking**, **smoothie**, **snap**, **sneaker**, **snowball**, **snowflake**, **snowman**, **soda**, and **spam**. Each was written from my own knowledge and the style guide, without looking at any published dictionary.

A few points about the content:
- **slide** has six senses: a page in a talk shown on a screen (with the older photographic slide as a sub-sense), the playground slide, a fall in value (*stop the slide in sales*), a sliding movement, the glass slide used under a microscope, and the British hair clip.
- **slip** has four senses (a small mistake, a small piece of paper such as a *permission slip*, a fall, and the piece of clothing) and two idioms: *a slip of the tongue* and *give someone the slip*. The idiom *let something slip* stays on the list for the **let** entry, because idioms are filed under their first noun, else their first verb.
- **snap** covers the sound, the fastener on clothes, the informal British word for a photograph, and the informal American "something very easy" (*the test was a snap*), plus the phrases *a cold snap* and *in a snap*.
- **snowflake** includes the recent informal insult for a person thought to be too easily upset, labeled disapproving.
- **spam** has a learner error that learners really make: *I got a spam* instead of *a spam email*, because the word is uncountable.

**Checks before review.** Plurals come from a rules tool, never written by hand; it produced *snowmen* correctly and marked **smoking** and **spam** as having no plural. All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel (three models voting, plus the open CMU pronouncing dictionary for American English).

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** Each entry went to the two-reviewer panel: reviewer A (a Claude model, no charge) and reviewer B (a Google model, paid per call). They read every field and raise issues, some marked *blocking* (must be settled before the entry is published). They raised 28 issues, 3 blocking. I applied 26 and rejected 2.

The blocking ones were all right:
- The playground sense of **slide** said children "slide down" it, defining the word with itself. It now says they "go down sitting or lying".
- The note for translators on **slide** only listed the senses and said they are different words in other languages. That is the generic kind of note the style guide forbids. I removed it, and the same kind of note on **slip**.
- **snap** was missing the common American phrase *in a snap* ("very quickly"). Added.

Useful minor catches: *pay slip* is British (Americans say *pay stub*), so it came out of the list of common word combinations; **snowflake** was defined using *snow*; one **slide** example described a car skidding out of control, which is not a smooth movement; the summary sentences (*core idea*) of **slide**, **slip**, and **snap** did not fit all their senses and were rewritten.

Rejected: reviewer B wanted *a snap decision* added as a noun sense. In that use **snap** works as an adjective, so I queued a separate **snap** adjective entry instead. Reviewer B also said *a snap of his fingers* does not fit the "sound" sense; the gesture is named for its sound, so the example stays.

After review, the basic-vocabulary check (definitions should use only the dictionary's list of about 2,400 basic words) caught *slope*, *microscope*, *photographic*, *everyday*, and *alcoholic* in definitions. I reworded all five. Three related entries (**mistake**, **error**, **breeze**) got links back to the new ones; the tool could not tell which sense to link, so I placed those by hand.
