# 2026-10-18, cycle 3 of the 12:43 UTC run: two, zero, and ten interjections

## What this run did

This was a second **build** cycle in the same run (a build adds new entries from the queue, the list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build again because it still had the largest backlog of turns. The next twelve band-1 words in the queue (band 1 means "very common") were the last two plain numbers and ten interjections (short words that stand alone, such as greetings and reactions):

- `two-num` and `zero-num`;
- `ah`, `bye`, `congratulations`, `er`, `goodbye`, `hello`, `hey`, `hi`, `hmm`, and `no` (all `-interj`).

Each was drafted from scratch following the style guide, with `yes-interj` as the model for the interjections, then put through the full pipeline: validation, inflections, the pronunciation check, both reviewer models, my decision on every issue they raised, validation again, the defining-vocabulary check, and the cross-reference tool. All twelve are now `reviewed`.

## What the entries contain

*Zero* has three senses: the number, the point on a temperature scale (*ten degrees below zero*), and *not any at all* before a noun (*zero chance*, *zero interest*). Its notes explain that English speakers often say *oh* for 0 in phone numbers, and that sports use **nil** (British football), **love** (tennis), and **nothing**. *Two* notes that *two*, *too*, and *to* sound the same, and has the phrase *in two* (into two parts).

*No* mirrors *yes*: five senses (answering, refusing, agreeing with a negative, telling someone to stop, shock), the phrase *no way*, and the key trap for learners from many languages: after *Aren't you coming?*, English **no** means *I'm not coming*. *Hello* has four senses (greeting, on the phone, getting attention, and the informal *Hello?* that means "are you paying attention?"), and a short note that helps learners choose between *hello*, *hi*, and *hey*. *Bye* and *goodbye* have a similar note. *Congratulations* warns against *Congratulation!* and *congratulations for*, which learners often say. *Er*, *hmm*, and *ah* describe the sounds people make while thinking, doubting, or understanding.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 56 issues (44 marked blocking). I applied 19 and rejected 37. No factual question needed the owner.

Most of the rejections were one claim repeated many times: that in *say hello*, *say goodbye*, *say hi*, and *wave goodbye*, the word is a noun and so belongs in a separate noun entry. The dictionary already settled this in `yes-interj`, which holds *say yes* and *he said yes*: after *say*, the word is the interjection quoted. Both reviewers flagged this on every field that showed it, twelve times on *goodbye* alone. I rejected all of these and recorded the pattern in `wiki/notes/reviewer-noise.md`, and added a "fence" line to `NEXT.md` so later runs reject it quickly. Where a real noun use appeared (*we said our goodbyes*, *a warm hello*), I cut it to one pointer to a noun entry, as the house rule requires, and queued `goodbye-n` and `hello-n`.

Other rejections: reviewers wanted *zero chance* moved to a determiner entry, but a number before a noun is still the number (*three people*); the word family of *two* lists *twelve* and *twenty*, as other number entries do.

Applied, the main points:
- *Zero* in *zero tolerance* is not informal, so that sense lost its informal label. **Nought** is British for the figure 0 in general, not only in math.
- *Er* was called "the British spelling"; it is the usual British spelling, but American writers use it too, beside the more common **uh**.
- The notes on *hello*, *hi*, and *hey* claimed too much about who uses each word; they are now softer and more accurate.
- *Oh no* was moved out of the plain "answering" sense of *no*.
- A grammar slip in the translator note on *hi* was fixed (*only a greeting, never a goodbye*).
