Several already had entries under another part of speech (*around* and *before* as prepositions, *before* as a conjunction, *back* as a noun). Each new adverb says in its usage note where the other uses live. Some points worth knowing:

- *away* has eight senses, from *go away* and *two miles away* to *put your toys away*, *the music faded away*, and *singing away in the kitchen*. The sport sense (*playing away on Saturday*) is labeled British.
- *around* covers *around fifty people* (not exactly), the circle, turning, *walk around*, *Is Kim around?*, and *has been around for years*. A short note sets *around*, *about*, and *approximately* side by side.
- *before* (adverb) is set against *ago*: *ago* counts back from now, *before* from another time in the past. This matches the existing *ago* entry.
- *briefly* warns that *shortly* means *soon*, not *in a few words*.
- *back* has the idiom *back and forth*, and learner errors for *return back* and *give me back it*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from other companies read every field of every entry. They raised 33 issues, 27 of them blocking (the entry cannot be published until each is settled). I accepted 23 and rejected 10. None needed the owner. *back* and *carefully* came back clean. Almost everything came from the first reviewer; the second raised only two points, both right, on the same entry.

- *badly* had a muddled split between *seriously* (*badly hurt*) and *very much* (*badly need*). The reviewer pointed out that *badly affected*, *badly shaken*, and *badly needed* fit neither definition as written. Sense 2 is now about degree of harm or damage, sense 3 about strength of need or wish, and *a badly needed rest* is shown. The *feel badly* sense lost a claim that *feel bad* is the British form; it is common in both varieties.
- *around* had a learner error whose corrected sentence (*show you around the town*) used the preposition, not the adverb. Both reviewers caught it. It is now *Let me show you around*.
- *backward* had a sense that mixed *toward a worse state* with *toward the past*. It now holds only the first.
- *barely* said it takes *any*, not *no*. The reviewer objected that some dialects use *barely nothing*. I limited the rule to standard English but kept the learner error, since learners should write the standard form.
- Smaller fixes: *before* sense 1 lost a semicolon in its definition; *briefly* and *certainly* lost explanations that limited them to one position in the sentence; *basically*'s spoken sense now says it appears in informal writing too.

Rejected, with reasons logged: the British label on *away*'s sport sense (American English usually says *on the road*; a region label means *mainly used there*); the objection to listing *around* (preposition) in the word family of *around* (adverb), which is house practice; a claim that *basically* has four syllables (the transcription has three, and the pronunciation check agrees).

The cross-reference tool added four back-links, all word-family links (*before* preposition and conjunction, *care* noun and verb). It also put a *compare* link from *home* (adverb) back to *away*'s sport sense, but *home* has no sport sense, so I removed the link on both sides.
