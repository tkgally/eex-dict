# 2026-10-19, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 4: build

## What this run did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken in order from the queue, the list of words still to be written. The queue is working through the most common phrasal verbs (a phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word such as *up* or *out* that together have their own meaning, like *give up*). The scheduler allowed up to twelve entries; I claimed ten, to keep each entry full.

One claimed item, **come from**, turned out not to need an entry. Its meaning (*Where do you come from?*) is already sense 6 of the verb *come*, and the house rule is that a verb followed by a preposition whose use the verb's own senses already cover does not get a phrasal-verb entry. I marked its queue row as a duplicate.

The other nine are written, reviewed, and published:

- **catch up**: reach someone ahead, reach the same level, do work you missed (*catch up on sleep*), share news with a friend, cause problems later (*his lies will catch up with him*), and the passive *be caught up in something*.
- **check out**: leave a hotel, look at or try something (*check out the new restaurant*), find out whether something is true (with *his story checked out*), borrow from a library (American), and pay in a store or online.
- **clean up**: make a place clean, wash yourself, remove crime or pollution from a place, and the informal *win a lot of money*.
- **clear up**: explain or solve a misunderstanding, of weather, of an illness, and the British *make a room tidy*.
- **come back**, **come in**, **come on**, **come out** (nine senses, from *leave a place* to *a stain won't come out*), and **come up with** (*think of an idea*; *find the money*).

## Pronunciation

The pronunciation panel (three language models whose votes are compared with ours and, for American English, with an open pronouncing dictionary) confirmed only *come up with*. For the other eight, two panel members agreed with each other on a different stress, as happened with *break up* and *break down* earlier today. The house practice is to put the main stress on the small word (*come BACK*, *check OUT*), as in *give up* and *pick up*, so I kept our transcriptions, flagged the eight entries as "pronunciation disputed", and added one line to the curator file for the owner. This now looks like a pattern for nearly every two-word phrasal verb.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 50 issues; I applied 16 and rejected 34. Every decision is in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. One reviewer's reply on *catch up* came back empty; a single re-run was clean.

Applied, among others:
- *check out* sense 2 said only "look at"; it now covers visiting or trying something (*check out a restaurant*, *a website*).
- *come out* sense 7 was limited to being gay; it now covers telling people something private about who you love or about your sex, with an explanation that it is most often used of being gay.
- The core ideas (the one-sentence summary that opens an entry with three or more senses) of *catch up*, *clean up*, and *come on* were rewritten to cover all their senses.
- Library and store collocations were added to *check out*; an unneeded "informal" label came off *come in* sense 4.

Rejected:
- 19 issues from reviewer-b, and 4 from reviewer-a, asked for a pattern called "verb + adverb". The closed list of patterns has no such value; phrasal verbs use "verb + adverb or preposition" by house practice, so these are rejected every time.
- A demand to drop the British label on *come on* sense 5 (*How's your English coming on?*): a region label here means *mainly used there*, and American English usually says *coming along*.
- A claim that *come up with* can be split (*come an idea up with*); it cannot.

Six definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions are written from), such as *pollution*, *cloudy*, and *sexual*. All were reworded. One automatic back-link landed on the wrong sense of *enter* and was moved by hand to "go in".
