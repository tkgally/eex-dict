# 2026-10-21, cycle 1 of the 12:44 UTC run: twelve phrasal verbs, *run away* to *stand up*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build because it was the mode furthest behind its share of the work, and it allowed up to twelve entries. The queue is still working through the most common phrasal verbs (a verb plus a small word such as *up* or *off*, with a meaning of its own). I claimed twelve and wrote all twelve:

- *run away* (escape; leave home, with a subsense for leaving with a partner; avoid a problem)
- *run out* (have none left; be used up; come to the end of its time, as a passport does; leave someone who needs you)
- *set off* (start a trip; make an alarm ring or a bomb explode; cause a series of events; make something look better beside it)
- *set up* (start a business; get equipment ready; arrange a meeting; trick someone; give someone a start in life)
- *show up* (arrive; be easy to see; embarrass someone)
- *shut up* (stop talking; close a building; keep someone inside; and, added after review, a reply showing happy surprise)
- *sit down*, *slow down*, *speed up*, *split up*, *stand for*, *stand up*

Each entry has definitions, examples, grammar patterns, collocations (word partners), cross-references, at least one common learner mistake, and notes for translators. Before the reviewers saw them, every entry passed the format checker. The vocabulary checker had flagged four definition words outside the defining vocabulary (the limited word list that definitions must use): *reaction*, *embarrassed*, and *upright* twice. I replaced them.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from outside companies read every field of every entry. They raised 65 issues, 57 of them marked blocking. I made 65 decisions: 39 applied, 27 rejected, none escalated. One entry, *run out*, drew no issues at all. The main changes:

- *stand up* had a second sense, "be on your feet, or stay straight up". Reviewer-a showed, over eight related issues, that this is just the meaning of the plain verb *stand*, and that its examples (*he could hardly stand up*) really meant rising to your feet. I removed the sense and moved one example to sense 1.
- *shut up*: reviewer-b pointed out that the usage note described a meaning, a happy *Shut up! Really?* on hearing news, that had no sense of its own. I added it as sense 4, labelled *very informal*.
- *slow down* sense 2 was signposted *relax* and gave *relax* as a synonym. Reviewer-a was right that it means doing less, not relaxing; the signpost is now *do less* and the link is gone. Reviewer-b also noted that sense 1 defined *slow down* with *slower*, which comes from the headword itself; it now says "at a lower speed".
- *set up* sense 5 (give someone a start in life) was too broad and mixed in *be set up for life*, a different, money-only meaning. Both are fixed.
- *speed up*: the usage note now says *sped up* is the usual past form, and *speeded up* is used mainly when someone makes something faster.
- Summary sentences (core ideas) were reworded on *set off*, *set up*, *shut up*, *sit down*, and *stand up* so that every sense fits.

I rejected, among others: reviewer-b's repeated grammar patterns ("verb + adverb", four-part patterns) and collocation types that are not in our closed lists, which a settled house rule covers; reviewer-a's view that a summary sentence must not start "To ... is to", which is house style; its claim that *show up to a party* is wrong (it is common); and its claim that *not stand for* is the opposite of *put up with* (*stand for* itself means *tolerate*, and the negative applies to both).

## Pronunciation

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were verified by the three-model pronunciation panel, 3 of 3 for eleven words and 2 of 3 for *stand for*. The previous build (*move out* to *put up*) had every phrasal verb disputed because one panel model marks the first word with a second main-stress sign. That did not happen this time. The note `wiki/notes/phrasal-verb-stress-recheck.md` explains the earlier result. This cycle shows the dispute comes and goes from run to run, which supports the notation fix the note proposes.

## Back-links

The cross-reference tool adds a link back whenever one entry names another, but it guesses which sense of the other entry to use. Two of its guesses were wrong, and I moved them by hand: on *avoid* to sense 3 (*not do*) and on *rise* to sense 5 (*stand up*). The other fourteen were on the right sense. The tool also queued the words these entries name that do not exist yet, among them *run away with*, *set out*, *show off*, *turn up*, *setup*, and *stand up for*.
