# Report for 2026-11-20

## Cycle 1: review

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a review cycle: re-reading entries that are already published rather than writing new ones. There were no draft entries waiting and no reader reports open on GitHub, so the cycle continued the "coverage pass", the owner's instruction of 2026-10-24 that every common (band 1) word be read again to check that no current meaning is missing. Four entries were read: *double* (adjective), *dream* (verb), *dress* (verb), and *driver* (noun). With these, 385 band-1 words have had this second reading.

**What was added.** Before the reviewers saw the entries, I added what a learner would look for and not find:

- *double*: a use for letters said or written twice (*Is it spelled with a double s?*), and a sub-meaning for things with two different sides, often in an unfair or dishonest way (*a double life*, *a double standard*, *a double agent*).
- *dream*: the informal *You're dreaming if you think he'll pay you back* (believing something will happen when it almost certainly won't), and the common pairing *dream big*.
- *dress*: *dress for dinner* (wear formal clothes for an event), a new meaning "decorate" (*dress a store window*, *streets dressed with flags*), the idiom *dressed to kill*, and a pointer to the phrasal verb *dress down*, which has been added to the list of words to write.
- *driver*: the common pairings *a drunk driver*, *a student driver*, and *a delivery driver*; the idiom *in the driver's seat* is now marked as mainly American, with the British *in the driving seat* noted.

I also removed two notes for translators (on *dream* and *dress*) that only repeated what the learner-error notes already said; the house rule is that each fact lives in one place.

**What the reviewers found.** Every changed entry goes to two independent reviewing models, which check each field and mark problems as blocking or minor. They raised 29 issues, 2 of them blocking, and I accepted all 29 after checking each one myself:

- The first meaning of *driver* was defined as "a person who **drives** ...", which breaks the rule against defining a word with its own base word. It now reads "a person who controls a car, a bus, a train, or another vehicle and makes it go".
- An example for *in the driver's seat*, "With two goals ahead, ...", was awkward English; it now reads "Two goals ahead, the team is now in the driver's seat."
- Saying numbers as *four double one* is mainly British, so that example was replaced and the explanation now says so.
- *Clothe* was listed as a synonym of the wrong meaning of *dress* (wearing a style of clothes); it now sits with the meaning "put clothes on someone".
- Several notes on *dress* claimed it always takes a person as its object, which is untrue for the new wound, salad, and window meanings; they now speak only of the clothes meanings.
- Smaller fixes: a five-word sense label shortened to "style of clothes", a hotel note that said a double room always has one large bed, a doubled "clean" in the wound definition, and *store window* for the British *shop window*.

One reviewer fix could not be taken exactly as proposed: putting "golf" into the golf-club definition of *driver* fails the vocabulary check (the dictionary has no entry for *golf* yet, so it may not appear in a definition). The definition now names the club, and the short explanation says it is used in golf, which is the house practice for limiting a meaning to one field.

**Cross-references.** *Driver* and *passenger* pointed at each other twice (once as "compare", once as "see also"); the duplicate pair was removed. *Decorate* now points back to the new "decorate" meaning of *dress*; the linking script could not tell which meaning of *decorate* matched, so I placed it by hand.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The scheduler-picked block size was 20 entries, but as in recent review cycles I read four entries in depth rather than twenty in passing.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *earth* (noun), *easy*, *edge*, and *education*. The next full maintenance check ("lint") is due in about seven cycles; the next weekly digest is due 2026-11-21.

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build cycle: writing new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). It allowed up to twelve; I wrote seven, all common (band 2) nouns, to keep each one full: *stock*, *storage*, *strawberry*, *stretch*, *strip*, *stripe*, and *struggle*. The dictionary now has 4,600 entries, all reviewed.

**What the entries contain.** *Stock* covers goods in a shop (*in stock*, *out of stock*), a supply kept for later, shares in a company, and the liquid used for soup, with the idioms *take stock* and *a laughing stock*. *Stretch* covers a length of road or water, a period of time, a body movement, the give in cloth, and the informal *a bit of a stretch* (hard to believe), with *at a stretch*, *by no stretch of the imagination*, and *the home stretch*. *Strip* covers a narrow piece (with landing strips as a sub-meaning), the American shopping street, comic strips, and the British word for a sports team's clothes. *Stripe* covers lines of color, the stripes on a uniform (*earn your stripes*), and the American *of every stripe* (of every kind). *Struggle* separates a long effort, something hard to do (*it was a struggle to get up*), a physical fight, and a power struggle. Both pronunciations of every word passed the automatic check (two or more pronunciation models agree, and the American form matches an open pronouncing dictionary).

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewing models raised 34 issues, 2 of them blocking. I accepted 29 and rejected 5.

- Blocking, accepted: an example said *A zebra has black and white stripes*, a checkable fact about the real world, which house rules keep out of examples. It now describes a pet cat.
- Blocking, rejected: one reviewer said *struggle* in the "long effort" meaning is only countable. Uncountable use is common (*after years of struggle*), so the grammar stays, and I replaced an example so the entry now shows it.
- Coverage: the reviewers named common uses I had missed, and I added them: *a laughing stock*, storage as space or cupboards (*the apartment has lots of storage*), *a bit of a stretch*, and *of every stripe*. These additions were written after the panel and were not sent back to it; they follow the same rules and were checked by the validator and the vocabulary check.
- Smaller fixes: comic strips are a row of boxes and need not be funny; three "core idea" sentences (the one-line summary over an entry with three or more meanings) did not cover every meaning; a translator's note on *stock* only repeated the learner-error note and was removed; *do a stretch* (which can also mean a prison term) became *do stretches*.
- Rejected: twice a reviewer said *often before another noun* is not a grammar code, but it is in the dictionary's closed list of noun codes; one reviewer asked for single marks on named phrases, against the style guide; and one asked to put the shares meaning of *stock* first, but a learner meets the shop meaning first.

**Vocabulary and links.** The vocabulary check (definitions must use the core defining words, or words with entries) caught six words (*loosen*, *rank*, *British*, *English*) in definitions and summaries; I rewrote those lines. The linking script placed two back-links itself and left three for a person because it could not tell which meaning matched; I placed them in *supply*, *conflict*, and *strip*. *Stock* (verb) was added to the queue.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. One pre-existing warning on *conflict* (two notes on its pronunciation say nearly the same thing) was noticed and left for that entry's next review.
