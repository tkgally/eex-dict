## What this cycle did

A **build cycle** writes new dictionary entries from the queue (the list of words still to be written, ordered by how common they are). The scheduler, a small program that picks each cycle's job from what has been neglected longest, chose build. I drafted twelve very common nouns (band 1, the most common of five frequency bands), in queue order: *track, trade, traffic, train, training, transport, trap, travel, treatment, tree, trial,* and *trick*. Each went through the full checking pipeline and is now marked `reviewed`, which means it is published on the site at the next build.

Before starting I checked for unfinished work from earlier runs. There were no open pull requests. Two old working branches remain on GitHub; both are fully merged and contain nothing new. One was already listed for the owner to delete; I added a line for the other in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` (the list of questions and tasks only the owner can settle), because this tool set cannot delete branches.

## The entries

Most of these words have several clearly different meanings, and the entries try to show all the current ones a learner will meet:

- *track*: a rough path, a racing ground (with the American *run track* as a sub-sense), the rails a train runs on, marks left on the ground, and one song on a recording; eight phrases such as *keep track of*, *lose track of*, *on track*, and *cover your tracks*.
- *trade*: buying and selling, a kind of business (*the tourist trade*), a skilled job (*a carpenter by trade*), and one exchange.
- *traffic*: vehicles on roads, planes and people, visitors to a website, and illegal trade.
- *train*: the railway vehicle, a series of events, the back of a long dress, and a line of animals moving together; the phrase *train of thought*.
- *training*, *transport*, *trap*, *travel*, *treatment*, *tree*, *trial*, *trick*: each with its senses, collocations, and the common learner errors, for example *How was your travel?* (say *trip*), *the bird is on the tree* (say *in*), *He is in trial* (say *on trial*), and *make a trick to someone* (say *play a trick on*).
- *travel* carries a short guide to choosing between *travel*, *trip*, *journey*, and *tour*.

All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and by an open pronouncing dictionary consulted at run time. The plural forms come from the spelling rules.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from different companies read every field of every entry. They raised 47 issues, 40 of them marked blocking. I read each one: I agreed with 33 and fixed the entry myself, and rejected 15 with a logged reason. None needed the owner.

The most useful corrections:

- *trial*: the court sense now covers any case, not only crimes; the testing sense no longer insists on "a period of time"; the sports sense now includes selection for an event as well as a team; and the "difficulty" sense now covers an experience that tests your patience, not only a person.
- *trade* and *training*: two illustrations used the word as a verb (*Training a puppy takes...*), which belongs in the verb entry; they were replaced with noun uses.
- *transport*: the usage note had said both varieties use *transport* for carrying goods; American English usually says *transportation* there too, and the note now says so.
- *trap*: a note about *tourist trap* moved from a sense to the usage note, and a weak learner error was removed.

The main rejections: both reviewers said that *travel to a place* and *trade with someone* are verb patterns, but they are also ordinary noun uses (*Travel to the city is slow*), so they stay as noun collocations. Reviewer-b objected that *travel* says "no plural" while one sense is the plural *travels*; that is a known limit of the tool that writes word forms, recorded in the wiki, and the examples mark **travels** by hand as the house rules require.

After review, the vocabulary checker found three words in definitions outside the defining vocabulary (the list of about 2,400 plain words definitions should use): *robe*, *refusal*, and *Halloween*. I rewrote those three definitions in plain words.

When one entry names another as a synonym or a word to compare, a tool adds the matching link in the other entry. It added four: *profession*, *exchange*, *instruction*, and *experiment* now point back to the new entries. I checked each by hand, and all four landed on the right sense.
