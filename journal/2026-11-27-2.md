# Run report, 2026-11-27 (second run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The tool that picks each cycle's job (the selector) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still waiting for an entry. It allowed up to twelve, and I wrote twelve, all now published as reviewed:

- **diversity** (noun): a range of different kinds of things (*a diversity of opinions*), and people of different backgrounds in one group (*cultural diversity*, *diversity training*).
- **elephant**: one sense, plus two idioms, *the elephant in the room* and *a white elephant*.
- **resistance**: six senses, from refusing a change and fighting back, through the secret army of an occupied country (*join the resistance*), to resistance to illness or heat, the force that slows things moving through air or water, and the electrical sense.
- **reunion**: a meeting of people who have not met for a long time (*a family reunion*), and the joining again of things that were separated (*a reunion of the band*). A note for translators explains what a class reunion is.
- **trailer**: the vehicle pulled behind a car, the American home on wheels (British *caravan*), and the short film that advertises a movie.
- **well-being**, **wrist** (with a note that the **w** is silent), and **yogurt** (with the British spelling *yoghurt* and the two ways of saying the first part).
- **zip**: the British word for a zipper, the informal American word for zero (*I know zip about cars*), energy, and a packed computer file.
- **zone**: an area with a special use (*a war zone*, *a school zone*), an official area of a city (*travel across three zones*), and the idiom *in the zone*.
- **abandoned** (adjective): of places, of people and animals, and of plans given up before they were finished.
- **about** (adjective): the *about* of *be about to* (*the movie is about to start*), and *not about to*, meaning determined not to. Two common learner mistakes are shown: using it with a later time (*about to leave tomorrow*) and with the *-ing* form.

**Checks.** The tool that writes word forms produced every plural from the rules. The pronunciation panel (three models plus an open pronouncing dictionary, consulted only to check) agreed with all American and British transcriptions but one: for the British *reunion* I had written four syllables; the panel preferred three, which is also the more common British form, so I changed it and it then passed.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 40 issues, 4 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). All four were on two entries:

- On *abandoned*, one reviewer pointed out that the example *he felt abandoned* (after friends moved away) did not fit a definition about people who should look after you, and that the common feeling use had no definition. I widened the second sense to cover feeling left alone by someone who should support you, and changed the example. The same reviewer noted that *an abandoned project* had no sense; I added a third sense for plans and attempts, and a one-sentence summary of the entry (the core idea), which three-sense entries need.
- On *about*, the reviewer saw that the second sense's short heading (*not willing*) contradicted its definition (*willing or intending*); the definition is right, because the *not* lives in the sentence, so the heading became *with not*.

I applied 35 of the 40 issues in all. The largest group: both reviewers said the explanation field, which the style guide keeps for function words and hard-to-use words, should not hold grammar or regional notes on ordinary nouns (*resistance*, *trailer*, *yogurt*, *zip*); I removed those four explanations, since the same facts were already in the labels and collocations. Others: a health claim in a *resistance* example was replaced, *water resistance* moved to the sense about not being harmed, an example of *zip* about a sauce (which is about flavor, not energy) was replaced, and *empty* was dropped as a synonym of *abandoned*.

I rejected 5. Both reviewers wanted the idiom *the path of least resistance*; our rule files an idiom under its first noun, *path*, so it is noted for that entry instead. One asked to put the film sense of *trailer* first; I kept the vehicle sense first because the others grow from it. One called the computing sense of *zip* not informal; *a zip* on its own is informal, while *zip file* is the neutral form. One said a learner error on *about* repeated the explanation; the error shows the real mistake, the explanation states the rule.

**Cross-references.** Two existing entries gained automatic return links (*nothing*, *well*). I placed three by hand: *opposition* now lists *resistance* as a synonym, and *area* and *district* list *zone*. I dropped *energy* as a synonym of *zip* rather than force a poor return link.

**What did not work.** My drafting script failed once partway because a new folder for the *zone* entry did not exist yet; I fixed the script and wrote the remaining entries. No entry was affected.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: a second reading of entries already published. With no drafts and no reader reports waiting, the work was the coverage pass, which rereads the most important everyday words (band 1, the most common of five frequency groups) to make sure each entry has every sense and phrase a learner needs. This cycle took the next four: **king**, **land**, **last** (adjective), and **lay** (verb).

Before the reviewers saw them, I added what I judged was missing:

- **king** gained the idiom *fit for a king*, and lost a translators' note that only repeated its usage note.
- **land** gained two subsenses (narrower uses within a sense): *lands* in the plural for large areas owned by one family (*the family's lands*), and an imaginary land (*a land of giants and talking animals*).
- **lay** gained four senses: putting blame or importance on someone (*lay the blame on her brother*), preparing a plan or trap (*lay a trap*), betting (*I'll lay you ten dollars*), and the slang sense of having sex, labeled slang and vulgar (*get laid*); and two idioms, *lay something bare* and *lay it on thick*. It now points to the phrasal verb *lay down*, which is still to be written.
- **last** already covered every common use, so I added nothing.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 64 issues, 6 of them blocking (must be settled before the entry stays published). I applied 47 and rejected 17.

The blocking ones: on **land**, the sense meaning a country (*distant lands*) carried no register label, although two notes said it was literary; I added the *literary* label and removed the notes that said it, so the fact lives in one place. One reviewer also found my new example *living in a land of dreams* unnatural, and it was right: English says *living in a dream world*. I replaced it. On **lay**, both reviewers said the slang sense should not have an explanation field, which is kept for function words; the fixed form *get laid* now appears in its collocations and its grammar says *usually passive*.

Other changes I accepted: a plainer chess definition for **king** that no longer uses the word *king* for the playing card; repeated notes trimmed on all four entries (both reviewers found the same facts in two or three fields); *plot* and *past* removed as synonyms of **land** and **last**, since neither swaps in cleanly; a calendar fact (*December is the last month*) replaced, because examples must not state checkable facts; the British *the last but one* replaced with *second to last*; and sharper definitions for three senses of **lay**.

I rejected eight requests to mark *laid* by hand in examples, since the website marks every listed form of the headword itself. I also rejected five idioms the reviewers wanted added to **lay** or **land** (*lay claim to*, *lay waste to*, *lay eyes on*, *lay a finger on*, *the lay of the land*): our rule files an idiom under its first noun, so they belong to *claim*, *waste*, *eye*, *finger*, and the noun *lay*. The first two are now noted for those entries. One reviewer called *humorous* a wrong place for a label; it is one of our register labels, so I kept it.

**Cross-references.** Removing a synonym also means removing the matching link on the other entry, so *plot* and *past* lost theirs. I linked *bet* to *lay* by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.20 for this cycle and US$0.94 for the day, against a cap of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *learn*, *let*, *lie*, and *light*.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again, allowing up to twelve new entries. I took six, the next six words in the queue, so that each could be as full as the earlier entries with the room this run had left. All six are adjectives, and all are now published as reviewed:

- **absent**: not where you are expected to be (*absent from school*), missing from something (a formal use: *Fear was completely absent from her voice*), and showing that your mind is elsewhere (*an absent smile*). A learner error shows *absent in school* corrected to *absent from school*.
- **absolute**: complete (*an absolute disaster*), with a subsense for the greatest or smallest possible (*the absolute minimum*, *the absolute best*); without limits (*absolute power*); true in every situation (*absolute truth*); and measured on its own (*in absolute terms*).
- **academic**: of schools and universities (*the academic year*), based on study rather than practical skills, good at studying (*he was never academic*), and not important in practice (*the question is academic now*). A note for translators warns that this last use does not mean *clever* or *scientific*.
- **acceptable**: good enough but not very good, and allowed by most people (*socially acceptable*), with a subsense for something people can agree to (*a price acceptable to both sides*).
- **accurate**: correct in detail (*accurate information*) and hitting a target (*an accurate throw*). A short box explains how to choose between *correct*, *accurate*, and *exact*, and a translators' note warns that similar-looking words in some languages mean *careful* or *tidy*.
- **advanced**: modern (*advanced technology*), at a high level (*an advanced course*), and far along (*at an advanced stage*), with a formal subsense meaning old (*at an advanced age*, *advanced in years*).

**Checks.** The word-form tool and the pronunciation panel agreed with all six entries the first time. Before review, the vocabulary check found one word outside our defining vocabulary (*technical*) in a definition, which I replaced. I also replaced an example about missiles with one about a throw, to keep the examples everyday.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 20 issues, 4 blocking. All four were the same point, made by both reviewers in two places: I had marked the sense of *academic* meaning *not important in practice* as used only after a verb, but one of its own examples used it before a noun (*a purely academic point*). They were right; I removed that grammar code.

Of the other issues I applied 14. The largest changes: *absolute* gained the subsense for *the absolute minimum*, which one reviewer found missing; *advanced* gained the old-age subsense, because one reviewer saw that the example *at an advanced age* did not fit the sense it sat in; and the definition of the target sense of *accurate* now covers a throw or a shot as well as a person. Smaller fixes: a summary sentence that missed a sense, an unnatural example (*Any sign of fear was absent*), the padding phrase *used to make what you say stronger* removed from a definition, and *perfectly acceptable* moved to the sense it belongs to.

I rejected 2. One reviewer read *especially of power* in a definition of *absolute* as a restriction that belongs elsewhere; it is part of the meaning, not a restriction. The other called a word-form note a duplicate, but that note is written by the word-form tool from the grammar codes and is not ours to remove.

**Cross-references.** I placed two return links by hand: *practical* now lists *academic* as an opposite, and *modern* lists *advanced* as a synonym. Twelve new words that the entries mention went into the queue.

**Spend.** US$0.20 for this cycle and US$1.14 for the day, against a cap of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The next words in the queue, or the coverage pass with *learn*, *let*, *lie*, and *light*.
