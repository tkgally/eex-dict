## Cycle 1: review

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose *review*: rereading entries that are already published, to fix and complete them. It chose review because that mode had gone longest without a turn. There were no draft entries and no reader reports ("Entry report" issues on GitHub), so the cycle continued the coverage pass. Under your rulings of 2026-10-24, the most important words (band 1, the most common) are read again one at a time to make sure every current meaning, common phrase, and grammar pattern is there. This cycle took the next four: **development**, **die** (verb), **direct** (adjective), and **dish**. The pass now stands at 381 entries.

Before starting, I checked for stranded work. There were no open pull requests and no reader reports. The three old `claude/` branches hold nothing that is not already on `main`, and each is already listed for pruning in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`.

**What was added before the reviewers saw the entries.**
- **development**: making a story, idea, or character fuller (*there is very little character development*), and the start and growth of an illness or problem (*the development of a crack in the wall*). Its usage note only repeated the grammar fields, so I removed it.
- **die**: a new informal sense for exaggerated feeling (*I nearly died when I saw the price*, *we almost died laughing*, *dying of boredom*), and *die on someone* for a machine that stops working (*the car died on me*). The adaptation note for translators now names a real trap: *the plant is dying* means it is still alive.
- **direct**: the family-line use (*a direct descendant*, *in a direct line*), and *a direct hit* and *direct speech* under the "exact" sense.
- **dish**: the amount a dish holds (*a dish of strawberries*), *the dish of the day*, and an old-fashioned informal sense, an attractive person (*her new boyfriend is quite a dish*). Two "see also" links repeated the *compare* links to **plate** and **bowl**, so I removed them.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers were reviewer A (a Claude model, at no cost) and reviewer B (a Google model, paid per call). They read all four entries and raised 40 issues, none of them blocking. I applied 34 and rejected 6.

- **direct** drew the most (15), and most were right. Three definitions were narrower than their own examples. A *direct train* still stops at stations; what makes it direct is that you do not change trains. *Direct sunlight* and *a direct result* are not "between two people or things". *A direct hit* is not "not changed". All three definitions were widened. The signpost (the short label that helps a reader pick a sense) "honest" now reads "plain speaking", because an honest person need not be blunt. For the same reason, **honest** is no longer listed as a synonym, and I removed the matching link in the **honest** entry as well. The note for translators no longer uses the phrase "direct speech" in a second meaning.
- **die**: both reviewers found that the idiom *die hard* (*old habits die hard*) was missing, and it is now a phrase. Sense 2 said "slowly", but hope can die suddenly. The usage note repeated a learner-error note and was removed.
- **development**: two new examples made health claims that could be checked (*Exercise may slow the development of heart problems*). House style bans checkable facts in examples, so both were replaced. A learner error that no learner really makes (*a new develop*) was removed. *The council* is mainly British, so it became *the city*.
- **dish**: the new subsense used the headword in its own definition; *cooked* became *prepared*, since salads are dishes too; two collocations moved to the right type.

What I rejected: four requests to put single asterisks on phrases that are named as phrases. House style uses double asterisks for a phrase named as a word, as in "**be dying**". I also rejected *plural only* for the washing-up sense of **dish** (*one dirty dish left* is normal), and a request to split a sense of **direct**; widening the definition fixed the real problem.

**Checks.** Every definition stays within the basic defining vocabulary. One of my new definitions used *thirst*, which is outside that list, so I changed it to *hunger*. All gates pass.

**Spend.** US$0.19 this cycle; US$1.28 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. Reviewer A raised 33 of the 40 issues and was right on almost all of them. Reviewer B's lower precision this cycle (64 percent) comes from its usual asterisk objections.

**Mechanical checks.** The size caps on the rules files and the wiki are all respected, and every relative link in the repository resolves. No new type fell below the line. Reviewer B's formatting objections stand at 38 percent and stay switched on by your ruling.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *build*, which means writing new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The cap is twelve entries a cycle. I took eight, since fewer, fuller entries are better than more thin ones. All eight were added to the queue because existing entries already point to them:

- **grant** (verb), from the word family of **grant** (noun);
- **spy** (verb), from the word family of **spy** (noun);
- **flake**, named by **snowflake**;
- **microscope**, used in a definition of **slide**;
- **snapshot**, named by **snap**;
- **soft drink**, named by **soda**;
- **stadium**, named by **park**.

**What the entries contain.** Each one has American and British pronunciations, word forms, definitions in the basic defining vocabulary, two to four examples per sense, collocations (the words that typically go with it), and cross-references. Notes for translators are written only where there is a real trap. Some highlights:
- **grant** has the formal "give officially" sense (*The bank granted her a loan*) and the "admit" sense (*It's a clever idea, I grant you, but...*). It points to *take something for granted*, which lives under **take**.
- **spy** has three senses: working as a spy, watching someone secretly (*spy on*), and the literary "catch sight of" (*I spied an empty seat*).
- **flake** includes the informal American sense of an unreliable person (*I'm such a flake!*).
- **snapshot** includes the "picture of a situation at one moment" sense and the computer-backup sense.
- **microscope** has the idiom *under the microscope*.
- **soft drink** has a translator's note: here *soft* means "without alcohol", so a word-for-word translation goes wrong.

**Pronunciation.** Each transcription is checked by a panel of three models, plus a public pronouncing dictionary for American English. Fifteen of the sixteen were confirmed. The American form of **soft drink** (stress on *soft*) got only one panel vote in three checks, and the public dictionary does not list the phrase. Following the rules, I flagged it and added a line to the curator file for you, with my working assumption. That assumption is that American compound nouns usually stress the first word.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 19 issues, 3 of them blocking. I applied all 19.

- All three blocking issues were in **spy**, and reviewer A was right. My summary sentence and my note for translators both said that *spy* without *on* means "notice". But sense 1 uses *spy for* (*she spied for the other side*), so both statements contradicted the entry. Both are rewritten.
- **hamburger** meat is ground by a machine, not just "cut into very small pieces"; both definitions now say so.
- **flake** was defined as a piece that "has broken off something", which does not fit a flake of snow. The definition is now wider.
- *watch* is not a true synonym of *spy on*, so the link was removed. Two collocations I had simply lifted from my own examples were replaced: *spy an empty seat* became *spy a familiar face*, and *secretly spy on* was dropped.
- Reviewer A noticed that sentence-opening *Granted, ...* was missing. It is a different part of speech (adverb), so I queued **granted** (adverb) as a new word rather than adding it to the verb.

**Cross-references.** Eight back-links could not be placed automatically, because the tool could not tell which sense of the target they belong to. I placed them by hand: **give**, **allow**, **award**, **admit**, **refuse**, and **deny** now point to **grant**; **see** points to **spy**; **juice** points to **soft drink**. Four more were placed automatically.
