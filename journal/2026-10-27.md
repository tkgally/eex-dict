# Run report, 2026-10-27 (scheduled run 20261027T004409Z-n9o1cm)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose **build**: writing new entries from the queue of words still to be written. It gave a ceiling of twelve entries and a paid-call budget of US$3. The words came in the order the owner set for milestone 1 (the roughly 2,400 words the dictionary uses to write its own definitions, which must all have entries before other words). The twelve were: *thankful*, *yearly*, *scarcely*, *seldom*, *wrongly*, the phrasal verb *take apart*, and six prefixes: *anti-*, *de-*, *mis-*, *non-*, *post-*, *pre-*. Milestone 1 now has 18 words left.

Before the build, the pre-flight checks found no stranded pull requests, no open entry reports from readers, and nothing in the owner's inbox. Three old work branches remain on GitHub with nothing unmerged on them; each is already listed in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` for deletion, since the session's tools cannot delete branches.

Each entry was drafted from scratch and then went through the full pipeline: the schema check; the inflection tool (which writes forms like *took apart*); the pronunciation check, where three outside models vote on each IPA transcription and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary is consulted where it has the word; and the two-reviewer panel. Reviewer A is a Claude model and reviewer B is a Google model. They read every field and return a closed verdict on each.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 106 issues, only one of them blocking. In the *grateful*/*thankful* comparison box for *thankful*, the example (*I'm thankful the train was late too*) made no sense on its own. I replaced it with one that does. Of the issues, 79 were applied and 14 rejected, each with a logged reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. None needed escalation. The main changes:

- *wrongly*: both reviewers saw that sense 2 mixed two ideas, *unfairly* (*wrongly accused*) and *mistakenly* (*wrongly believe*). The mistaken uses moved into sense 1, and sense 2 is now just the unfair one. The word is now marked as having no comparative form. A learner-error item that only repeated the usage note was removed.
- *anti-*: reviewer A pointed out that a real use was missing, *anti-* meaning the opposite (*anticlimax*, *antihero*, British *anticlockwise*). It is now sense 3. *anti-bullying* moved to the "prevents" sense, where it belongs.
- *pre-*: my note on hyphens was wrong for American English, which usually writes *preexisting* as one word. The note now gives the American and British habits separately. The stress note now admits that some nouns stress the prefix (*PREE-school*), and the British *pre-match* example became *pregame*.
- *scarcely*: the core idea and the third sense were reworded. The third sense is an understatement (*it is scarcely surprising*), not a flat "certainly not".
- Six entries had the same fact stated in two or three places, which the house rule "one fact, one field" forbids. The extra copies were removed.

Rejections included a reviewer calling *comparative with more* "not a grammar code" (it is one; this is a settled house rule), a request to mark *took apart* in an example (the site marks listed inflected forms itself), and two proposals to merge senses of *non-* and *pre-* that differ in grammar and in how they are usually translated.

The pronunciation panel agreed on every transcription except American *anti-*. I gave *AN-tee* first and *AN-tie* as a variant, and the panel leaned to the other form. Both are standard, so the entry carries a `pronunciation-disputed` flag and a line for the owner in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`. This is the same situation as *semi-* yesterday.

After review, the cross-reference tool listed nine places where a new entry points to an older one but the tool could not tell which sense of the older entry should point back. I placed all nine back-links by hand (for example, *beat* sense 2, "defeat", now lists *take apart* as a synonym). The compare links from *mis-* to *un-* and *dis-* were dropped rather than mirrored, because *mis-* ("badly, wrongly") is not a negative prefix.

**Spend.** US$0.36 of the day's US$15 cap, all of it on reviewer B and the pronunciation panel. Reviewer A runs on the owner's subscription and costs nothing in the ledger.

**What did not work.** The definition-vocabulary check caught seven definition words outside the defining list (*reverse*, *undo*, *advance*, *mild*, *criticize*, *glad*, *deserved*). Each was rewritten in plainer words before the entries were marked reviewed.

**What is next.** The selector now points to a review cycle. Milestone 1 has 18 words left. New queue rows from this build include *yearly* (adverb), *wrong* (adverb), *rightly*, *incorrectly*, *pro-*, and *in-*.

**For the owner.** One new pronunciation line (*anti-*, American). Nothing else new.

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose **review**: a second reading of existing entries. This one continued the coverage pass the owner ordered on 2026-10-24, which reads the most important everyday words, those with the most senses first, and adds whatever a learner would expect to find but cannot. The four entries were *drive* (noun), *full* (adjective), *front* (noun), and *place* (noun), each with eight senses. I read each against my own knowledge of current English. I made the additions below and then sent each entry to both reviewers.

What I added before review:
- *full*: two uses of the "complete" sense: *a full member of the club* (having every right, not only some) and *a full hour* (not less than the amount, often stressing that it seems long).
- *place*: a mathematics sense (*correct to two decimal places*) and the common phrase *in places* (*the path is steep in places*).
- *front*: a political sense (parties that join to form a front).
- *drive*: the collocation *test drive*, and a corrected learner-error note. It had said the hour must be singular, but *two hours' drive*, with an apostrophe, is also correct.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 63 issues, five of them blocking, all on *full*. Of these, 58 were applied and 5 rejected. Every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The blocking issues on *full* were all real. Sense 2 (*full of*) was coded "predicative only", meaning it is used only after a verb, but *a box full of books* shows it right after a noun. Sense 4 was coded "attributive only", meaning it is used only before a noun, while its moon subsense had *the moon is full tonight*. And an example said *Since he got the promotion, he's very full of himself*, where *since* needs *he's been*. All three are fixed. The reviewers also caught several real weaknesses I had missed:
- The clothing use (*a full skirt*) was filed under "rounded and quite large", which is a different meaning. It is now its own sense.
- The phrase was headed with the British *to the full* although the dictionary is American-first. It is now *to the fullest*, with the British form named.
- They noted two missing informal phrases, *full of it* and *full up*. Both were added.
- An invented learner error (*I'm full of my homework*) was removed.

On *drive*, reviewer A pointed out that the British "road to a house" sense came second, ahead of the far more common computer drive. The senses were reordered. The usage note had repeated facts found elsewhere in the entry; it now says only that **drive** appears in street names. I rejected a request for an American-football "drive" sense as too narrow for this dictionary.

On *front*, I had added *up front* meaning "honest" to the phrase's definition, but the phrase's explanation already said the same thing with the one-word spelling *upfront*. The reviewers caught the repetition, and I reverted my addition. The point that English says **opposite**, not **in front of**, for something across a street was stated in three fields; it now lives only in the learner-error box. *Tail* was removed as an antonym of *front*, together with the matching link in the *tail* entry.

On *place*, the new decimal sense now speaks of one figure in a number, not "a number". The signpost "chance to join" became "student or team", which fits *he lost his place on the team*.

**Spend.** US$0.27; US$0.63 for the day so far.

**What did not work.** The defining-vocabulary check caught three words in new definitions (*rank*, *decimal*, *membership*). All were rewritten in plainer words.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with the band-1 words that have the most senses and were read least recently: *pick up*, *come out*, *field*, *away*, *power*, *quarter*.

**For the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose **build** again. The next twelve milestone-1 words in the queue were six prefixes (*sub-*, *super-*, *under-*, *en-*, *in-*, *inter-*), three combining forms (*bio-*, *mid-*, *multi-*), and three grammar terms (*auxiliary*, *conjunction*, *determiner*). A combining form is a word part like *bio-* that comes from another language and joins with other parts, some of them not English words on their own (*biology*). Milestone 1 now has only six words left.

The entries went through the same pipeline as in cycle 1: schema check, inflection tool, pronunciation panel, both reviewers, my adjudication, the definition-vocabulary check, and the cross-reference tool.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 80 issues; 18 decision lines were for blocking issues. Of these, 68 were applied and 12 rejected, each logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. This batch drew more blocking issues than cycle 1. Prefixes are hard to get exactly right, and several of the reviewers' catches were real mistakes of mine:

- *super-*: I had described informal *super-easy* ("very easy") as a use of the prefix, while the usage note in the same entry called *super* in *super easy* an adverb. The adverb reading is the right one, so the prefix explanation no longer mentions it.
- *inter-*: I called *intercity* a noun, but it is an adjective (*an intercity train*) made from the noun *city*. Fixed.
- *en-*: my list of words where *en* is not the prefix included *enjoy*, which really is *en-* plus *joy*. It is now *enter*, *energy*, and *engine*. Two examples (*encircle*, *entrust*) did not fit the definition "put into or onto". The definition now includes "around", and *entrust* was replaced by *encode*.
- *sub-*: *subzero* means below zero, so it moved to the "under or below" sense. Reviewer A also found two real uses missing: "lower or passed on" (*subcontract*, *sublet*) and "nearly" (*subtropical*, *subarctic*). Both are now senses.

Other coverage added after review:
- *super-*: "above or beyond" (*supernatural*, *superimpose*).
- *under-*: "lower in position" (*understudy*, *undergraduate*).
- *auxiliary*: the American use for a volunteer group that supports a hospital.
- *conjunction*: the common phrase *in conjunction with*.

The spelling forms *em-* (before b, p, m) and *il-*, *im-*, *ir-* are now recorded in each entry's variants field instead of only in prose.

I rejected three kinds of request:
- That grammar terms such as *conjunction* should have no explanation field. The existing *preposition* entry, the model for these, has one, and it tells a learner how the thing behaves in a sentence.
- That the age use of *under-* (*an under-16 team*) is not a prefix use. I kept it but dropped the British plural-noun use (*under-fives*), which was the real problem.
- Hand-marking prefixed words in examples, which the house style does not do.

**Pronunciation.** Two British transcriptions are disputed: *auxiliary* and *en-*. For *en-*, my first transcription (*in*) failed in both varieties, so I changed the main form to *en* with *in* as a variant. American *en* then passed. Both disputed entries carry the `pronunciation-disputed` flag and have a line in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` stating my working assumption.

**Spend.** US$0.34; US$0.97 for the day.

**What did not work.** The defining-vocabulary check caught *rank* (three times) and *volunteers* in new definitions; they now read "position or importance" and "working without pay".

**What is next.** Six milestone-1 words remain. When they are built, the owner's ruling E5 asks the run to report that milestone 1 is finished.

**For the owner.** Two new pronunciation lines (*auxiliary* and *en-*, both British).
