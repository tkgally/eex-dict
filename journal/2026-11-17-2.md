# Run report, 2026-11-17 (second run)

## Cycle 1: build

**Why this cycle was a build.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a build: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still waiting for an entry. It had the largest backlog of the eligible kinds of work. The limit was twelve new entries and US$3 of paid model calls.

**Before the work.** No pull requests were left open by earlier runs. The two old working branches still on GitHub (`claude/nice-hawking-93pz5c` and `claude/zealous-mayer-8da3yb`) hold nothing that is not already on `main`, and each already has a line in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` asking the owner to delete it, because a run's tools cannot delete branches. The inbox was empty and there were no open entry reports (problem reports filed by readers).

**What was built.** Twelve band-2 nouns (band 2 is the second most important frequency group), in the queue's alphabetical order from *salesperson* to *scholarship*:

- **salesperson**: one sense, with a usage note on *salesperson* versus *salesman* and *saleswoman*. Its plural *salespeople* cannot be predicted by the inflection rules, so it was added to the project's own exceptions table (`schema/inflection-exceptions.json`).
- **salon**: a hair or beauty business, and a regular meeting of writers and artists at a rich person's home; a "false friends" note for translators warns that a similar word means a living room in some languages.
- **sample**: four senses (a part that shows what the whole is like, with a free-sample subsense; a substance taken for testing; a group chosen for a survey; a piece of recorded music reused in a new song).
- **sandal**, **scarf** (plural *scarves*), **sausage** (with the British, old-fashioned idiom *not a sausage*, meaning "nothing at all").
- **savings**: money kept, and money not spent because something costs less; it is plural only, and a note records that American English also says *a savings of ten dollars*.
- **saying**, with the idiom *as the saying goes*.
- **scare**: a sudden fright (with a subsense for a health scare) and a public panic such as a bomb scare.
- **scenery**: natural scenery and stage scenery, with a short guide to choosing between *scenery*, *view*, and *landscape*, and a learner error (*sceneries*).
- **scheme**: a British official plan, a dishonest plan, and an arrangement (*a color scheme*), with a note that the word sounds neutral in British English but suspicious in American English, and the idiom *in the scheme of things*.
- **scholarship**: money for a student's education, and serious academic study.

**Checks.** All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, three outside models plus an open pronouncing dictionary.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 33 issues, one of them blocking. The blocking one was right: I had given *sample* a learner error (*for sample* instead of *for example*) that I could not be sure learners really make, and the house rules forbid invented errors, so it was removed. Of the 32 minor issues, 26 were applied: shorter signposts (the short labels that help a reader pick a sense), a better *salon* example showing the meeting rather than the room, *downtown* instead of the British *town center*, a *sausage* definition that no longer says every sausage is cooked (some are dried and sliced), *headscarf* as one word, a clearly dishonest example for *scheme*, and the idiom written as *in the scheme of things*. Six were rejected with reasons logged: both reviewers asked for a hand mark on *salespeople*, but the site already marks listed plural forms; *it goes without saying* already lives under **go**; *a change of scenery* belongs under **change** by the rule that an idiom goes under its first noun (noted in `NEXT.md` for that entry's next review); and two notes were kept because they add a fact that a label alone cannot show.

The cross-reference tool left twelve return links for a person to place because it could not tell which sense of the target entry they belong to; I placed all twelve by hand (for example, `plot-n`'s "secret plan" sense now lists `scheme-n` as a synonym, and `view-n`'s "what you see" sense compares `scenery-n`).

**Spend.** US$0.34 this cycle; US$0.68 so far today of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. My first pass at the exceptions file re-sorted the whole table; I reverted that and inserted the one new line in place.

**What is next.** More cycles in this run if the clock allows; the build queue continues after *scholarship*.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. The two stale branches can be deleted when convenient.

## Cycle 2: review

**Why this cycle was a review.** After the first cycle merged, the mode selector chose a review: a careful second reading of entries that already exist. There were no unreviewed drafts and no open entry reports, so the cycle continued the "coverage pass" set by the owner's ruling B1 of 2026-10-24: a reading of the 500 most important band-1 entries (band 1 is the most frequent group of words), most senses first, asking of each one whether anything a learner needs is missing. The next four on that list were **care** (noun), **care** (verb), **certain**, and **child**, each with four senses.

**What I added before the reviewers saw them.**

- **care** (noun): the common combinations *intensive care*, *a care home*, and *customer care*, and an example of *take care not to* do something.
- **care** (verb): a new subsense for *care for someone* meaning to love them (*I know he cares for you*), which is different from the polite, usually negative *I don't much care for his music*; and a note on the American *could care less*, which means the same as *couldn't care less* though many people think it is wrong.
- **certain**: a subsense for *a certain Mr. Diaz* (someone known only by name), an example of *a certain charm* (a quality hard to describe), and a pointer to a future pronoun entry for *certain of the guests*, which was added to the queue.
- **child**: the saying *children should be seen and not heard*, *childbirth* in the word family, and the headword marked in bold in the core idea. I also removed a usage note and a translator's grammar note that only repeated the learner errors and the comparison of *child*, *kid*, *baby*, *toddler*, and *teenager*: the house rule is one fact in one field.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 34 issues, 6 of them blocking; all six were right and were fixed. *Take care* is also a friendly goodbye, and its definition said only "be careful", so the goodbye example did not match it; the definition now covers both. The phrase *care of* (an address on a letter) claimed that the full form is mainly British, which is not true; that claim is gone. **Certain** in *a certain amount* or *to a certain extent* had been defined as "some, but not a lot", but *a certain level of skill* can be high; it now means an amount or degree that is not stated exactly. In **child**, the example *He treats the dog like his own child* did not show the sense "someone's son or daughter", and *He sulked like a child* used the ordinary sense in a comparison; both were replaced.

Of the minor issues, 22 were applied (for example, *make certain* got a clearer definition, a usage note about *certain to* now says it is the speaker who is sure, and several repeated facts in **care** (verb) were cut down to one place). Six were rejected with logged reasons: a closed-list grammar code that the reviewer did not like, a formatting point that house rule 18 settles, the claim that *attention* is not a synonym of *care* in *with care*, the claim that the new *love* subsense does not belong under *like*, the claim that *a certain amount* is not a separate sense (it has its own word combinations), and a request to move the note that *in care* takes no article into a grammar code, when no code exists for it.

**Spend.** US$0.18 this cycle; US$0.86 so far today of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Two decisions at first did not record because a reviewer had raised two issues on the same field; the logging tool asked which one was meant, and I gave the number. One new definition used *fond*, which is outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words that definitions are written in); it now says *have warm feelings for*.

**What is next.** The coverage pass stands at 361 entries; the next four are **choice**, **circle**, **climb**, and **cloud**.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**Why this cycle was a build.** After the second cycle merged, the mode selector chose a build again. Its limit was twelve new entries; I took eight, because a third cycle in one run has less room left for the checking work that follows the writing, and the rule is that later entries must be as full as the first ones.

**What was built.** Eight band-2 nouns, next in the queue's order:

- **science fiction**: stories, books, and movies about invented future events, and, after the reviewers' advice, a second sense for something that seems impossible (*Talking to your phone used to sound like science fiction*). The informal short form *sci-fi* is recorded as a variant.
- **scientist**, with a comparison to *researcher*.
- **scooter**: three kinds, each its own sense: the small motorcycle, the stand-up kind pushed with one foot or driven by an electric motor, and the electric seat for people who cannot walk well.
- **scratch**: the mark, the act of rubbing your skin, and the sound, with the idioms *from scratch* (from the very beginning), *up to scratch* (good enough), and *without a scratch* (not hurt or damaged at all).
- **scream**: the cry, a loud machine sound (*a scream of tires*), and the informal *He's a scream* (very funny).
- **screenshot**.
- **screw**: the metal fastener and a ship's propeller, with *have a screw loose* (be slightly crazy) and *put the screws on someone* (pressure someone with threats).
- **sculpture**: the work of art and the art itself, with a short note on how a **statue** differs.

**Checks.** All 16 pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 17 issues, 3 of them blocking, and all three were right. One reviewer said the figurative use of *science fiction* deserved a sense of its own rather than an example with a note, and that the example did not fit the first definition; it now has its own sense. The other caught that I had defined the *scratch* "rubbing" sense as something you do only because your skin feels uncomfortable, while my own example showed a man scratching his head while thinking; the definition is wider now and also covers an animal's claws. Of the minor issues, 13 were applied (a clearer *scooter* definition, *has used* after *since*, a typical collocation in place of *a small screw*, *without a scratch* covering objects too). One was rejected: a reviewer wanted *a scream* (a very funny person) marked as dated, but it is still in everyday informal use.

The cross-reference tool found that `cry-n` lists **scream** as a synonym while I had listed **cry** only as a comparison; I made them match. I also removed **scientist** from the word family of **science fiction**, where it did not belong, and placed two return links by hand (`mark-n` and `cut-n` now compare **scratch**).

**Spend.** US$0.23 this cycle; US$1.09 so far today of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Two new definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (*imaginary*, *itches*); both were rewritten in plain words.

**What is next.** The build queue continues after *sculpture*; the review coverage pass continues with **choice**, **circle**, **climb**, and **cloud**.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
