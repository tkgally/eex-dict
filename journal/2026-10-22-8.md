## What the reviewers found, and what I decided

There were 52 issues. I applied 18 and rejected 34, each with a logged reason. Reviewer-a raised all but four of them. Reviewer-b raised nothing on seventeen of the twenty entries; its three calls on *son* and *sort* were rejected.

Applied:
- **solution**: sense 3 said the mixed-in substance "cannot be seen", which is not what makes a solution (a colored liquid can be one). It now says the substance is mixed so completely that it becomes part of the liquid. Sense 2 said only "the answer to a question in a game or a test"; it now covers problems in mathematics too. The core idea, the one-sentence summary at the top of a long entry, follows both changes.
- **sort**: the comparison of *sort*, *kind*, and *type* said *sort* is more informal than *kind*. It now says only that *sort* is more common in British English and *kind* in American English. The "putting in order" sense lost its "informal" label (it is neutral, and used in computing) and the unnatural collocation *do a sort*. The usage note no longer bans *sort of* from all serious writing.
- **soil**: the note on *soil*, *earth*, and *dirt* is more careful about *earth* (a material you dig or hold) and about American *dirt*.
- **space**: the learner error *not enough place in my suitcase* stays, but its note no longer claims that *place* can never follow *enough*.
- **speech** and **speaker**: they were listed as words learners confuse. They are related, not confused, so each now points to the other under "see also". I changed both entries; the cross-reference tool would otherwise have put the old link back.
- Smaller fixes: *spirit* (an article removed from *great team spirit*, which is uncountable; a sharper definition of the "mood" sense), *society* (the "rich people" sense now says it is often found in *high society*), and *sofa*.

Rejected, among others:
- Eight objections to sense 4 of *speech*, "a group of lines that an actor says in a play". Reviewer-a held that actors only have "lines" or "monologues". *She has a long speech in the second act* is ordinary theater English.
- The claim that *something that you hear* is too broad a definition for *sound*.
- Several requests to remove the "informal" label from conversational phrases (*by the sound of it*, *that's the spirit*, *for a song*). The house test is whether the phrase would jar in a report, and these would.
- The request to give the phrase *sort of* its own entry. The house rule keeps idioms under their keyword entry.
