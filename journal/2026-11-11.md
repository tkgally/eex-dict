**The entries.** A few points worth knowing:

- *jelly* means different foods in American and British English. The entry gives the American fruit spread first (the dictionary is written in American English), then the British cold dessert, labeled British, with *gelatin* named as the usual American word for it, then the general sense of a soft, partly solid substance. It also has the idiom *turn to jelly* (of legs or knees, to go weak with fear).
- *lead* here is the word that rhymes with *need*: first place in a race (with a subsense for the margin, *a two-goal lead*), an example to follow, the main part in a play, a clue (with a subsense for a possible customer, *a sales lead*), and two British senses, a dog's strap and an electric wire. The metal *lead*, which sounds like *red*, has a different origin, so by the project's rules it will be a separate entry (`lead-n-2`); the same is true of *launch* meaning a motorboat (`launch-n-2`). Both are already pointed to from the new entries and are listed in `NEXT.md` for a later build.
- *laughter* and *laptop* each got one learner error that learners really make: *a laughter* (it is uncountable) and *in my laptop* (English says *on*).
- *learner* has a British sense, a person learning to drive, with a short note for translators about the red L sign on such cars in Britain.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Every entry goes to two automatic reviewers from different companies, who check each field against the house rules. They raised 42 issues, of which 2 were *blocking* (must be settled before the entry can be published). Both concerned the same thing: in *jelly*'s third sense, marked uncountable, one example said *a clear jelly*, which is a countable use. The example also stated a science fact about frog eggs, which examples must not do, so I replaced it with an ordinary sentence rather than change the grammar label.

Of the 40 minor issues, I accepted 37 and rejected 3. The accepted ones were mostly small corrections: collocations filed under the wrong type (*grape jelly* is noun plus noun, not adjective plus noun), one definition of *laughter* that used *laughing* (a dictionary should not define a word with its own close relative), a misleading note that *suit* (as in lawsuit) is "used mainly by lawyers", and a translator's note on *lead* that only listed its senses without naming a real trap. That reviewer also noticed that the business sense, a possible customer, was missing; it was added. I rejected three suggestions to restructure senses, each with a logged reason: launching a ship stays a subsense of launching a rocket, the example and first-to-act uses of *lead* stay together, and *market leadership* is covered by the first sense of *leadership*.

Five definitions were reworded afterwards to keep them within the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions are written in): for example *occasion* became *event*, and *spacecraft* became *into space*. Five cross-references from other entries back to the new ones had to be placed by hand, because the tool could not tell which sense of the other entry they belonged to (*introduction*, *case*, *clue*, *cable*, *student*).

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: rereading entries that are already published to improve them. There were no unreviewed drafts and no reader reports, so the cycle continued the *coverage pass*. This is your ruling B1 of 2026-10-24: the 500 most important common words are reread to check that every frequent present-day use, idiom, and pattern is covered. The pass goes through the entries with the most senses first. This cycle took the next four: *only* (adverb), *paper* (noun), *pay* (verb), and *personal* (adjective). The one entry between them in the list, *out* (preposition), is a function word. Function words have a later round of their own, so it was left for that. The pass now stands at 283 entries.

**What I changed before the reviewers saw the entries.**
- *only*: added the use *that will only make things worse*, where the action has a bad result. I trimmed a usage note that repeated the word-order rule from the second sense. That note also mentions a conjunction use (*I'd come, only I'm busy*) that has no entry yet, so I queued *only* as a conjunction.
- *paper*: the entry stated three times that the material is uncountable, so I removed the usage note that repeated it. I added *paper money*.
- *pay*: added the sense of an account or investment producing money (*this account pays very little interest*). I removed a usage note that repeated a learner error.
- *personal*: removed a usage note and part of a pronunciation note. Both repeated the *personal* / *personnel* confusion, which the entry already covers elsewhere.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two automatic reviewers raised 58 issues. Four were blocking:
- In *only*, the "no one else" sense had a definition that began with *and*, so it could not replace the word in a sentence. It is now *not anyone or anything except the person or thing mentioned*.
- In *only*, the "very recently" sense said *not longer ago or later than*, which was confusing and partly wrong. It is now *as recently as a particular time*.
- In *pay*, the use in *the job pays well* was defined as if a person were paying. The definition now takes the job as its subject.
- Both reviewers objected to *alone* (adjective) as a synonym of *only*. I rejected this one. In this dictionary, *you alone can help* sits in the adjective entry for *alone*, under its sense "only", so the link points to the right place.

The reviewers also found real gaps, and I added them:
- *only if*, *only when*, *only then*, including the word-order change in *Only then did she understand*
- a newspaper as a company (*she works for a local paper*)
- a person's collected *papers*
- *pay someone to do something*
- *a personal computer*, *a personal trainer*: things made for or working for one person

Several definitions were reworded and several repeated facts removed. In total I applied 47 suggestions and rejected 11, each with a logged reason. Two rejections: *give* stays a synonym of *pay*, because one sense of *give* means exactly that (*I gave him ten dollars for it*); and *pay the price* belongs under *price*, because idioms go under their first noun. *price* does not have that idiom yet, so it is now on the list in `NEXT.md`.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again, which means writing new entries from the queue. A build may write up to twelve entries. I took eight: this was the run's third cycle, and the rules prefer fewer, fuller entries to a full but thinner batch. The eight words, in queue order, are *lecture*, *left* (the noun), *legislation*, *lemonade*, *liar*, *lifestyle*, *lifetime*, and *light bulb*. The dictionary now has 4,168 entries, all reviewed.

**The entries.**
- *lecture* has two senses: the teaching talk, and the "telling-off" sense (*I got a lecture from my dad about coming home late*), which is labeled disapproving.
- *left* has two senses: the direction, with a subsense for a turning (*take the next left*), and the political sense. It also carries one learner error that learners really make: *in the left* instead of *on the left*.
- *legislation* records that the word is uncountable, with the common error *a new legislation*.
- *lemonade*, like *jelly* in the first cycle, names different things in the two main varieties. In American English it is a fresh drink of lemon juice, water, and sugar. In British English it is a sweet drink with bubbles. Each sense is labeled with its region, and a note warns translators to check which variety a text uses.
- *lifetime* has the length of a person's life, the working life of a thing, and the phrases *once in a lifetime* and *the chance of a lifetime*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two automatic reviewers raised 31 issues. None were blocking. I applied 22 and rejected 9, each with a logged reason.

What I applied:
- Some explanation fields only restated the collocations, so I removed them.
- A repeated fact about *legislation* being uncountable was kept in one place only.
- The wording "small balls of gas" for the British drink became "bubbles".
- An awkward example for *lifetime* was replaced.
- Both reviewers noticed that *lifetime* is often used loosely to mean "a very long time" (*it felt like a lifetime*, *that was a lifetime ago*), so that is now an informal subsense.
- *light bulb* gained the spelling *lightbulb* and the informal phrase *a light bulb moment*.

What I rejected:
- Both reviewers objected that *left* is marked as having no plural although *take two lefts* is possible. That marking is made by a script from the main sense, not by hand, so it is not a drafting error.
- The idiom *out of left field* belongs under *field*, because idioms go under their first noun. *field* does not have it yet, so it is listed in `NEXT.md`.
- A region label means "mainly used there", so *lemonade* as a fresh drink keeps its American label even though the drink is also known in Britain.

The cross-reference tool left two links back to the new entries for me to place by hand:
- *law* now compares itself with *legislation* in its "one rule" sense.
- *lifestyle* now points to the phrase *way of life* under *way*, instead of comparing itself with the whole entry for *way*.
