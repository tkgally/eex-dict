**The entries.** A few points worth knowing:

- *jelly* means different foods in American and British English. The entry gives the American fruit spread first (the dictionary is written in American English), then the British cold dessert, labeled British, with *gelatin* named as the usual American word for it, then the general sense of a soft, partly solid substance. It also has the idiom *turn to jelly* (of legs or knees, to go weak with fear).
- *lead* here is the word that rhymes with *need*: first place in a race (with a subsense for the margin, *a two-goal lead*), an example to follow, the main part in a play, a clue (with a subsense for a possible customer, *a sales lead*), and two British senses, a dog's strap and an electric wire. The metal *lead*, which sounds like *red*, has a different origin, so by the project's rules it will be a separate entry (`lead-n-2`); the same is true of *launch* meaning a motorboat (`launch-n-2`). Both are already pointed to from the new entries and are listed in `NEXT.md` for a later build.
- *laughter* and *laptop* each got one learner error that learners really make: *a laughter* (it is uncountable) and *in my laptop* (English says *on*).
- *learner* has a British sense, a person learning to drive, with a short note for translators about the red L sign on such cars in Britain.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Every entry goes to two automatic reviewers from different companies, who check each field against the house rules. They raised 42 issues, of which 2 were *blocking* (must be settled before the entry can be published). Both concerned the same thing: in *jelly*'s third sense, marked uncountable, one example said *a clear jelly*, which is a countable use. The example also stated a science fact about frog eggs, which examples must not do, so I replaced it with an ordinary sentence rather than change the grammar label.

Of the 40 minor issues, I accepted 37 and rejected 3. The accepted ones were mostly small corrections: collocations filed under the wrong type (*grape jelly* is noun plus noun, not adjective plus noun), one definition of *laughter* that used *laughing* (a dictionary should not define a word with its own close relative), a misleading note that *suit* (as in lawsuit) is "used mainly by lawyers", and a translator's note on *lead* that only listed its senses without naming a real trap. On *lead*, the first reviewer also rightly pointed out that *a lead role* uses *lead* as a modifier rather than as the noun being defined; the example now reads *the lead in a new TV series*. That reviewer also noticed that the business sense, a possible customer, was missing; it was added. I rejected three suggestions to restructure senses, each with a logged reason: launching a ship stays a subsense of launching a rocket, the example and first-to-act uses of *lead* stay together, and *market leadership* is covered by the first sense of *leadership*.

Five definitions were reworded afterwards to keep them within the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions are written in): for example *occasion* became *event*, and *spacecraft* became *into space*. Five cross-references from other entries back to the new ones had to be placed by hand, because the tool could not tell which sense of the other entry they belonged to (*introduction*, *case*, *clue*, *cable*, *student*).
