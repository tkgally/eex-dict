# Run report, 2026-10-26 (fourth run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (the script that picks each cycle's job from the state of the dictionary) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words still missing. Its reason: building had gone longest without a turn among the jobs allowed to run, and closure (entries for words that only the definitions use) still waits until milestone 1 is finished. Milestone 1 is the list of words the dictionary itself uses in its definitions, built first by the owner's ruling of 2026-10-24.

Before the work, the usual pre-flight checks found nothing stranded: no open pull requests, no open entry reports from the public, nothing in the inbox, and the three leftover work branches from earlier runs fully merged already. One of them was new since the last check, so it was added to the list of branches the owner may delete by hand (this tool set cannot delete branches).

Twelve nouns were taken, the per-cycle limit: *pasture*, *pest*, *pulse*, *razor*, *reptile*, *sorrow*, *stepdaughter*, *stepfather*, *stepmother*, *stepson*, *temper*, and *texture*. Each was drafted in full from my own knowledge and the house style guide, then passed through the standard checks: the schema validator (file shape and the closed lists of labels and codes), the inflection generator (plural forms from rules), the pronunciation check (three or four models and an open pronouncing dictionary must agree; all 24 transcriptions agreed), and two independent reviewers who read every field. All twelve entries are now `reviewed` and will appear on the site at its next build. Forty-two milestone-1 words remain.

Some of the content worth knowing about: *pasture* carries the idioms *greener pastures* and *put someone out to pasture*; *sorrow* has *drown your sorrows* and *to someone's sorrow*, and a short note on choosing between *sadness*, *sorrow*, and *grief*; *temper* has three senses (a person's nature, a burst of anger, a mood) with *lose your temper* and *keep your temper*; *stepfather* and *stepmother* each carry a learner-error note on the common mix-up with *father-in-law* and *mother-in-law*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 35 issues, none marked blocking (the kind that must be settled before an entry can be published). I accepted 30 and rejected 5, each with a logged reason.

The useful catches:

- Two everyday senses carried field labels that made them look specialist: *pest* (insects in houses, not only on farms) was labeled *agriculture*, and the heartbeat sense of *pulse* was labeled *medicine*. Both reviewers flagged each; the labels are gone.
- *Temper* first meant only "a tendency to become angry quickly", which left no home for *an even temper* (a calm nature). The first sense now means a person's nature, especially how quickly they become angry, and the example list shows both a calm and a quick temper.
- *Sorrow* was labeled *formal* for the whole entry, which clashed with the informal idiom *drown your sorrows*. The label now sits on the two senses only. A note comparing *sorrow* with *sorry* was removed: it was an English-internal mix-up, not a trap for translators.
- Small fixes: *gets on well with* (mainly British) became *gets along well with*; the reptile definition now says *scales* rather than *small hard plates*; weak collocations in the step-family entries were replaced; *rhythm* moved from a synonym of *pulse* to a "compare" link.
- The house tool that checks definition words against the defining vocabulary (the list of about 2,400 plain words definitions should use) caught *burst* in *pulse* and *combine* in *texture*; both definitions were reworded.

Rejected, with reasons: moving *drown your sorrows* to the verb *drown* (house rule: idioms go under their first noun); widening the step-parent definitions to cover same-sex marriages (the definitions follow the core use and match the existing *mother* and *father* entries); merging the anger and mood senses of *temper* (they take different phrases: *in a temper* versus *in a good temper*); and dropping a short note that tells learners which verbs introduce *greener pastures*.

Five back-links (the mirror of a synonym or antonym link) could not be placed by the tool because it could not tell which sense to attach them to; I placed them by hand on *field*, *beat*, *rhythm*, *joy*, and *mood*.

**Spend.** US$0.31 this cycle, nearly all on reviewer B (the pronunciation check cost about one cent); reviewer A runs on the owner's subscription at no charge. The day stands at US$3.61 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. One gap: *pulse* has an unrelated second meaning (beans, peas, and lentils as food), which by house rules needs its own entry, `pulse-n-2`. The queue tool cannot add a numbered second entry, so it is noted in `NEXT.md` to be queued by hand, like `bill-n-2` before it.

**What is next.** More milestone-1 building, the coverage review of the most important words, and a lint pass (the periodic housekeeping sweep), which falls due next run.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** After cycle 1 merged, the scheduler chose *build* again for the same reason: building is the job with the longest wait, and milestone 1 (the words the dictionary's own definitions use) must be finished before anything else is built. Twelve more words were taken: the nouns *treaty*, *vein*, *waterfall*, and *wax*, and the adjectives *blunt*, *dishonest*, *impatient*, *mechanical*, *modest*, *occasional*, *slippery*, and *tame*. They went through the same steps as cycle 1: drafting, the validator, the inflection generator, the pronunciation check (all 24 transcriptions agreed), and both reviewers. All twelve are now `reviewed`. Thirty milestone-1 words remain.

Worth knowing: *vein* has four senses (in the body, on a leaf, in rock, and a quality running through speech or writing) and the phrase *in the same vein*; *blunt* covers both a knife that will not cut and a person who speaks too directly, with the phrase *to be blunt*; *dishonest* and *impatient* each carry a learner-error note on the made-up forms *unhonest* and *unpatient*, which learners really do produce; *occasional* has a short note on *the occasional visitor*, where **the** does not point to one particular person.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** 50 issues, 8 of them blocking (the kind that must be settled before an entry can be published). I accepted 32 and rejected 18, each with a logged reason.

The useful catches:

- *Slippery*: I had marked it as forming its comparative only with *more* (*more slippery*). Both reviewers said *slipperier* and *slipperiest* are standard too, and they are right. The dictionary keeps a table of word forms its rules cannot predict; *slippery* now has a line there giving *slipperier*, *slipperiest*, and noting that *more slippery* is also used.
- *Mechanical*: the "machines" sense was coded as used only before a noun, but *the problem is purely mechanical* is normal English; the code is gone. *Automatic* was listed as a synonym of that sense, but a wind-up clock is mechanical without being automatic, so *automatic* moved to a "compare" link (it stays a synonym of the "without thinking" sense).
- *Tame*: a note for translators claimed that a pet cat or dog is not usually called tame. The reviewer pointed out that this was misleading and clashed with the definition; it was removed.
- *Wax* lacked the hair-removal use (*a leg wax*), which is now a third sense.
- Smaller fixes: a leaf example that stated a science fact was replaced; *film* became *movie* in an example; weak synonyms (*dull* for *tame*, *proud* as an opposite of *modest*) were dropped; *modest*'s one-line summary now covers all three senses.
- The defining-vocabulary check caught *mineral*, *attract*, and *showy* in definitions; all were reworded.

Rejected: ten objections from reviewer A that *comparative with more* is "not a grammar code" or that the inflection note repeats it. That code is house practice, already listed in the project's notes as an objection to reject, and the inflection note is written by a script to match it. Also rejected: two requests to remove one-line explanations that state who or what a sense applies to (*used of animals*), which house rule 15 requires.

Seven back-links (the mirror of a synonym or antonym link) were placed by hand, for example *occasional* as the opposite of *regular* and *frequent*.

**Spend.** US$0.32 this cycle; the day stands at US$3.92 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. Reviewer A's precision this cycle (the share of its issues that I accepted) was low, 56 percent, almost all because of the repeated grammar-code objection. The next lint pass, due next run, should look at whether that objection needs a line in the reviewers' house rules.

**What is next.** More milestone-1 building if the scheduler picks it, then the lint pass.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: lint

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler forced a *lint* pass, the periodic housekeeping sweep over the whole dictionary, because ten runs had passed since the last one. A lint pass writes no new entries. It runs the mechanical checks over everything and then reads the project's own records for contradictions and loose ends. This was the eighty-ninth.

The mechanical checks:

- **Size caps and links.** Every capped file is within its limit, and every link between project pages resolves. The wiki (the project's knowledge base of rules and notes) stands at 29,885 of its 30,000-word cap, so I shortened one note before adding to it.
- **Back-links.** When entry A lists B as a synonym or opposite, B should list A back. The tool added six missing mirrors by itself, for example *waterfall* now lists *falls* (from the *fall* entry) as a synonym, and *occasional* lists *odd* (*the odd visitor*). It could not decide where two others belonged, so I placed them by hand: *commonly* on the first sense of *widely*, and *simple* on the "not large" sense of *modest*.
- **Vocabulary check.** No definition anywhere uses a word outside the defining vocabulary (the list of about 2,400 plain words definitions are written in) without that word having an entry.
- **Reports.** Seven notes say "this has its own entry" for an entry that does not yet exist; all seven are the same false alarms as last time (the tool cannot name numbered second entries such as a second *school*, for fish). 317 definitions use their own headword, nearly all secondary senses naming the first (*wax*: a way of removing hair using warm wax), which is acceptable. 710 pairs of linked entries may be pointing at the wrong sense; these wait for the cross-reference audit already in the queue.
- **Band vote.** Every queued word gets a band from 1 (most important) to 5 by a vote of three models. 65 words queued since this morning had none. The vote cost about one cent. Seven had votes more than one band apart, which I settled: six at the lower middle vote, and the verb *blunt* (to make less sharp) raised to 4, since the verb is much rarer than the adjective. One row, *bulls*, was only a plural that the vocabulary tool had queued by mistake; it is now marked as a duplicate of *bull*. A known fault recurred here: the vote rewrites the day's file instead of adding to it, so the morning's 64 votes were merged back by hand. The fault is already recorded in a note; it has not been fixed yet.
- **Leftover claims.** Six files recording which words earlier runs had taken were cleared, since those runs have finished.

**Reviewer precision.** Precision is the share of a reviewer's issues that I accept. A kind of issue that falls under 30 percent over at least twenty decisions is turned off for that reviewer. Reviewer A (the Claude model) stands at 78 percent over 1,212 decisions, and reviewer B (Gemini) at 80 percent over 349. One pair is under the line: reviewer B's spelling-and-format objections, at 22 percent. That pair stays on by the owner's ruling of 2026-10-05, as it has for weeks. Reviewer A's lowest kind is grammar codes, at 54 percent, dragged down by the repeated objection to the code *comparative with more* that cycle 2 described. Nothing was turned off.

**Records.** The wiki's index, the log, the open questions, and the list of items for the owner were read against each other. The index line for the precision note was out of date and is fixed. Three merged work branches from today are still on the remote; they wait in the owner's list for deletion.

**Spend.** US$0.009 for the band vote. The day stands at US$3.93 of US$15.

**What is next.** The next lint pass is due in ten runs. Building milestone 1 continues; thirty words remain.

## Cycle 4: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose *review*: rereading existing entries rather than writing new ones. There were no unfinished draft entries and no public entry reports, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That pass is the owner's ruling B1: the most important everyday words, those with the most senses first, are reread to make sure no common meaning or use is missing. Four entries were read, each with eight senses: the verbs *call*, *draw*, and *close*, and the noun *cut*. Forty-five words have now been through the pass.

I read each entry against my own knowledge of the word first and added what was truly missing. Both reviewers then read the revised entries.

What was added:

- *call*: birds and animals *calling* (*an owl calling in the woods*), and the British use for a train stopping at a station (*the train calls at every station*).
- *draw*: *draw blood*; receiving money regularly (*draw a salary*, *draw a pension*); causing a reaction (*the new law drew strong criticism*); getting strength or comfort from something (*she drew strength from her family*); and breathing in (*she drew a deep breath*).
- *close*: fingers or hands closing around something (*her fingers closed around the key*), and prices or shares *closing higher* at the end of a day's trading.
- *cut*: the British *power cut* (a stop in the electricity supply), a film's *cut* (a version of a movie, as in *the director's cut*), and the phrase *the cut and thrust of something*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** 65 issues, 2 of them blocking (the kind that must be settled before an entry can be published). I accepted 54 and rejected 11, each with a logged reason.

- The blocking pair, both on *call*: the "decide or predict" sense said *say what the result of something is or will be*, but its example *the referee called a foul* is a referee's decision, not a prediction. The definition now begins *decide or say*, which fits both uses.
- Several definitions were tightened: *close* no longer says *covers an opening*, which did not fit closing your eyes or a book; *draw* in the "take out" sense now covers blood and water, not only containers and bank accounts; three *cut* definitions no longer use the word *cut* to define itself.
- Notes that named no specific trap were removed: the general "this word has many senses" notes for translators in *draw* and *cut*. Facts said twice were trimmed: *call*'s usage note repeated a collocation, and *close*'s see-also note repeated the pronunciation note.
- *Drawed*, a made-up past tense, was removed from *draw*'s learner errors. House rules forbid errors made by regularizing a form, and the pronunciation note already gives *drew* and *drawn*.
- Pattern labels that called a preposition an adverb (*out of my account*, *out of a hat*) were corrected.
- The vocabulary check caught five words outside the defining vocabulary in the new definitions (*announce*, *version*, *steadily*, *pension*, *reaction*); all were reworded.

Rejected: requests for *call it a day*, *call the shots*, *draw the line*, *draw a blank*, and *close ranks*. Each has a noun, so by house rule it belongs under that noun (*day*, *shot*, *line*, *blank*, *ranks*). Also rejected: splitting *call*'s decide-or-predict sense in two, and turning *draw*'s breathing sense into a phrase, since it takes several objects (*a deep breath*, *its first breath*).

**Spend.** US$0.27 this cycle. The day stands at US$4.20 of US$15.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with the next eight-sense words, oldest reading first: *drive* (noun), *full*, *front*, and *place*. Building milestone 1 continues; thirty words remain.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. This was the run's fourth and last cycle.
