# 2026-09-26 (third run): ten common verbs, imagine to introduce

## What this run did

This was a **build** run. A build run writes new dictionary entries for words taken in order from the queue (the project's list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each run's kind of work) chose build because it was the kind of work that had waited longest.

Before starting, the run found no unfinished work from earlier runs and nothing in the owner's inbox.

The run wrote ten verbs, all in frequency band 1 (the most common words, which the dictionary also uses to write its definitions): *imagine, improve, include, increase, inform, injure, install, intend, interest,* and *introduce*. Each entry has two to four senses (separate meanings) with a short signpost, examples, grammar patterns, and collocations (the words it typically goes with). Some examples of what the entries cover:

- *imagine* separates making a picture in the mind, wrongly believing something (*you're imagining things*), and the weak use that means *suppose* (*I imagine you're tired*).
- *increase* explains when to use *increase*, *rise*, and *raise*.
- *introduce* warns translators that it does not mean *put something into something*, which is what its look-alike means in several languages.

## Checks and what the reviewers found

Every entry went through the full pipeline:

- **Inflections** (the forms *-s*, *-ed*, *-ing*) were made by the rules script. All ten verbs are regular, and all forms were verified.
- **Pronunciation.** All twenty transcriptions (American and British for each word) were verified by the panel of models, and the American ones also match the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. Nineteen were unanimous; *interest* got three of four votes, since it can also be said with three syllables, which the entry's pronunciation note mentions.
- **Two reviewer models** (from two different companies, neither of them Anthropic) read every field. They raised 16 issues, 5 of them marked blocking (meaning the entry cannot be published until the issue is settled). I read each one and decided it myself:
  - **Applied (4).** Both reviewers were right that one learner error on *increase* was wrong: I had marked *The prices were increased last year* as a mistake, but it is correct when someone raised the prices. It was removed. One reviewer was right that the second sense of *intend* (*The book is intended for young children*) should not say "make"; it now says "have a particular person, purpose, or use in mind for something". A second sense of *introduce* was reworded so it no longer repeats its own synonym, *bring in*.
  - **Rejected (12).** Most were objections that go against the house style: a quoted headword at the start of a translator note (which the style allows), the one-sentence "core idea" at the top of an entry (the one place where a sentence is allowed), where the stress mark sits in a transcription, and a request to list the rare British spelling *instal*.
  - **Escalated: none.**

The script that checks definitions against the defining vocabulary (the list of about 2,400 words definitions should be written with) found one word outside it, *ceremony*, in the third sense of *install*. It now says "often at an official event".

## Something that did not work

The cross-reference script (`tools/crossref.py`) adds a matching link to the other entry when one entry names another as a synonym or a word to compare. It added three correct links this run. But in two cases it added a second link of a different kind: *contain* already listed *include* as a word to compare, and the script added it as a synonym too; *tell* already listed *inform* as a synonym, and the script added it as a word to compare too. I undid both and changed the new entries to match what the older entries already said. The problem is written up in `wiki/notes/crossref-cross-type-backlinks.md`; the fix to the script is for a later run, as the rules require.
