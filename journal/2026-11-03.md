# Run report, 2026-11-03

## Cycle 1: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks each cycle's kind of work from what is most overdue) chose a review. There were no unreviewed drafts and no open entry reports from readers, so the cycle continued the coverage pass: a careful reading of the most important everyday words (band 1, the most frequent of the dictionary's five frequency groups), asking of each entry whether it is missing a meaning that a learner will meet. This cycle read four entries with six meanings each that had not been read since your rulings of 2026-10-24: **straight** (adjective), **stone**, **step**, and **state** (all nouns). That brings the pass to 159 entries.

**What was added.**
- *straight*: four new meanings. A drink with nothing added (*a straight whiskey*); tidy (*it took all weekend to get the house straight*); understood correctly (*let me get this straight*, *set the record straight*); and an informal meaning, living an honest life without crime or drugs (*after prison, he decided to go straight*). An American example, *straight A's*, now shows the "one after another" meaning.
- *stone*: a sub-meaning for paving stones and stepping stones, a new meaning for a gravestone, and the idiom *cast the first stone*.
- *step*: the British *steps* for a small folding ladder, the American music meaning (*a whole step*, *a half step*), and the idiom *every step of the way*.
- *state*: a sub-meaning for the form of a substance (solid, liquid, gas) and the informal *the States* for the United States.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Each entry went through the two-reviewer panel (two different language models that check every field and give closed verdicts). They raised 52 issues, 3 of them blocking (must be settled before the entry can be published). I applied 43, rejected 8, and escalated 1.

The blocking issues were fair: an example under the idiom *in step* actually showed its opposite, *out of step* (removed; the explanation keeps the opposite), and two examples for the solid-liquid-gas meaning stated science facts, which the style guide forbids (replaced with everyday sentences about wax and frozen samples).

Many of the minor issues were about one fact being stated in two places, which the notes rules of 2026-10-24 forbid: the weight of a stone in pounds appeared in a definition-side explanation and again in a translator's note; the American word *pit* for a fruit stone was in a label, a synonym, and a usage note; *take a step* was covered by both a usage note and a learner-error box. Each now lives in one field. The reviewers were also right that I had first filed "tidy" under the "level" meaning of *straight*; it is now a meaning of its own. Generic translator notes ("these meanings may need different words") were removed from *straight*; the one on *step* stays, because it names the actual split.

Rejected, with reasons logged: *keep a straight face* belongs under *face* by the keyword rule (the first noun of an idiom decides where it lives), and it is already there; the plural *stone* after a number is not written into the inflection table, which only a script writes; *a few steps from my house* is a natural use of the walking meaning.

Escalated: *a state of affairs* belongs under *state* by the keyword rule but lives under *affair*. Moving it means editing *affair* too, which needs its own review, so it is queued for when *affair* is next read, with a line in the curator file.

Changing cross-references touched four other entries: *brick* lost a weak "compare with stone" link, and *neat*, *correct*, and *clean* gained links back to the new *straight* meanings.

**Spend.** US$0.25, all on the second reviewer and the pronunciation check; the first reviewer runs at no charge.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. Two definitions had to be reworded because they used words outside the defining vocabulary (the plain-word list definitions are written from): *kilograms* and *sexually*.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with the remaining 48 six-meaning band-1 entries, from *smart*. The other queues are unchanged in `NEXT.md`.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build: new entries taken from the queue (the list of words still to be written) in the order set by the build-order decision. That order is now at the band-2 nouns, the second most frequent of the five frequency groups. The cap is twelve new entries; I wrote five so that each one could be complete: **agency**, **agenda**, **aid**, **analysis**, and **angel**, all nouns.

**The entries.**
- *agency*: a business that provides a service (*a travel agency*); an organization, often part of a government, with a particular job (*aid agencies*); and the formal sense of having the power to make your own choices (*a sense of agency*). A note for translators warns that a similar-looking word in some languages means a bank branch, which English calls a **branch**.
- *agenda*: the list for a meeting (*the next item on the agenda*); the things a person or group means to deal with (*high on the agenda*); and a secret aim (*a hidden agenda*). A translator's note warns that in many languages the similar word means a small book for dates, which English calls a **planner** or, in British English, a **diary**.
- *aid*: help given to countries or people in need (*foreign aid*, *food aid*); help from someone or something (*with the aid of a cane*); and a thing that helps (*a hearing aid*, *teaching aids*). It also has the idioms *come to someone's aid* and British *in aid of*, plus a learner-error box for *many aids*, since aid in the first sense is uncountable.
- *analysis*: careful study of something's parts, and the testing of a substance. The irregular plural **analyses** comes from the project's exceptions table, and the pronunciation note says how it sounds. Idiom: *in the final analysis*.
- *angel*: the religious spirit, a very kind person (*You're an angel!*), and a person who puts money into a new business. A note warns about mixing up **angel** and **angle**.

All ten transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and, for American English, by the open CMU pronouncing dictionary, which is consulted at run time and never stored. All plurals were confirmed.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two-reviewer panel raised 20 issues. Five were blocking, and they all came from one fault. I had defined the second sense of *aid* as help "that someone gives you", but two of its examples showed things: a cane and a lamp. Both reviewers caught it. The definition now covers help from someone or something. Of the 20 issues I applied 19. They included two weak synonyms that I removed (*plan* for *agenda*, *test* for *analysis*), two collocations filed under the wrong type, a decoration example for *angel* that did not show the religious sense, and a more exact definition of the money-giving angel, who invests rather than donates. I rejected one: reviewer A wanted the second sense of *aid* folded into the others, but it has its own fixed pattern, *with the aid of*, and is neither country aid nor an object.

Linking the new words to existing entries needed five links placed by hand. These are cases where the cross-reference tool could not tell which sense of the target entry to link to. *agency* is now compared with *organization*, *aid* is a synonym of *help*, *analysis* is a synonym of *study* in its research sense, and *angel* and *angle* point to each other.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. One idiom definition used *charity*, a word outside the defining vocabulary, and I reworded it.

**What is next.** More band-2 nouns in queue order, from the word after *angel*. The other queues are unchanged.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.
