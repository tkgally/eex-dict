# 2026-10-23, cycle 1 of the 12:43 UTC run: the forty-third originality check

## What this cycle did

This was an **originality** cycle. Every tenth run, the scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks each cycle's kind of work) forces a check that our definitions are our own and not copied from a published dictionary. This was run 500, so the check was due. The tool `tools/originality_check.py` picked ten fields at random from the 2,733 entries. For each one I ran an exact-phrase web search and looked only at whether a published dictionary shows the same wording or the same distinctive shape of wording. I also asked one of the outside reviewer models (reviewer-a) whether each definition reads as copied. The completed checklist is in `reviews/originality/2026-10-23.md`.

Start-of-run housekeeping found nothing to do: no pull request left open, no unmerged work on old branches, an empty inbox.

## Results

- **Original (5):** the usage note on `life-n` (when *life* takes an article), the usage note on `father-n` (*Dad*, *Daddy*, *Father*), `ah-interj` (*used when you suddenly understand, notice, or remember something*), `off-adv` (*away from work or school for a time*), and `over-adv` (*another time, from the beginning*). The searches found only unrelated pages: essays, school leave policies, song titles.
- **Generic overlap (2):** `girl-n`, *a female child*, which several dictionaries also give; and `disagree-v`, *have a different opinion from someone*, which is the plain way any dictionary or teacher would say it. A short plain definition that resembles others by necessity is not copying under the style guide, so both stay.
- **Rewrite (3):** none matched word for word, but each followed the shape of a learner's dictionary definition too closely, so I rewrote them in new words:
  - `rule-n`, the phrase *rule of thumb*: a search snippet showed a learner's dictionary's frame (a practical, approximate way of doing something), and ours (*a simple, practical way of judging or doing something that is not exact*) followed it. Now: *a rough guide that is easy to follow and gives a good enough answer most of the time*.
  - `effectively-adv` sense 2: ours was *in fact, although not officially or not in name*; a learner's dictionary defines the adjective *effective* as *in reality, although not officially intended*. Now: *looking at the real result, rather than at how things seem or what they are called*.
  - `close-adj` sense 8 (a room or the weather that feels stuffy): ours was *warm and uncomfortable, without fresh air*, the same frame as a learner's dictionary's. Now: *hot and heavy, so that the air feels still and hard to breathe*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

A rewrite counts as a review of the entry, so the three entries went through the full pipeline again: validation, the inflection and pronunciation checks (all verified), and the two-model reviewer panel. reviewer-b raised nothing. reviewer-a raised ten issues; I applied seven and rejected three.

Applied: my first rewrite of *effectively* (*in what really happens, whatever the name, title, or rules say*) was too narrow, since *the game was effectively over* involves no name or rule, so I rewrote it again (the wording above). The entry's false-friend note said *In several European languages*, which names a language group; the style guide wants language-neutral notes, so it now says *In many languages*, and its gloss of the sense now matches the new definition. Its learner-error example (*Effectively, you are right*) can be good English in the sense *in effect*, so the error is now a one-word reply used to agree (*"Is this your bag?" "Effectively."*). In `rule-n`, the note calling *against the rules* a *fixed* phrase was too absolute; it now says *usual*. In `close-adj`, sense 5 (*a close game*) said *won or decided by a very small difference*, which misses contests not yet decided; it now reads *with only a very small difference between the people or teams trying to win*.

Rejected: that `rule-n` sense 2 excludes rules of calculation (it covers *what is correct in a system*); that *follow the rules of the road* is a poor correction (it is natural); and that *during his rule* contradicts the uncountable label (an uncountable noun takes a possessive normally).
