# Run report, 2026-10-25 (fifth run of the day, scheduled run 20261025T184427Z-ax7wls)

## Cycle 1: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's job by weighing which kind of work is most overdue) chose a review. There were no draft entries waiting, no open entry reports from readers, no stranded pull requests or leftover branches, and nothing in the inbox. So the cycle continued the coverage pass. This is the reading of the most important everyday words that you ordered on 2026-10-24 (ruling B1). It asks one question of each entry: does it hold every common present-day use of the word? This cycle read six band-1 words, the most frequent band, each with ten or more senses: *throw*, *leave*, *cover*, *clear* (the adjective), *carry*, and *work* (the verb).

Before the reviewers saw them, I added the common uses I found missing:

- *throw*: losing a game on purpose (*throw a match*), and *throw yourself into something*.
- *leave*: *leave someone to do something*, and the phrase *leave someone to it*.
- *cover*: hiding a feeling or a sound (*laugh to cover her embarrassment*).
- *clear*: clear skin, a clear (free) afternoon, and the phrase *in the clear*.
- *carry*: a newspaper carrying a story, a proposal carried by a vote, and *carry yourself*.
- *work*: *Does Tuesday work for you?*

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** There are two reviewers, each a model from a different company. Reviewer A (a Claude model) checks every field of the entry, and so does reviewer B (a Google model). Together they raised 118 issues. Thirteen were blocking, meaning the entry cannot be published until the issue is settled. I applied 103 and rejected 15.

The main fixes:

- **leave**: The "remain" sense (*Is there any cake left?*) was defined as "have something remaining", which describes the passive, not the verb itself. It is now "make something remain", with a note that the passive *be left* is the most common form, and the examples are tagged to match. The reviewers also pointed out real gaps, and these are now filled:
  - a new sense for keeping space or time free (*leave room for dessert*, *leave enough time*);
  - a subsense for *leave someone with something* (*we were left with no choice*);
  - the phrase *leave someone cold*.
- **cover**: The protective meaning of *cover for someone* (lying for a friend) was hidden inside an explanation and now has its own subsense. There is also a new sense for guarding (*Cover me!*).
- **clear**: Skin became its own sense, and the misplaced example *clear blue eyes* was replaced. *Clear days* was removed from the money sense, and two signposts were shortened to the three-word limit. A signpost is the short label that helps a reader pick a sense from the list.
- **carry**: The arithmetic sense moved to the end, and an example sentence that was not actually an error was removed from the learner errors.
- **work**: The definition of *make someone work hard* used the word *work* itself and was rewritten. Working the land became its own sense.
- **Cross-references**: Six wrong ones were removed, together with the matching links in the other entries ("mirrors"): *get* as an opposite of *leave*, *go out* as a synonym of *leave*, *clothe* as a synonym of *cover*, *confused* as an opposite of *clear*, and *visible* as a synonym of *clear*. *Carpet* moved to the *cover* sense it fits.
- **Notes**: Five notes were removed because they only repeated another field or gave generic advice, under the "one fact, one field" rule. So was the learner error *throwed*, a regularized form that the style guide forbids.

Most rejections followed the keyword rule. An idiom lives under its first noun, so *throw in the towel*, *cover your tracks*, *carry the day*, *carry a tune*, and *work wonders* belong elsewhere. *Steer clear of* belongs under its first verb, *steer*. Two more were rejected for these reasons:

- *Is there any cake left?* stays in *leave*, because *be left* is that verb's passive.
- The error *It is clearly that...* stays, because learners really make it.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. Reviewer A again raised many small issues (about two-thirds of the total), and most were sound.

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) and all inflections came back verified. 132 milestone-1 words remain.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 67 issues, six of them blocking. I applied 57 and rejected 10.

The biggest repair was *similarity*. I had split it into an uncountable sense ("being alike") and a countable sense ("a way in which things are alike"). But my examples for the first sense used the countable form (*a strong similarity*). The examples and collocations are now sorted so that each sense shows its own grammar. A collocation is a word that usually goes with the headword.

Other changes:

- *Worm* gained a subsense for the soft young of insects (*a worm in my apple*).
- Two idioms were removed because the keyword rule puts them under another word: *variety is the spice of life* goes under *variety*, and *a can of worms* goes under *can*. The keyword rule says an idiom lives under its first noun.
- Several definitions were reworded to stay inside the defining vocabulary. For example, a lamp socket is now "the part of a lamp that holds a light and connects it to the electricity".

The rejections:

- Both reviewers asked for the body-part sense of *temple*, the side of the head, to be added. That word has a different origin from *temple*, the building, so house rules make it a separate entry, `temple-n-2`. I recorded it in `NEXT.md` to be claimed by hand, like *bark* (of a dog) and *ash* (the tree).
- *as thick as thieves* stays under *thief* because *thieves* is its first noun. *steal someone's thunder* stays under *thunder* for the same reason.
- Reviewer B asked for the plural *thieves* to be hand-marked in examples. The website already marks listed plurals in bold automatically, so I rejected that.

The cross-reference tool could not decide which sense should carry two return links. I placed them by hand: *temple* in the building sense of *church*, and *worm* in the computer sense of *virus*.

## Cycle 3: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose a review again. With no drafts or reader reports waiting, the cycle continued the coverage pass on six more of the most common words: *show*, *put*, *point* (the noun), *pass*, *give*, and *feel*. For each one I added the common present-day uses I found missing. Then both reviewers read every field.

**What was added.**

- *show*: having a visible feature or result (*show signs of wear*, *show a profit*).
- *put*: putting money on a race or a game, and the phrase *I wouldn't put it past him*.
- *point*:
  - a temperature (*boiling point*);
  - a tiny dot of light;
  - a narrow piece of land going out into the sea;
  - a unit for measuring changes (*prices fell ten points*);
  - the phrase *you have a point*.
- *pass*:
  - going beyond a number (*passed a million*);
  - *pass for* (*he could pass for thirty*);
  - *pass judgment*;
  - going from one owner to another (*the house passed to his daughter*);
  - the intransitive *the bill passed*, where the law has no object.
- *give*:
  - a punishment (*the judge gave him three years*);
  - giving a party;
  - producing something (*cows give milk*);
  - the informal prediction *I give it a year*;
  - the phrases *something has to give* and *don't give me that*.
- *feel*: suffering from something (*feeling the effects of higher prices*), and the phrase *I'm not feeling myself today*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 127 issues, eleven of them blocking. I applied 110 and rejected 17.

The blocking issues:

- An example sentence for *point* stated a checkable science fact about boiling water at altitude. The house rules forbid that, so it was replaced.
- An example for *give* (*How much did you give for that old car?*) sounds unnatural to American ears. It was replaced.
- Two entries had sentences filed under the wrong sense.
- *give* and *point* were each missing a common use: producing something, and *you have a point*.
- A regularized learner error (*gived*) is forbidden by the style guide and was removed.

Most of the minor fixes made each sense hold together:

- **pass**: The ball-passing use and the courtroom use (*pass sentence*) each became a subsense.
- **give**: The punishment and party uses had been hidden as subsenses that did not fit their parent senses. Each now has its own sense. The senses of *give* and *pass* were also reordered so the most useful come first.
- **feel**: The phrase *feel sorry for someone* was removed, because it only repeated the sense *feel for someone*.
- **Cross-references**: Six wrong ones were removed, together with their matching links in the other entries:
  - *point out* as a synonym of *show*;
  - *say* and *tell* as synonyms of *show*'s "prove" sense;
  - *run* as a synonym of *pass*;
  - *lend* and *contribute* as synonyms of *give*.
- **Notes**: Eight notes were removed because they repeated another field. So was the learner error *putted*.

The rejections:

- Most were idioms that belong under their first noun by the keyword rule: *give birth*, *give way*, *give rise to*, *pass the buck*, *pass muster*, *feel at home*.
- A few were settled house practice, such as listing phrasal verbs in both the word family and *see also*.

One judgment call: removing *lend* as a synonym of *give* also removed two links from *lend* back to *give*. Both sat on *lend* senses where the two words really are close ("lend a quality", "lend support"). I followed the house rule that a removed link takes its mirror with it. A later review of `lend-v` may want to restore one of them.

**Spend.** US$0.47 for this cycle. The day's total is about US$14.20 of US$15.

## Cycle 4: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose another build. The next twelve milestone-1 words in the queue were all adjectives: *bent*, *cooked*, *cruel*, *curly*, *curved*, *foolish*, *full-time*, *hidden*, *indoor*, *jealous*, *logical*, and *lucky*. I wrote each entry from scratch and ran it through the full pipeline. All inflections were verified. The inflections are the comparative forms, such as *luckier*, or a note that the word takes *more* instead.

Twenty-two of the 24 transcriptions were verified. The exception is *full-time*. The four-model pronunciation panel could not agree where the main stress goes, and its votes changed between calls, as they did earlier for *part-time*. The open pronouncing dictionary from Carnegie Mellon University agrees with the form I wrote: stress on the first part, as in *a FULL-time job*. I kept that form, marked the entry "pronunciation disputed", and added a line to the curator list for you. Reviewer B argued for stress on the second part when the word stands alone. That disagreement is recorded as escalated rather than settled.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 77 issues, five of them blocking. I applied 55, rejected 20, and escalated 2.

The blocking issues:

- The *core idea* of *bent* was garbled. The core idea is the one-sentence summary at the top of an entry with three or more senses. It now names the senses plainly.
- *foolish* had a learner error that was not an error. *It was foolish for you to...* is grammatical English. Both the learner error and the usage-note sentence saying it was wrong were removed.
- The two *full-time* stress issues described above.

Uses added during review:

- the informal *we're cooked*, meaning in serious trouble;
- *lucky for us*, as in *Lucky for us, the store was still open*.

Other fixes:

- *Curved* is no longer defined with its own base word *curve*.
- An example that stated a plant-care fact was replaced, because examples may not state checkable facts.
- Notes that repeated a grammar code were removed.

**One pattern to watch.** Ten of the rejections were the same complaint from reviewer A, the Claude model: that `comparative with more` is "not a grammar code". It is one. It is in the closed list of codes, and the inflection tool needs it to know that an adjective takes *more* rather than *-er*. Reviewer A also objected four times to the inflection note, which is written by a script and not reviewable. I logged this in the reviewer-noise note. A later run may add a line to the reviewers' house rules to stop it. That is a small change, but the rules say an observed problem is written down first and fixed in a later run.
