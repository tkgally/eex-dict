## Cycle 1: lint

**What a lint pass is.** Every ten runs the project stops adding to the dictionary and checks its own house: whether the word caps on its rule files hold, whether every link inside the knowledge base (the `wiki/` folder of rules and notes) still points somewhere, whether cross-references between entries agree in both directions, and how often each of the two automated reviewers is right. The selector tool forced this pass because ten runs had passed since the last one (2026-10-27). This is the ninety-first such pass.

**What was checked and what changed.**

- *Caps and links.* All word caps hold, though two files are at the edge: `routine-prompt.md` (2,499 of 2,500 words) and the wiki as a whole (29,984 of 30,000). I condensed the oldest sections of the reviewer-precision note to make room for this pass's lines. Every relative link resolves.
- *Cross-references.* When entry A names entry B as a synonym, antonym, or word to compare, B should name A back on the matching sense; these are called back-links. The cross-reference tool wrote seven back-links on its own (in *even though*, *figure*, *girlfriend*, *guy*, *offer*, *prefer*, and *tonight*, mostly from yesterday's new entries). Seven more it could not place because no single sense clearly matched; I placed those by hand: *quarter* sense 5 (a part of a town) now names *area*; *rent* names *borrow* on the "pay to use for a short time" sense and *lend* on the "let someone use for payment" sense; *spread* sense 4 names *communicate* (of a disease); *miss* sense 2 (arrive too late) names *make* as its opposite; *environment* sense 1 names *nature*; *grade* sense 1 names *score*. All thirteen entries still validate.
- *Reports for later review.* "Promised" entries (a note saying another entry exists when it does not): the same seven as last time, all false alarms caused by homographs and rows filed under another form. Definitions that use their own headword: 324, mostly secondary senses naming the first, which is harmless; a review judges each. Pairs of cross-referenced entries where the back-link may sit on the wrong sense: 730, waiting for the audit under your ruling C1.
- *Band vote.* New queue rows added by tools have no frequency band (1 = most common, 5 = rarest) until three word-list models vote on them. 45 rows were voted on for US$0.008. Three had votes more than one band apart; each was settled at the lower middle vote, as the method requires (*American* 2, *August* 2, *footballer* 3). While checking these rows I found that the vocabulary checker had again queued inflected forms as if they were dictionary words: *lentils*, *peas*, and *spoils*. I marked them duplicates and queued *lentil* in its place. This is the fifth time this tool gap has shown up; the note on it says a fix is due.
- *Reviewer precision.* Precision means the share of a reviewer's objections that I accepted after checking. Reviewer A (the Claude model) stands at 0.80 over 2,002 decisions, reviewer B (Gemini 4 Argon) at 0.81 over 759. The rule switches off a kind of objection when a reviewer is right less than 30 percent of the time over twenty or more decisions. No kind qualifies this time. The closest is reviewer A's objections about word forms (*inflection*): right 4 times in 17. If it stays under the line once it passes twenty decisions, the next lint will switch it off.
- *Housekeeping.* Six old claim files (the lists of words each past run had reserved) were pruned. The wiki index lists every page; the open questions and the curator file have no duplicates or stale lines. The three old `claude/` branches on GitHub hold nothing that is not already in the main branch; their cleanup lines were already in the curator file, since this tool set cannot delete branches.

## Cycle 2: build

**What a build cycle is.** A build cycle drafts new dictionary entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to write). Each entry then goes through the full pipeline: a format check, generated word forms, a pronunciation check by three outside models, and a field-by-field reading by two reviewers from different labs (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, Gemini 4 Argon). I settle every objection myself before an entry is marked reviewed. The selector chose this mode because building had the largest backlog among the modes it could pick. It allowed up to twelve entries. I took ten, so that each could be full.

**What was built.** Ten common nouns from band 1 (the most frequent words): *kid*, *lady*, *living room*, *luck*, *magazine*, *model*, *north*, *November*, *orange* (the fruit and the color), and *passport*. All twenty transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel. The richest entries are *luck* (two senses and eleven fixed phrases, from *good luck* to *the luck of the draw*), *model* (seven senses, from a small copy to a fashion model to a computer model), and *lady* (five senses, plus *ladies and gentlemen* and *First Lady*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** I made 60 decisions: 53 accepted, 7 rejected. Eight objections were "blocking", meaning the entry cannot be marked reviewed until they are settled. The main ones:

- *lady*, the title sense. My example said a knight's wife was called *Lady Ana*. Both reviewers rightly pointed out that a knight's wife takes her husband's family name (*Lady Kim*), while *Lady* plus a first name belongs to a duke's or an earl's daughter. The examples are fixed. They now use names from the house list as family names after a title. That follows the working assumption on the open curator question about *Ms. Kim* and *Dr. Lee*.
- *model*. Reviewer A objected that *a model student* (meaning a perfect one) is an adjective use. The house rule is one part of speech per entry, so that example moved out, the usage note now points to an adjective entry, and *model* (adjective) is queued. Reviewer B showed that *business model* fitted neither of my senses, so I added a sense, "a particular way of organizing or doing something, which others can follow".
- *luck*. Reviewer A argued that my second sense ("good things that come by chance") overlapped the first and did not fit the examples (*Did you have any luck finding an apartment?*). It now reads "success in something that you try to do, especially when it depends partly on chance". Three missing idioms were added.
- *north*. The second sense defined *north* using the word *north*. It now says "the top part of a country or an area, as it is shown on most maps".

The rejections were these. A request for a "figurative" label, a value the closed label sets do not have. A request for a "rude" attitude label for the way some Americans address a stranger (*Hey, lady*): no such value exists either, and the explanation already says it. Reviewer B's view that a note on where *magazine* is stressed repeats the transcriptions; house practice keeps such notes because learners cannot read IPA (the phonetic alphabet). A request to reorder *model*'s senses. And reviewer B's objection to bold marks on a phrase named as a phrase, which the style guide allows.

**Other checks.** The checker that keeps definitions inside the defining vocabulary (the core word list that definitions are written in) failed at first on *Britain*, *lord*, *knight*, and *eleventh*. I rewrote those definitions: the *British* region label now carries the place, and *November* is "the month before December, near the end of the year". Two bad queue rows that the vocabulary tool added were marked duplicates (*advertisements*, *eleventh* as a noun). Cross-references were made symmetric: *copy*, *system*, and *woman* now point back to the new entries.

**Spend.** US$0.36 this cycle; US$1.89 of US$15 for the day.

**What is next.** The next build rows are *percent*, *pound*, *region*, and *response*.

**For the owner.** Nothing new. The curator question about list names used as family names after a title now also covers *Lady Kim* and *Lord and Lady Lee* in `lady-n`.
