# Run report, 2026-10-27 (scheduled run 20261027T064413Z-tm5zwm)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose **build**: writing new entries from the queue of words still to be written, with a ceiling of twelve entries and a paid-call budget of US$3. The reason it gave was the usual one, that build had the highest "debt" among the eligible modes; closure (writing entries for words the dictionary already uses in its own definitions) was still blocked because milestone 1 had six rows left.

Milestone 1 is the set of about 2,400 words the dictionary uses to write its definitions; the owner ruled that it must be finished before anything else is built. The twelve words claimed this cycle were the last of it plus six pronouns that point back to existing determiner entries: the prefixes *out-*, *over-*, *trans-*; the suffix *-dom*; the combining forms *micro-* and *tele-*; and the pronouns *half*, *many*, *more*, *most*, *much*, and *no one*. **Milestone 1 now has no queue rows left.** Five homographs (a second, unrelated word with the same spelling, such as *bark* of a tree versus a dog's *bark*) are still to be claimed by hand, as `NEXT.md` lists; the selector now points to closure.

Pre-flight found no stranded pull requests, no reader reports, nothing in the owner's inbox, and three old work branches with nothing unmerged on them, already listed for deletion in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`.

Each entry went through the full pipeline: the schema check, the inflection tool, the pronunciation check (three outside models vote on each IPA transcription, and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary is consulted for American English where it has the word), and the two-reviewer panel. Reviewer A is a Claude model; reviewer B is a Google model. Both read every field and give a closed verdict on each.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 71 issues, 10 of them blocking. I applied 53 and rejected 18, each with a logged reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`; nothing needed escalation. The main changes:

- *over-*: my third sense lumped together "across", "beyond", and "through a period of time" and leaned on *overtake* and *overflow*, which fit badly. Reviewer A was right. The entry now has five senses: too much, above, across or through (*overseas*, *overnight*), control (*overcome*, *overrule*), and turn or fall (*overturn*, *overboard*).
- *micro-*: reviewer A caught that a *microwave* is not "very small", and that "on a local scale" is not a meaning of *micro-*. Sense 2 now means "dealing with small details, or one small part of something larger" (*micromanage*, *microeconomics*, *microclimate*), and *microloans* moved to the "very small" sense.
- *tele-*: reviewer B noted the missing "by telephone" use (*telesales*, *telemarketers*); it is now a sense.
- *many*: reviewer A argued that *the many* (*for the many, not the few*) is a noun use, not a pronoun sense. The house already treats *the former* and *the following* this way, so I moved it out and queued a noun entry, `many-n`.
- *most*: I had defined it as "nearly all", but *most* means more than half. Fixed in the definition, the signpost, and a learner-error note.
- In *out-*, *more*, *most*, and *much*, a fact was stated twice (once in an explanation, again in a note); the extra copies were removed.

The rejections were mostly requests for idioms that already live in a sister entry (*more or less* in the determiner *more*, *what's more* and *so much for* in the adverbs) or that belong under their first verb (*make the most of* under *make*), plus seven requests to bold the affixed word in *trans-* examples, which house practice leaves unmarked.

The pronunciation panel agreed on everything except *trans-*, where I had written *tranz* and two of the three votes said *trans*. I changed the transcription to *trans*, kept *tranz* in the plain-English note as the form often heard before a vowel, and it then passed.

**Spend.** US$0.34 this cycle; the day stands at US$1.32 of the US$15 cap. Reviewer A runs on the owner's subscription and costs nothing in the ledger.

**What did not work.** The definition-vocabulary check caught *further* in two core-idea sentences and *millionth* in *micro-*; all three were reworded in plainer words before the entries were marked reviewed.

**What is next.** With milestone 1 finished, closure opens for bands 1 and 2. For the owner: nothing new needs a ruling this cycle; the open items in `NEXT.md` stand.

## Cycle 2: closure

**What the cycle did.** With milestone 1 finished, the selector chose **closure** for the first time since the owner's ruling of 2026-10-24 paused it. Closure writes entries for words the dictionary already uses in its own definitions or names in its cross-references. Under that ruling it takes only words in bands 1 and 2 (the two most important of the five frequency bands). It allowed twelve entries and US$3 of paid calls.

The `closure` rows (words found in existing definitions) gave a poor start: four of the six were inflected forms queued as if they were headwords (*polished*, *candles*, *eyebrows*, *nails*). This is a known weakness of the vocabulary tool, which guesses a word's base form and part of speech and sometimes fails to strip an ending. I marked the four rows as duplicates and claimed the real words instead: *candle*, *eyebrow*, and *nail*. *Polish* is in band 3 and has to wait. With the `closure` rows used up, I took five `family` rows (words named in an existing entry's word-family list): the verbs *please* and *step*, and the nouns *base*, *board*, and *boyfriend*. *Diamond* and *lemon* complete the set. Midway through, the vocabulary gate refused *baseball* in two definitions (in *diamond* and *base*), because no entry existed for it. I wrote that entry too, for eleven in all. The note on the vocabulary tool's weakness now records this third recurrence, and says a fix is worth a logged decision in a later run.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 49 issues, 3 of them blocking. I applied 42 and rejected 7.

- *base*: both reviewers caught an example that made a checkable science claim (*the paper turns blue in a base*), which the example rules forbid. It is gone. Reviewer A argued that my "starting point" sense mixed two things: a foundation of skills or ideas, and a group of supporters (*customer base*, *fan base*). These are now two senses. Its other suggestions added a mathematics sense (*base 10*) and widened the "main place" sense to cover a hotel used as a base on a trip. Reviewer B pointed out a real pronunciation trap: the plural *bases* is said differently for *base* and for *basis*. That now goes in the entry's pronunciation note.
- *nail*: I had defined *hit the nail on the head* as naming a cause. Reviewer A was right that it means saying something exactly right, and that is now the definition.
- *diamond*: the card sense now covers both the suit and a single card. The core idea no longer claims that diamonds are usually cut with four sides.
- *board* gained the British idiom *take something on board*. The American and British phrases for meals that come with a room moved into the usage note.
- *boyfriend*: an example about an *ex-boyfriend* did not fit the definition and was replaced.

Rejected: a request to call *whatever she pleases* transitive (the verb stands for an omitted *to do*), a request to file *burn the candle at both ends* under *burn* (the house rule takes the first noun), a request to reorder the senses of *board*, and two reviewer objections to my *nail* adaptation note. That note follows the style guide's own model for *bank*.

The pronunciation panel voted three to one for a three-syllable American *diamond* (*DY-uh-mund*). That is now the main form, and the two-syllable form is a variant.

**What did not work.** The cross-reference tool placed a back-link from *walk* to *step* on the wrong sense of *walk* (its "explain step by step" sense, matched by the shared word *step*). I moved it by hand. It also could not choose a sense of *basis* for the link from *base*, so I placed that one by hand too. The vocabulary gate then refused *bat* in the new *baseball* definition. *Bat* has no entry yet, so the definition now says *a long stick*.

**Spend.** US$0.36 this cycle; the day stands at US$1.67 of US$15.

**What is next.** Closure continues from `family` and `crossref` rows in bands 1 and 2. Lint is due in three runs.

## Cycle 3: closure

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose **closure** again, with the same limits: twelve entries and US$3 of paid calls. No `closure` rows (words found in existing definitions) are left in bands 1 and 2, so the claim tool took the next ten `family` rows, words named in an older entry's word-family list. All ten are band-1 nouns, the most important band: *call*, *classroom*, *cover*, *gift*, *gold*, *homework*, *ice cream*, *meeting*, *mom*, and *mum*. The heavy ones, *call* (six senses) and *cover* (nine after review), were drafted with the matching verb entries open beside them, so the noun does not repeat the verb's senses or idioms.

Each entry went through the full pipeline. The pronunciation panel verified every transcription, including the two stress patterns of *ice cream*: American English stresses the first word and British English the second. The pronunciation note says so.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 39 issues, 4 of them blocking. I applied 30 and rejected 9.

- *gold*: all four blocking issues were here, and both reviewers were right. My "first prize" sense was two definitions run together with a comma. It is now one definition: a small, round piece of gold given as first prize. (*Medal* has no entry yet, so the definition avoids that word.) My color examples (*the leaves turned red and gold*) used *gold* as an adjective, which belongs in the existing adjective entry. They now show the noun (*decorated in cream and gold*).
- *cover*: reviewer A found two missing uses, *cloud cover* (a layer over an area) and the British *provide cover* for a worker who is away. Both are now senses.
- *call*: the "visit" sense had a dated example (*made a call on every store*). It now covers house calls and sales calls, which are current. The "need" sense (*there's no call for that*) lost an explanation field in favor of a collocation. *A wake-up call* was added as an idiom.
- *gift*: the third sense had joined "easy" and "cheap"; it now means only "easy". The first sense now also covers gifts to schools and hospitals, and *don't look a gift horse in the mouth* was added.
- *mom* and *mum*: the point about a capital M when the word is used as a name moved from the explanation field into the usage note, as house style asks for ordinary nouns.

Rejected: *heart of gold* (it already lives under *heart*, the first noun), marking *struck* in a phrase example, a "dated" label on the narrowed visit sense, and treating *mum* as a spelling variant of *mom*. They are separate words linked to each other.

**What did not work.** After review, the vocabulary gate refused *occasion*, *property*, and *charity* in the reworded definition of *gift*, and *occasion* in *meeting*. An earlier check by the same tool had passed these words, and I could not see why. The gate is what continuous integration runs, so I trusted it and reworded all four. The lesson for later cycles is to run the gate itself (`--gate --changed`) after the last edit, not the plain report.

**Spend.** US$0.31 this cycle; the day stands at US$1.98 of US$15.

**What is next.** More `family` rows remain in band 1 (*reading*, *return*, *run*, *set*, and others). Lint is due in two runs.
