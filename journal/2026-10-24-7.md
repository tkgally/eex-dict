# Journal, 2026-10-24 (seventh run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each run's kind of work) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to be written. It gave this reason: build had waited longest among the kinds of work that were allowed. Closure (writing entries for words the dictionary already uses in its definitions) is blocked until milestone 1 is finished. Milestone 1 is the first target: an entry for every word on the defining vocabulary list, the roughly 2,400 plain words that definitions are written in. Before this cycle it had 258 rows left.

The queue handed out twelve number words, and all twelve were written and reviewed:

- **Five "teen" numbers:** *eleven*, *fourteen*, *fifteen*, *seventeen*, *nineteen*. Each has one sense, the same shape as the *eighteen* entry from the morning run. Four of them carry a pronunciation note on the stress trap that learners really meet: *fourteen* and *forty*, *seventeen* and *seventy*, and so on are easy to mix up in speech.
- **Five "tens" numbers:** *forty*, *fifty*, *seventy*, *eighty*, *ninety*. Besides the number itself, each has a second sense for the plural: *in her forties* (age), *in the high eighties* (temperature), *the seventies* (the years 70 to 79 of a century). *Fifty* also has an informal paper-money sense (*Can you change a fifty?*). The usage note says that 41 to 49 and so on are written with a hyphen (*forty-one*). *Forty* and *ninety* keep real, common misspellings as learner errors (*fourty*, *ninty*).
- **Two ordinals:** *fourth* and *fifth*. (An ordinal is the number that gives a place in a series: *fourth*, *fifth*.) Each has three senses: place in order (*the fourth floor*), place in a competition (*fourth place*), and dates (*the fifth of June*). They follow the pattern of *second* and *third*, which were reviewed earlier.

Every entry went through the full pipeline: the schema check, the inflection tool, the pronunciation check (all 24 transcriptions, American and British, were confirmed by the voting models), the two outside reviewers, my decisions on each problem they raised, and the final checks.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 41 issues; I applied 28 and rejected 13, and none needed the owner.

- The biggest finding was good: in *fourth*, examples such as *He finished fourth in the race* use the word as an adverb, which belongs in a separate entry under the house rule of one part of speech per entry. I replaced them with uses that belong here (*He took fourth place*), and I made the same change in *fifth*, where the reviewers had not caught it.
- Both reviewers objected to the wording "used for" in the plural sense of the tens numbers, which is padding the style guide bans. I rewrote the definition in all five entries ("the numbers from 40 to 49, especially as a person's age, a temperature, or the years of a hundred-year period") and made the explanation more accurate: the plural usually comes after *the*, and after *her* or *their* only for age.
- One reviewer said *fiveteen* and *fiveth* were made-up errors. They are regularized forms, which the style guide does not allow as learner errors, so I removed them.
- I rejected these objections: that the date sense (*on the fourth*) belongs in a noun entry (the earlier ordinals settled this as the house pattern); that *fifty-fifty* should be a phrase inside *fifty* (it is a headword of its own, now queued); that *the Fourth of July* is missing from *fourth* (it is a holiday name, not a sense of the number); and that *ninty* is an invented misspelling (it is a common real one).

New queue rows: *fourth* and *fifth* as nouns (the fractions, *a fifth of the class*), *fifty-fifty* (adjective), and the ordinals from *eleventh* to *ninetieth*. They wait unbanded until the next band vote, the step where the word-list panel gives each row a frequency band.

**Spend.** About US$0.70 for this cycle (the reviewers and the pronunciation check). The day's total is about US$10.68 of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. One reviewer raised seventeen issues on *fourth* alone; about half of them repeated the date objection on each field, which is noise rather than a fault.

**What is next.** The remaining numbers (*twelve*, *thirteen*, *sixteen*, *twenty*, *thirty*, *sixty*) are next in the queue and should follow the same shapes. After them, milestone 1 continues in queue order.

## Cycle 2: site

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *site*, the check of the public website, because the site check had waited longest among the allowed kinds of work. The website is not stored in the repository: it is built from the entry files by a script (`tools/build_site.py`) each time the project's automatic checks run, and published at <https://tkgally.github.io/eex-dict/>. In this cycle I built the site locally and ran the site checker (`tools/site_check.py`). The checker opens the pages in a real browser, at a phone width (360 pixels) and a desktop width, and tests what a reader does.

The build made 2,430 headword pages from 2,816 entries. A headword page can hold more than one entry: *forty* the number and any other part of speech of the same word share a page. No entry was held back as an unfinished draft. There were no redirects, the stub pages kept for renamed entries.

- search, including an inflected form (*ran* finds *run*), a phrase, and a misspelling (*abiilty* offers *ability*);
- the hover preview on linked words;
- the translator's view toggle, and whether it is remembered after a reload;

- the menu;
- the page for an address that does not exist, which runs a search for the name;

- the grey display of a cross-reference whose target entry does not exist yet;

I also opened the new number pages from cycle 1 (*forty*, *fourth*). They show the new plural sense (*in her forties*) and the *fourth place* examples.

**What changed.** Nothing in the site tools or templates; nothing needed fixing. The only file this cycle changes, apart from the record-keeping, is the metrics history (`metrics/history.jsonl`), which now records that the site build was fine.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *build* again, for the same reason as in cycle 1. It handed out twelve rows from milestone 1. All twelve were written and reviewed through the full pipeline.

- **Six more numbers:** *twelve*, *thirteen*, *sixteen*, *twenty*, *thirty*, *sixty*. They use the shapes settled in cycle 1. The teens have one sense and a note on the stress trap (*thirteen* / *thirty*). The tens have a plural sense (*in their twenties*). *Twenty* also has the informal paper-money sense (*Can you lend me a twenty?*). *Twelve* has a short usage note: *twelve o'clock* can mean noon or midnight, so *twelve noon* or *twelve midnight* is clearer. *Twenty* has a pronunciation note: in fast American speech the **t** is often dropped (*TWEN-ee*).
- ***-est***, the suffix that makes superlatives (*biggest*, *happiest*). It covers which adjectives take it, the spelling changes, and two common learner errors: *the most biggest*, and leaving out *the* (*She is tallest girl*).
- ***whereas***, with two senses: comparing two facts (*My brother loves sports, whereas I prefer reading*) and the formal legal use at the start of a document's reasons.
- ***dare*** as a modal verb (a helping verb like *can* or *must*): *I daren't tell him*, *How dare you!*, and the phrase *I dare say*. The ordinary verb *dare* (*He dared me to jump*) is a separate entry; it is now in the queue.
- ***ache***: to feel a dull, lasting pain (*My back aches*), and also to want something very much (*She ached to see her children*).
- ***annoy***: to make someone a little angry.
- ***bore***: to make someone bored, and to make a deep hole (*bore a well*). It has a sub-sense for eyes that "bore into" someone. A usage note says that *bore* is also the past tense of *bear*.

All 24 transcriptions (American and British) were confirmed by the pronunciation check.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 34 issues. I applied 19 and rejected 15; none needed the owner.

- On *-est*, one reviewer was right on several points. The first draft said the suffix goes only on "short" words, but then gave *happiest* as an example, and it claimed that *-est* goes on exactly the same adjectives as *-er*. I rewrote the definition and the explanation. I also replaced a doubtful learner error (*of the class*) with a real one (a missing *the*).
- On *bore*, the reviewers found missing uses: boring for water or oil, and eyes boring into someone. I added both.
- On *dare*, the phrase *I dare say* was described in prose as old-fashioned and British, but it had no labels saying so. The labels are now set and the prose is shorter.
- I removed two learner errors that did not belong. The one on *annoy* (*annoyed* or *annoying*) belongs in the adjective entries. The one on *whereas* (adding *but*) is really a mistake learners make with *although*.
- I rejected these: the reviewers' repeated request to move the plural number uses and the *thirty of the workers* uses into separate noun or pronoun entries (a standing project rule keeps them in the number entry); an objection to the legal *whereas* (it does mean "considering that", not a contrast); and a claim that *bore*'s note on its two meanings is generic (it names the specific split, like the model note for *bank* in the style guide).

**Fixes outside the batch.** The cross-reference tool reported two places where a link needed a person's choice, and I placed both by hand: *drill* now lists *bore* as a synonym, and *although* now points to *whereas*. I also caught an error of my own in *twenty*: it listed *twelve* as a family word. I removed it on both sides.

Two small inconsistencies in entries merged in cycle 1 are left for a review cycle; they are listed in `NEXT.md`. The money sense of *fifty* says "marked with the number 50", where *twenty* now says "with a value of 20". And *eleven* and *ten* list each other as family words, though they share no form.

**Spend.** About US$0.67 for this cycle. The day's total is about US$11.35 of US$15.

**What is next.** Milestone 1 continues in queue order. A lint pass is due soon.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: lint

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler forced a *lint* pass, because ten runs had gone by since the last one. Lint is the periodic check of the whole project. It runs the mechanical checks over all 2,828 entries, then reads the wiki (the project's knowledge base of rules, decisions, and notes) for problems a script cannot see. No entries were written or rewritten. This was the eighty-sixth pass.

**What the checks found and what I did.**

- **Word caps and links.** All within limits; every link between wiki pages resolves.
- **Back-links.** The cross-reference tool keeps links two-way: if *receive* names *give* as an opposite, *give* should name *receive*. It added mirror links in six entries. It also reported six pairs where it could not tell which sense of the target the link belongs to, and I placed those by hand: *dismiss* (two senses, against *discount*, *employ*, and *hire*), *dip* against *duck*, *series* against *season*, and *whereas* against *where*.
- **Promised entries.** Some usage notes say "this has its own entry". Ten such promises had no entry and no queue row. I queued four: *oh boy*, *Buddhist* and *Muslim* as adjectives, and *double over*. Three already had rows under slightly different names. The other three are unrelated words with the same spelling and part of speech: the *corn* on a foot, the *school* of fish, and the American *story* meaning a floor of a building. The queue cannot yet name a second word of that kind, a known tool gap with a note already in the wiki.
- **Circular definitions.** 325 definitions use their own headword. Most are harmless, because a later sense leans on the first (*walk*: "the distance that you walk"). 83 are on a first sense and are worth a look in review. I changed none.
- **Missing senses.** 681 pairs of entries where a synonym or opposite may point at the wrong sense, or where a sense may be missing. They are the list for the planned cross-reference audit.
- **Band vote.** 49 queue rows had no frequency band yet. (Bands, 1 to 5, tell the queue how important a word is.) Three word-list models voted, for less than one cent. Thirteen rows had votes more than one band apart, mostly the ordinals such as *thirteenth* and *sixtieth*. I settled each at the lower middle vote. All 49 rows can now be handed out.
- **Reviewer precision.** Precision is the share of a reviewer's flags that I accept. Reviewer A is at 70 percent overall and reviewer B at 50 percent. One of reviewer A's checks, *coverage* (does the entry miss a sense?), has fallen to 23 percent over 65 decisions. Usually a check that low is switched off. This one stays on, because your ruling B1 requires it. The rejected calls mostly asked for compounds, phrasal verbs, holiday names, or plural number forms as senses.
- **Index and log.** Every wiki page is in the index, every link in the index points to a page, and every recent run has a log entry.

**What did not work.** The band vote tool overwrote the record of an earlier vote from the same day. Those votes had already been applied, so nothing was lost from the queue, but the record file had dropped 16 rows. I merged them back. The vote tool also wants its `--votes` path relative to the vote folder, which failed the first time. I wrote both problems up as a note (`wiki/notes/band-vote-unbanded-overwrite.md`) for a fix in a later run, as the project's rules require.

**Spend.** Less than one cent. The day's total is about US$11.36 of US$15.
