**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from what is most overdue) chose *review*: a second reading of entries that already exist. There were no draft entries waiting and no open entry reports from readers, so the cycle continued the coverage pass, which re-reads the most important words (band 1, the first of five editorial frequency groups) to check that every current sense, phrase, and grammar point a learner needs is there. The four words read were *grass* (noun), *great* (adjective), *hang* (verb), and *happy* (adjective). The coverage pass has now read 409 entries since the owner's rulings of 2026-10-24.

Before the work, the run checked for unfinished work left by earlier runs. There were no open pull requests (proposed changes waiting to be merged). The four old working branches on GitHub hold nothing that is not already in the main copy, and each is already listed in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` for deletion, which this tool set cannot do. The inbox was empty.

**What was added before review.**

- *grass* gained the British slang sense of a person who secretly tells the police about other people's crimes, the common phrase *a blade of grass*, the British idiom *put someone out to grass* (make someone stop working because they are old), and the recent slang *touch grass* (go outside and stop arguing online), labeled as new and dismissive.
- *great* gained *great for something* (*great for learning words*) and a subsense for the names of larger kinds of animals and plants (*a great white shark*).
- *hang* gained subsenses for fixing a door on its hinges and for the way clothes and hair fall (*the dress hangs beautifully*), an informal American sense meaning to spend time with friends (*do you want to hang this weekend?*), and the idiom *hang loose*. One of its two learner errors said the same thing as the other and was removed.
- *happy* gained the informal compound sense of being too ready to use something (*trigger-happy*). A usage note and a translator's grammar note that only repeated other fields were removed, following the rule that each fact lives in one place.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (two language models from different companies that read every field) raised 65 issues, 7 of them blocking (an entry cannot be finished until a blocking issue is settled). I accepted 53 and rejected 12, each with a recorded reason. All seven blocking issues were real:

- The new *trigger-happy* sense was coded "not gradable", but one of its own examples says *so click-happy*. Both reviewers caught this; the code was removed.
- *happy* sense 3 joined two meanings with different grammar: satisfied (*happy with the result*) and willing (*happy to help*). It is now two senses.
- An example of *great* was a bare *Great!* reply, which one reviewer read as an interjection rather than the adjective. It now reads *You got the job? That's great!*
- *great* sense 4 (*a great fan*, *a great reader*) was defined with a verb phrase. It is now an adjective phrase, *very interested or active, more than most people*, with the restriction to person nouns in an explanation. The *great big* subsense, which is about size, moved under the size sense.

Other accepted changes: *hang* no longer lists *crash* as a synonym for a frozen computer (a crash closes the program; a hang does not), and the matching link on *crash* was removed; the spend-time sense now comes before the computer sense; the *grass* lawn definition no longer uses the word *grass*; the drug sense is labeled old-fashioned; two translator notes on *great* and *grass* that were generic or repeated the pronunciation note were rewritten or removed.

Rejected: requests to add *a snake in the grass* and *hang your head*, which belong under their first nouns (*snake*, *head*) by the house rule for idioms; a request to call *happy* "satisfied" only after a verb (*a happy customer* is normal); and two formatting objections to bold marks that the house style requires.

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: new entries from the queue of words waiting to be written. It allowed twelve; I claimed ten, to leave room for checking them properly. All ten are band-2 nouns (band 2 is the second of five editorial frequency groups: words most intermediate learners know or need soon), and all ten were written and finished: *torch*, *tourist*, *tradition*, *traffic light*, *tragedy*, *trail*, *translation*, *trash*, *trash can*, and *trend*. The dictionary now has 4,697 entries, all reviewed.

**The entries.** Several of these words mean different things in different varieties of English, or have uses a learner can easily miss:

- *torch* is first an electric hand light in British English (American *flashlight*), then a burning stick, then a tool that makes a hot flame. It has the idioms *carry a torch for someone* (love someone who does not love you back) and *pass the torch* (hand on a responsibility).
- *trash* is labeled American for its main senses (waste, and *the trash* as the container or the computer folder), and it covers the informal *that movie is trash* and the offensive use about people. It also has *talk trash*. A learner error warns against *trashes*, since the word is uncountable.
- *trail* has four senses (a path, a line of marks with *a paper trail*, the signs that lead to someone, and the campaign trail) and the idiom *blaze a trail*.
- *tragedy* has a subsense for the common use about a waste (*it would be a tragedy to cut down that tree*). A translator's note warns that the matching word in some languages sounds much too strong for this.
- *trend* has *buck the trend*. *Traffic light* has a usage note on the British habit of saying *the traffic lights* for one set.

Every plural was produced by the rules script and checked. Nineteen of the twenty pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel (three language models voting, plus an open pronouncing dictionary for American English). The exception is the British form of *translation*. In four checks the panel kept splitting between an **s** and a **z** sound in the first part. Both are standard, so I listed the **s** form first and the **z** form as a variant. The entry carries a "pronunciation disputed" flag, and there is a line for you in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 45 issues, 4 of them blocking, and I accepted all four:

- An example about a band *on the concert trail* sat under the sense about politicians looking for votes, which it does not fit. Both reviewers caught it, and it now reads *the candidates are back on the trail*.
- The offensive sense of *trash* had an explanation, a field the style guide keeps for function words. I removed it.
- The definition of *buck the trend* was ungrammatical. It now reads *act differently from most others, or develop in the opposite way to what is generally happening*.

Of the other issues, 38 were accepted. The *torch* tool sense now includes heating, and it lost its "American" label, since British speakers use it too. *Traffic light* is now defined as standing where roads meet rather than at the side of a road. *Translation* is defined as mainly about written words, to match its comparison with *interpretation*. Two translator's notes on *trash* and *torch* only repeated the labels and were removed. Three issues were rejected:

- A request to put the American sense of *torch* first. The electric light is the sense most learners meet, and its label shows it is British.
- Two requests to widen the campaign-trail sense. With the concert example gone, the political sense is accurate.

The cross-reference tool links related entries both ways. It could not decide which sense of *traveler*, *disaster*, and *track* should point back to the new entries, so I placed those three links by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.32 for the reviewers and the pronunciation panel. The day's total is US$0.54 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** The pronunciation panel was unstable on *translation*. The same transcription got different votes on repeated checks, so repeating the check was not a reliable way to settle it.

**What is next.** The build queue continues after *trend*.

**What needs the owner.** One new pronunciation question: the British first sound of *translation* (**s** or **z**). It is in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` with the working assumption.
