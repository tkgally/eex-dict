# 2026-10-24, run at 00:43 UTC, cycle 1: closure

## What this run did

This was a **closure** cycle. Closure means writing entries for words that the dictionary already uses in its own definitions but does not yet define, so that a learner who meets a word in a definition can look it up. The cycle added twelve new entries, all now marked `reviewed` (checked by two outside reviewer models and by me):

- `tend-v` (*tend to do something*; *tend toward*; *tend the garden*, with the American *tend bar*)
- `ounce-n` (weight, liquid, and *not an ounce of sense*), with the saying *an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure*
- `dye-n` and `dye-v`
- eight verbs from the word-family list: `equalize-v`, `equip-v`, `err-v`, `exemplify-v`, `eye-v`, `falsify-v`, `familiarize-v`, `fan-v`

Two of the three rows the closure list gave me were not real headwords: *tending* and *ounces* are inflected forms (a verb's *-ing* form and a noun's plural) that a tool had queued as if they were base words. I marked those rows as duplicates and wrote the base words, *tend* and *ounce*, instead. I also added a queue row for the verb *dye* so that it could be written beside the noun.

## Pronunciation and word forms

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each entry) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, a group of models that vote on each transcription, plus, for American English, a check against the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. One British form, *falsify* with the vowel of *fall*, was disputed. I switched the main British form to the vowel of *doll* (`ˈfɒl.sɪ.faɪ`), which the panel confirmed, and kept the other as a listed variant. All inflected forms (*dyeing*, *eyeing*, *equipped*, *fanned*) came from the regular rules and were confirmed.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 49 issues. I applied 39 and rejected 10, each with a logged reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The useful corrections:
- `tend-v`: the *look after* sense had a "formal" label, but *tend the garden* or *tend sheep* is ordinary English. I removed the label and rewrote the explanation. A made-up example (*the road tends to the left*) was replaced.
- `ounce-n`: the liquid sense was labeled American, but fluid ounces are a British unit too. I removed the label. The weight note now says "one ordinary ounce", because a troy ounce, used for gold, is heavier.
- `familiarize-v`: I had offered *get used to* as an everyday replacement. That is wrong, since *get used to* means becoming comfortable with something, not learning about it. Removed.
- `dye-v` and `familiarize-v`: two adaptation notes (the notes that warn translators about traps) made absolute claims ("always", "never"). Both are softened.
- `equip-v`, `exemplify-v`, `fan-v`, `falsify-v`: collocation types corrected, and examples that did not match their grammar labels were fixed.

The rejections:
- `equalize-v`: one reviewer said the sports sense (*score the goal that makes the score level*) is not mainly British. I kept the British label. In this dictionary a region label means "mainly used there", a rule settled earlier, and American English usually says *tie*.
- `exemplify-v`: one reviewer wanted the two senses merged. I kept them apart: one has a thing as its subject (*this church exemplifies the style*), the other a person (*the teacher exemplified each rule*), and many languages translate them differently.
- *err on the side of caution*: one reviewer wanted this idiom as a phrase under *err*. By the house rule an idiom goes under its first noun, so it already lives under *side*. The *err* entry points there.
- A "proverb" label (none exists in the closed list) and a style objection to a core-idea sentence.

The cross-reference tool, which adds the matching link on the other entry, put the new *exemplify* link on the wrong sense of *represent* ("act for someone"). I moved it by hand to "be a sign of something".
