## What this cycle did

The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose a **build**: new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to write. It allowed up to twelve entries and US$3 of paid model calls. This cycle wrote the next twelve very common nouns in queue order: *sort, soul, sound, soup, space, spark, speaker, speech, speed, spelling, spirit,* and *spoon*.

Each entry was drafted from the house style guide, not from any published dictionary. Each has every current sense with its grammar, two to four examples per sense, collocations (the words that usually go with it), cross-references, and, where they help, idioms, a usage note, learner errors, and adaptation notes for future translators. Some highlights:

- *sort* has three senses (a kind, a person, putting in order) and five idioms (*sort of*, *of sorts*, *out of sorts*, *nothing of the sort*, *it takes all sorts*). It agrees with the existing *kind* entry on *these sort of problems* and on how *sort*, *kind*, and *type* differ.
- *space* has five senses, from a parking space to outer space to "I need some space", and it warns against *not enough place*.
- *spirit* has seven senses, including the mood sense (*in high spirits*) and strong alcohol, with a learner error for *in a good spirit*.
- *sound* and *speech* point learners to the comparison with *noise*, *talk*, and *lecture*.

## Checks

Before review, two tools checked each entry. The inflection tool generated the twelve plurals from its rules. The pronunciation check asked a panel of models to transcribe each word and compared the result with the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. All 24 transcriptions (American and British) were verified, and CMU agreed on every American one.

## What the reviewers found

Two reviewer models from different companies, called reviewer-a and reviewer-b, read every field. They raised 47 issues, 31 of them "blocking", meaning the entry cannot be published until each is settled. I read every issue and decided each one myself: 29 were applied and 18 rejected. Every decision is logged.

Reviewer-a was right about several things:

- *speed* said that *a fast speed* is an error. It is not; *a high speed* is only more usual. The learner error and the adaptation note were removed.
- *soup* said you *drink* soup only from a cup. Thin soup can be drunk from a bowl too, so the note now says so.
- The *space* learner error (*no place for my bag*) is natural English. It was replaced by a real error: *not enough place in my suitcase*.
- *speech* listed *direct speech*, *reported speech*, and *freedom of speech* as collocations of "spoken language". They are other uses, so they moved to a usage note.
- The slang sense of *speed* (a drug) no longer says the drug is always illegal.
- The note on *the Speaker* of a parliament now allows the title without *the* (*Speaker of the House*).

I rejected reviewer-a where it went against house rules or the facts. It wanted *sort of* moved out of the *sort* entry, but the dictionary keeps *kind of* under *kind*, and the two must match. It also said *sort* is not more British than *kind*. It is somewhat more common in British use, and the *kind* entry already says the same. I rejected three of reviewer-b's issues that asked for marks inside example sentences, which the style guide does not allow.

## Back-links

When an entry names another as a synonym or a word to compare, a tool adds the reverse link to the other entry. Two of the new links were wrong, and I fixed them by hand. A link from *spirit* landed on the "angry feeling" sense of *mood* instead of the plain "feeling" sense, so I moved it. And *spirit*'s pairing of *the spirit of the law* with *the letter of the law* had put a link on *letter* the written message. I dropped that link, and the contrast stays in an explanation. The vocabulary check also found eight words outside the 2,394-word defining list (such as *tiny*, *attraction*, and *whisky*), and I reworded those definitions.
