**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks each run's kind of work from what has gone longest undone) chose *build*: new entries taken from the headword queue in its set order. It allowed twelve. The next twelve rows were band-2 nouns (band 2 is the second-most-frequent group of words): *contrast*, *cook*, *cooking*, *cop*, *correction*, *corridor*, *costume*, *couch*, *count*, *counter*, *countryside*, and *creation*. All twelve were drafted, checked, and published as `reviewed`. Pre-flight found nothing left over: no open pull requests, no stray branches, no entry reports from readers, and nothing in the owner's inbox.

Each entry was drafted from the house style guide and my own knowledge, then went through the full pipeline: the schema validator; plural forms generated from rules; a pronunciation check by a panel of models plus the open CMU pronouncing data for American English (all 24 transcriptions, American and British, were confirmed); a field-by-field reading by the two reviewer models (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, a Google model); my decision on every issue raised; the defining-vocabulary check (definitions must use the dictionary's list of about 2,400 basic words, or words that have entries); and the cross-reference tool, which adds the matching link in the other entry when one entry names another as a synonym or related word.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 45 issues, one of them "blocking" (an issue that must be settled before the entry can be published). I agreed with 42 and rejected 4, each with a logged reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. None needed to go to the owner.

- The blocking issue: I had defined the phrase *in contrast to something* as "very different from something", which reads like an adjective. It works more like a preposition, so it is now defined as "unlike something, in a way that is very clear when you compare the two".
- Both reviewers noted that *in contrast* on its own, at the start of a sentence, was missing; it is now explained with *by contrast*, with an example.
- *count* (noun) was the weakest draft: its first sense described only the process of counting, while its own examples (*at the last count*, *the final count*) used the word for the resulting number. The definition now covers both, the second sense (*pollen count*, *calorie count*) is redefined as a number rather than an amount "measured by a test", and two phrase definitions were made clearer.
- Reviewer B found a real gap in *correction*: in American English, *corrections* means the prison system (*a corrections officer*). This is now a third sense. Because it was a whole new sense, I sent the entry back to both reviewers a second time; that pass caught a core idea and an explanation that did not match the new sense, which I fixed.
- Smaller fixes: weak collocations replaced (*learn cooking*, *a school cook*, *make a count*); noun-plus-noun pairs moved out of the adjective list (*traffic cop*, *home cooking*); a movie-costume example moved out of the "traditional clothes" sense; in *counter*, the counting-device sense now comes before the British game-piece sense; a doubtful claim about American use of *corridor* versus *hallway* was dropped.

Rejected: a request to replace bold marks on a phrase named as words (the style guide allows it); a request to define *creation* as "the act of making", a padding formula the guide bans; a request to move a subsense's capital-letter note out of its explanation (the guide puts such form restrictions there); and a claim that "often before another noun" is not a grammar code (it is on the closed list).

**Cross-references.** The tool could not decide which sense of *difference*, *comparison*, and *officer* should point back to the new entries, so I placed those three links by hand. *countryside* now lists *country* as a synonym, matching what *country* already says.

## Cycle 2: lint

**What the cycle did.** After cycle 1 merged, the mode selector forced a *lint* pass, because ten runs had passed since the last one. A lint pass is the dictionary's housekeeping: mechanical checks over every entry, plus a reading of the project's own notes for contradictions. It changes no definitions.

- **Caps, links, and gates.** Every file is inside its word or line limit, every relative link in the wiki resolves, and all 3,778 entries pass the validator's publishing gate.
- **Cross-references.** When one entry names another as a synonym or a word to compare, the other entry should point back. The cross-reference tool added six such back-links automatically (in *cereal*, *chart*, *chewing gum*, *classmate*, *cook*, and *cop*). In ten more cases it could not tell which sense of the other entry should carry the link, so I placed them by hand: for example, *counter* (sense 1, the shop counter) now points back to *bar* and *desk*, and *challenge* (sense 1, a difficult task) back to *test*.
- **Claims.** Seven old claim files (records of which run is drafting which word) from finished runs were removed.
- **Bands.** Forty-five queue rows had no frequency band yet (words queued by tools since the last vote). The three-model word-list panel voted on them for US$0.007; no word had votes more than one band apart, so all 45 now have a band and can be built in order.
- **Reports.** The checks that list possible problems for later review found 328 definitions that use their own headword (most are harmless, such as *walk* in a sense of *walk*), 958 cross-reference pairs where a sense may be missing, and 6 notes that promise an entry that does not exist; all six are known from earlier passes.
- **Index and notes.** The wiki index matches its pages, and the curator file has no duplicate lines.

**The one real change: a reviewer switched off for pronunciation.** Each lint pass measures each reviewer's *precision* by kind of issue: of the issues it raised that I or earlier sessions decided, the share that turned out to be right. The house rule is that a reviewer whose precision for one kind of issue falls under 30 percent, over at least twenty decisions, stops being asked about that kind. Reviewer B (the Google model) on pronunciation stood at 0.38 at the last lint. It is now 0.26: 11 right out of 42. Almost all its recent wrong objections were to the house way of writing IPA (the optional dots between syllables, how the *sh* sound in *butcher* is written, whether *form* and *warm* rhyme), each one against a transcription that four panel votes and the open CMU pronouncing data had already confirmed. I switched the pair off in `tools/review_panel.py` and recorded the figures in `wiki/notes/reviewer-precision.md`.

This means neither reviewer now comments on pronunciation: reviewer A's pronunciation flags were switched off by the owner's ruling of 2026-10-05, made when reviewer A was a different model. Every transcription is still checked by the separate pronunciation panel plus CMU, which is the check that verified all 24 transcriptions in cycle 1.

To make room in the wiki (it was within eight words of its 30,000-word cap), I condensed forty old entries in the precision notes, all about reviewer models that were replaced on 2026-10-25, into one paragraph. Their full text stays in the git history. The wiki now stands at 27,262 words.

## Cycle 3: review

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector chose *review*: a fresh reading of entries that are already published. There were no draft entries and no reader reports, so the work came from the *coverage pass* the owner set on 2026-10-24 (ruling B1). That pass rereads the most important band-1 content words, the entries with the most senses first, and asks whether anything a learner needs is missing. This cycle read five six-sense entries: *collect* (verb), *charge* (verb), *case*, *break*, and *box* (nouns). That brings the pass to 203 entries. I read each entry myself and sent it to both reviewer models. Because the fixes added new senses and subsenses, I sent four of the five back for a second round so that no new text went unreviewed.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Over the two rounds they raised 90 issues, 7 of them blocking. I agreed with 82 and rejected 8, each with a logged reason. None needed to go to the owner.

The most useful finding was a gap. *case* had no sense for a container (*a pencil case*, *a case of wine*, *a phone case*), one of its commonest uses. The container *case* comes from a different word in its history than the *case* meaning "a situation", so by house rules it is a separate entry, `case-n-2`, which does not exist yet. Both *case* and *box* now point to it, and `NEXT.md` asks a later run to build it.

Other added coverage:
- *collect*: "receive money or a prize" (*collect the rent*, *collect her prize*) is now its own sense. Before, it was squeezed into the sense for collecting money for a good cause.
- *charge*: a formal subsense, *Critics charged that ...* (to say publicly that someone did wrong), and the use with a direct object, *The bull charged the farmer*. The "charge it to my room" sense became a subsense of the money sense.
- *case*: the phrase *in the case of* (*In the case of children, the ticket is free*).
- *break*: *a tough break* (bad luck), *a tax break* as its own sense, and a subsense for a pause in something going on (*a break in the rain*).

Errors fixed:
- Two blocking examples in *collect* didn't fit their definition (*collect my thoughts* is about putting thoughts in order, not calming down), so the definition was widened.
- *charge* defined a criminal charge as saying someone "has done a crime". That models a learner error, since the natural verb is *commit*, so it now says *committed*.
- Two definitions were circular (a "numbered box" in *box*, "broken" in *break*).
- *break* had a learner error whose "correct" version (*a break of ten minutes*) was itself unidiomatic. It now reads *a ten-minute break*.

Much of the rest was the notes rule of one fact in one field. In *case*, the difference between *in case* and *if* was stated four ways in four fields. In *charge*, "no *for* before the amount" was stated three times. Each now lives in one place, and two generic translation notes ("translate each sense separately") were removed.

Rejected: requests to label the "go and get" sense of *collect* as British (Americans also *collect their luggage*; the usage note covers the British use with people); a request to remove "Always **the box**" (the grammar field cannot show the article); and a reviewer's objection to the *break* translation note, which names three specific senses.

**What did not work.** My own mistake: the small script I used to apply this cycle's fixes reused a helper from cycle 1, and loading it re-ran cycle 1's fixes on those twelve new entries. That would have duplicated an example, a phrase, and a sense. I caught it before committing and restored all twelve files from the merged version. Nothing reached the repository.

## Cycle 4: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again and allowed twelve entries. I took six: *chip*, *clip*, *coach*, *consume*, and *interview* (all verbs) and *champion* (noun), the next rows in queue order. The cap is a ceiling, not a target. This was the fourth cycle of a long run, and the house rules put full entries ahead of numbers and warn against running short of room during the merge. All six went through the full pipeline described in cycle 1 and are published as `reviewed`. All verb forms (*chipped*, *clipping*, and so on) were generated from the spelling rules, and all twelve transcriptions were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and CMU data.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 33 issues, 8 of them blocking. I agreed with 28 and rejected 5, none escalated.

- *champion*: I had used *He trains champion horses* as an example of the noun. A reviewer pointed out that *champion* there works like an adjective meaning "prize-winning", so the example did not show the noun's definition. It was replaced with *Last year's champion is playing again this year*.
- *consume*: an example said people who eat a lot of sugar are more likely to have bad teeth. That is a health claim, and the example rules forbid checkable facts, so it now says *We consumed far too much cake at the party*. A reviewer also noted the common modern use *consume news* or *consume media*, now a subsense. The "literary" label on *The fire consumed the house* was removed, since that is ordinary news English.
- *clip*: two examples did not fit "hit the edge of something while moving past it" (*clipped his head on the low door*; a ball that "fell over" the net). They were replaced.
- *chip*: golf players *chip onto the green* with no object, so that sense now allows both uses. A pet's microchip identifies the animal and its owner, which my definition had wrongly said lets it be "named". *chip away at* now points to a phrasal-verb entry, queued for later.
- *coach*: the second sense now covers workplace coaching (*coach its new managers*), not only private lessons and test preparation.

Rejected: three objections that *usually passive* is not a verb pattern (it is on the dictionary's closed list of patterns, a point the house rules already settle); a request to add *clip someone's wings* here (by the keyword rule it belongs under *wing*); and a request for a pattern for bare intransitive use, which the closed list does not have (the transitivity field shows it).

There are two words spelled *clip* with unrelated histories. This entry is the cutting verb; the fastening verb (*clip the papers together*) is a separate entry, `clip-v-2`, which is not built yet and is noted in `NEXT.md`. Five cross-reference links the tool could not place were placed by hand (for example, *trim* now lists *clip* as a synonym).
