## Cycle 1: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from what is most overdue) chose *review*: a second reading of entries that already exist. There were no draft entries, no open entry reports from readers, and nothing in the owner's inbox, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That pass re-reads the most important words (band 1, the first of five editorial frequency groups) to check that every current sense, phrase, and grammar point a learner needs is there. The four words read were *home* (adverb), *hour* (noun), *hurt* (verb), and *idea* (noun). The pass has now read 417 entries since the owner's rulings of 2026-10-24.

Before the start, the run checked for unfinished work left by earlier runs. There were no open pull requests. Four old branches remain on GitHub, but each has already been fully merged and is already listed in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` for deletion, because this run's tools cannot delete branches.

**What was added.** The reading found real gaps in all four entries.

- *home* (adverb) gained a sense for races and sport (*Her horse came home in first place*; in baseball, running home to score), a subsense for *press home an advantage*, and the common *back home* (meaning in the country you come from). A usage-note sentence that said the same thing as the explanation and both learner errors (no *to* before *home* after *go*) was removed, because the house rule is one fact, one field.
- *hour* gained three subsenses: *for hours* (many hours, or what feels like a long time), the twenty-four-hour clock (*at 0600 hours*, labelled formal and military), and a singular period of the day used for something (*lunch hour*, *rush hour*, *happy hour*). It also gained the phrases *by the hour* (paid for each hour), *of the hour* (*the hero of the hour*), and *till all hours*. The fact that the **h** is silent had been written in five places; it now lives in the pronunciation note and one learner error.
- *hurt* gained an informal sense, *be hurting* (to be in difficulty, often for lack of money; American *be hurting for* something), and an intransitive subsense for emotional pain (*She's still hurting after the breakup*). A collocation line that repeated the phrase *it won't hurt to do something* was removed.
- *idea* gained a subsense for *have an idea that* (a feeling that something is true), the phrases *not my idea of fun* and *what's the big idea?*, and a real learner error: *in my idea* for *in my opinion*.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** Two automatic reviewers from different labs (reviewer A, a Claude model, and reviewer B, a Gemini model) read every field. They raised 54 issues, 3 of them blocking (must be settled before publication). All three were right and were applied. The new *for hours* subsense had an explanation saying the phrase means "longer than it should", which its own example (*Hours later, they were still talking*) contradicted; the explanation and an *informal* label were removed. On *hurt*, an old learner error said that *hurt* always needs an object (*I fell and hurt myself*, never *I fell and I hurt*), but the entry's own sense 2 shows *I hurt all over*; the error was removed. Of the minor issues, 44 in all were applied and 10 rejected. Useful catches: *far from home* uses the noun, not the adverb; *finishing line* is British where American says *finish line*; the emotional *it hurts* did not fit a transitive sense, so it moved to its own subsense; *concept* is a synonym of the belief sense of *idea*, not of the knowledge sense. Rejections included a request to move *nothing to write home about* here (by the keyword rule it belongs under *write*, where it already is) and a request to mark named phrases with single rather than double asterisks (a phrase named as words takes the double mark).

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle did.** After cycle 1 merged, the selector chose *build*: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). It allowed up to twelve. The cycle took eight, so that each could be written in full: *wardrobe*, *warmth*, *welcome* (noun), *welfare*, *while* (noun), *wildlife*, *workplace*, and *workshop*. All are band 2, meaning common words that most intermediate learners know or will need soon. The dictionary now has 4,741 entries, all reviewed.

**What the entries hold.**
- *wardrobe*: the piece of furniture, all the clothes a person owns (*a summer wardrobe*), and the department of a theater or film company that looks after costumes. A note tells American readers that clothes are usually kept in a *closet* built into the wall.
- *warmth*: pleasant heat (with a subsense for what clothes give: *Wool socks are best for warmth*), friendliness (*the warmth of their welcome*), and a pleasant quality in a color or sound.
- *welcome* (noun): a greeting (*a warm welcome*, *a hero's welcome*), how people react to a new plan, a short speech at the start of an event, and the idiom *overstay your welcome* with its British and American alternatives.
- *welfare*: health and happiness (*animal welfare*), and government help, with the American *on welfare* (British *on benefits*).
- *while* (noun): *a while*, *quite a while*, and four idioms: *once in a while*, *all the while*, *worth someone's while*, *make it worth someone's while*.
- *wildlife*, *workplace* (including *the workplace* meaning working life in general), and *workshop* (a place with tools; a practical class).

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** The two reviewers raised 36 issues. One was blocking: the definition of *worth someone's while* used the word *worth* itself (a circular definition) and wrongly added *enjoyable*. It was rewritten as *useful enough to make the time or effort spent on it a good idea*. Of all 36 issues, 31 were applied and 5 rejected. Applied catches included *a built-in wardrobe*, a British use that contradicted the entry's own note on closets; *a car workshop*, which American English would call a repair shop; a definition of warmth that used the word *warm*; and general *the workplace* examples sitting under the countable sense instead of the subsense for working life. Rejections included a request to spell the domain label *theater*: the label comes from a closed list whose value is *theatre*, and only a logged decision can change it. Another rejected request asked to move a regional form of an idiom out of its explanation, which is where the house puts it.

Three cross-references needed a matching link back from the other entry, and the tool could not tell which sense to put it on. Those three were placed by hand: *cold* (low temperature) now names *warmth* as an opposite, *greeting* compares with *welcome*, and *office* compares with *workplace*.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The regular checking pass (lint) is due after one more cycle. The queue continues after *workshop*.
