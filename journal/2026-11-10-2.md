# Run report, 2026-11-10 (run 20261110T084342Z-c69i98)

## Cycle 1: closure

**What this cycle did.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's job from whichever kind of work is most overdue) chose *closure*: writing entries for words the dictionary already points to but does not yet define. Its reason was "highest scheduler debt", meaning closure had gone longest without a turn relative to how often it should run. Closure draws from three lists in turn. The first, words used inside definitions and notes, is empty for now. So this cycle took twelve words from the second list, *family* rows: words named in an existing entry's word-family box (for example, *insult* the verb lists *insult* the noun) that had no entry of their own.

The twelve new entries, all nouns: *instructor*, *insult*, *insurance*, *introduction*, *investigator*, *investment*, *invitation*, *jump*, *kick*, *killer*, *killing*, and *kingdom*. Several carry idioms filed under them by the house rule that an idiom lives under its first noun: *add insult to injury*, *need no introduction*, *get a jump on something*, *get a kick out of something*, *for kicks*, *a kick in the teeth*, *make a killing*, and *until kingdom come*. Every entry's pronunciation, in American and British forms, passed the automatic check on the first try: other models agreed with it, and for American English the open pronouncing dictionary agreed as well.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** Two reviewer models read every field of every entry. They raised 59 issues, 13 of them *blocking* (an entry cannot be published until a blocking issue is fixed or rejected with a reason). I applied 56 and rejected 3.

Nine of the blocking issues were on *killer*. My draft had a sense for "something very good" illustrated with *a killer smile* and *a killer idea*. The reviewer pointed out that in those phrases *killer* works like an adjective in front of a noun, so they belong in a separate adjective entry, not a noun one. That is right under the house rule that an entry holds only its own part of speech. The noun entry now keeps only true noun uses (*Her new song is a killer*), and *killer* as an adjective has been added to the queue of words to write, with a pointer from the noun. The reviewer also judged "a thing that kills" too close to the headword, so the first definition now reads "causes death".

The other blocking issues: two example sentences in *kingdom* stated scientific classifications (*Humans are part of the animal kingdom*), which breaks the rule against checkable facts in examples, so they were replaced with ordinary sentences about a biology class and a museum; one *insurance* definition used the word *insurance* itself; and one *introduction* example showed a thing that had been introduced rather than the act of introducing it.

Among the minor issues, the reviewers noticed gaps that I filled: *jump* now has a sense for a startled movement (*She gave a jump when the phone rang*), *kick* has one for a short burst of enthusiasm (*on a health kick*), and *introduction* gives a beginner's book or course its own sense. *Killing* no longer says "on purpose", because a killing can be accidental. The three rejections: keeping a separate *invitation* sense for a formal request to do something (*an invitation to join the team*), which differs in grammar and often in translation from an invitation to a party; and not adding a "humorous" label to *The kitchen is my father's kingdom*, which is figurative but not a joke.

Four definitions used words outside the dictionary's controlled defining vocabulary that have no entry yet (*professor*, *remark*, *property*, *occasion*); I reworded each in plainer words. Two cross-references needed a hand: *coach* now points back to *instructor*, and *instructor* lists *teacher* under "compare" to match how *teacher* already lists it.

**Spend.** About US$0.41 for this cycle, almost all on the second reviewer and the pronunciation panel. The day's total is US$1.20 of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. The one lesson is a drafting one: an attributive use of a noun (*killer app*) should be spotted before review and sent to its own adjective entry.

**What is next.** Further family rows in queue order; *killer* as an adjective waits for its band vote. The next maintenance pass (lint) is due in three more cycles.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. The older items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" still stand.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** After the first cycle's pull request was merged, the selector chose *build* (writing new entries from the main queue, the ordered list of words still to be written), again because that job had gone longest without a turn. I took the next twelve words in queue order, all nouns: *instance*, *instant*, *investigation*, *jam*, *jar*, *jaw*, *journal*, *ketchup*, *kettle*, *kiss*, *kit*, and *kitten*.

Several of these carry common idioms, filed under the first noun in each phrase: *for instance*; *in an instant* and *this instant*; *someone's jaw drops*; *a different kettle of fish*; *blow someone a kiss* and *the kiss of death*; *the whole kit and caboodle*; and *have kittens*, marked British, meaning to get very worried or upset. The baton file (`NEXT.md`, the running to-do list passed from run to run) had asked for *blow someone a kiss* to be added when *kiss* was built, and that is now done. *Ketchup* records the less common American spelling *catsup* as a variant. *Jam* has five senses: the fruit spread, a traffic jam, a paper jam in a machine, being "in a jam" (in trouble), and an informal music session. All 24 pronunciations passed the automatic check on the first try.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** The two reviewer models raised 34 issues, 5 of them blocking. I applied 31 and rejected 2.

The blocking issues: *Do you mind instant?* is not how people offer instant coffee, so it now reads *Is instant OK?*. *Instant coffee* had been listed as a collocation of the noun *instant*, but in that phrase *instant* is an adjective. It was replaced with true noun uses (*a cup of instant*). The third was *journal*, which I had defined as including "a newspaper". Outside a few newspapers' names, no one calls a newspaper a journal, so that was removed.

The useful minor finds: *in the first instance* is mainly British and now says so; two examples had British wording (*the shop*, *cheating in the exam*) that was changed to the dictionary's American base (*the store*, *on the exam*); the "amount" use of *jar* (*a whole jar of olives*) was folded into the main sense as a sub-sense, since it is a regular extension; *jaw* gained the literary phrase *the jaws of something* (*snatched victory from the jaws of defeat*). The kitten idiom's example had used *Mom*, an American word, under a British label; it now says *my mother*.

The two rejections: a reviewer wanted the academic-magazine sense of *journal* listed first, but keeping a journal (a diary) is at least as common for learners, so the order stays; and a reviewer wanted *the kiss of life* labeled informal, but it is the ordinary British term.

Three definitions used words with no entry yet (*tomatoes*, *burgers*, *spout*). They were reworded in plain words; *tomato* still appears in the collocation *tomato ketchup*. One cross-reference, from *moment* back to *instant*, had to be placed in the right sense by hand.

**Spend.** About US$0.36 this cycle; the day's total is US$1.56 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. The same kind of mistake as the first cycle's *killer smile* came up again with *instant coffee*: an adjective use was filed under a noun. It is worth a closer look while drafting.

**What is next.** More cycles may follow in this run. Lint (the periodic maintenance pass) is due in two more cycles.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: rereading existing entries to make them fuller and more accurate. There were no unfinished drafts and no open entry reports (public issues saying an entry is wrong), so the cycle continued the owner's *coverage pass*. That pass rereads the most important everyday words, those with the most senses first, and asks of each one: is any common meaning, idiom, or use missing? Before this cycle it had covered 271 entries. This cycle took the next four entries with five senses each: the verb *manage* and the nouns *market*, *match*, and *material*.

**What I added before the reviewers saw them.** *Manage* gained three sub-senses (narrower uses filed under a main sense): managing a singer's or a player's career; the ironic use for an unlucky result (*He managed to lose his keys again*); and managing an illness or pain. Its usage note said the same thing as one of its learner-error notes, which breaks the rule that each fact lives in one field, so I folded the two together. *Market* gained the stock market (*the market fell this morning*) and a new sense for the people or places a company sells to (*overseas markets*). *Match* gained the phrase *a shouting match*. *Material* now covers *reading material*.

I also found a false statement. The *match* entry's usage note said that *match* meaning a small stick for making a flame "has its own entry". It does not: that word, a separate homograph (same spelling, unrelated origin), has never been written. The note is gone. In its place is a cross-reference to the future entry `match-n-2`, which the site shows in grey until the entry exists, and `NEXT.md` now asks a later run to write it.

**What the reviewers found and what was decided.** The two reviewer models raised 45 issues, 4 of them blocking. I applied 39 and rejected 6.

The blocking issues were real. The fifth sense of *manage* mixed three different uses (carrying a box, eating more, being free for lunch) under one vague definition. It now covers handling a task or a load without help. *I don't know how she manages it* had been filed under "succeed in doing something difficult", but it means "cope", so it was replaced with *She finally managed to pass her driving test*. My new *shouting match* sub-sense had glossed *match* alone as "an argument or fight", which a learner might copy into *we had a match*; it became a fixed phrase instead.

Both reviewers objected to the general translation notes on *market* and *match*. These said only that the senses "may be different words in other languages", which the style guide calls too generic to help a translator. Both notes were removed. Two synonym links were dropped as inaccurate, each together with its mirror link on the other entry: *control* is not really a synonym of *manage* a business, and *equipment* is not a synonym of *materials*. The *material* entry's own note even warns that the two are confused. The rejections were mostly about formatting conventions the house rules already settle, such as how a word named in a note is marked.

**Spend.** About US$0.20 this cycle; the day's total is US$1.77 of US$15.

The gate then caught one definition word outside the defining vocabulary (*stock*), which I fixed. The metrics line was written once, as the rules require, but before that last fix.
