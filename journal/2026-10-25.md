## Cycle 1: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a review: rereading existing entries and fixing them, rather than writing new ones. The top of the review list is still the appendix of errors you attached to your rulings of 2026-10-24 (ruling B3). Twelve entries went through the full pipeline: the automatic checks, both outside reviewers, my decision on every point they raised, and the checks again.

The changes, entry by entry:

- **fifty** (number): the money sense now reads *a piece of paper money with a value of 50*, like *twenty*. **ten** and **eleven** no longer list each other as family words, since they share no form; each now points to the other under "see also". **ten** also gained an example of *a ten* as a ten-dollar bill.
- **manner**: the example *Dr. Lee has a wonderful bedside manner* used a list name as a surname; it now reads *Our new doctor has a wonderful bedside manner*. The fact that *manners* (polite behavior) is always plural was stated in four places; it now sits in the grammar code, the learner error, and the translator's note only.
- **proud**: sense 4 (*a proud moment*) was defined as *making you feel proud*, which defines the word with itself. It is now *giving you a strong feeling of pleasure because of something good that you or people close to you have done*. The translator's note now names the real trap: sense 1 praises, sense 2 criticizes, and many languages use different words.
- **light** (adjective): sense 2 was defined with the noun *light* in a way that explained nothing; it now says *having a lot of light from the sun or from lamps, so that it is easy to see; not dark*. You noted it had no phrases. Under the house keyword rule (an idiom goes under its first noun, else its first verb), *make light of something* belongs under **make**, the same way *fall flat* sits under **fall**, so I added it there and pointed to it from **light**. Every other idiom the reviewers proposed (*travel light*, *light years*) also has a different keyword.
- **dull**: sense 4 (*a dull ache*) was wrongly coded "before a noun only"; *the pain was dull* is normal, so the code is gone. A new sense 6, *slow to learn or understand things*, matches the *dull* that **sharp** already lists as its opposite. I first labeled it old-fashioned; a reviewer said it is current but insulting, and I agreed, so it is labeled "disapproving". **slow**, **stupid** and **clever** now link back to it; I placed those links by hand, because the linking tool no longer guesses which sense is meant.
- **Invented learner errors removed** from **transport** (*We transported to a new city*), **recent** (*I recent moved*), **well** (*weller*) and **sign** (*no signal of him*). The real errors in those entries stay.

**What the reviewers found.** They raised 44 issues, 33 of them blocking (meaning the entry should not stand as it is). I accepted 15 and rejected 29. The accepted ones were mostly about notes: three translator's notes that only said "translate each sense separately" were rewritten to name a specific trap, and repeated facts in **manner** and **well** were cut so each lives in one field. The rejected ones fell into three groups the house rules already settle: one reviewer wanted the money and age uses of **fifty** moved to a separate noun entry (number entries hold them by rule); one asked for phrasal verbs (*make up*, *make out*) as senses of **make** (phrasal verbs are separate entries); and several asked for idioms whose keyword is another word (*do someone proud* goes under **do**). I also turned down a "business was dull" sense as too specialized for now.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose to build: write new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The queue still gives milestone 1 first: the words of the defining vocabulary, the roughly 2,400 plain words the dictionary's definitions are written in, which you ruled must all have entries before anything else. Twelve verbs came up, all in band 2 (the second most important of five frequency bands): **chase**, **clap**, **conclude**, **dissolve**, **extend**, **frighten**, **heal**, **leak**, **organize**, **quote**, **roast**, and **rob**. Each was drafted from scratch with every field filled, then run through the full pipeline.

Some points a learner will notice: **organize** records the British spelling *organise*; **quote** has the spoken phrase *quote, unquote*; **frighten** has a short note on choosing between *scare*, *frighten*, and *terrify*; **conclude**, **dissolve**, and **rob** each carry one learner error that learners really make (*concluded to cancel*, *the ice dissolved*, *robbed my phone*). **leak** and **heal** say in a separate explanation what the subject of each sense is (the pipe or the water; the wound), because the house style keeps such restrictions out of the definition itself.

**Checks.** All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each verb) passed the agreement check, and every past tense and *-ing* form came from the spelling rules (*clapped*, *robbed*).

**What the reviewers found.** I sent the entries to the two reviewers in three groups of four, which keeps their replies complete. They raised 35 issues, 23 of them blocking, and I accepted 23 and rejected 12. The accepted ones were real improvements. The first definitions of **heal** and **leak** were clumsy about what heals and what leaks, and both were rewritten. **Roast** had said roasting usually uses oil, which is not true; it now says *dry heat*. The "offer" sense of **extend** now covers *extend a loan* and *extend credit*, so its example about a bank fits. Several pattern codes were wrong: *organize* does not take a to-infinitive in *the workers organized to demand better pay*; that is an infinitive of purpose. Most of the rejections were requests to treat phrasal verbs (*chase down*, *heal over*, *leak out*) as senses; the house rule makes those separate entries, and I added *chase up* to the queue. I kept one learner error in **frighten** (*I'm frightening of the dark*) that a reviewer wanted moved to the adjective entries, because those entries do not exist yet.

**Links.** The linking tool, which writes the matching link back when one entry points to another, no longer guesses which sense of the other entry is meant. It left seven links for me, and I placed them by hand: **follow**, **decide**, **end**, **begin**, **start**, **disappear**, and **order** now point back to the new verbs.
