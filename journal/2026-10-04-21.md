# 2026-10-04 (21): twelve words the dictionary already uses, *apple* to *block*

*First cycle of the run that started at 20:44 UTC on 4 October 2026.*

## What this cycle did

The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose **closure**: writing entries for words that the dictionary already uses in its definitions or names in its word-family boxes but that have no entry yet.

The first source of closure words (words found in definitions) gave ten rows, but several were not real headwords as queued. The tool that finds them guesses the part of speech and sometimes takes a plural for a lemma:

- *according* had been queued as a verb; the real word is the preposition *according to*, which I drafted instead.
- *settings* and *visitors* are plurals; I marked them duplicate and drafted *setting* and *visitor*.
- *Christian*, *Islam*, and *Judaism* cannot be defined accurately without a person's name or a holy book's name, and the dictionary's definition check does not yet allow proper names (open question 7 for the owner). Following the working assumption recorded there, I left them in the queue and skipped them.

That left seven words: *apple, banana, coal, tube, according to, setting, visitor*. I filled the cycle from the second source (words named in word-family boxes) with five verbs: *bicycle, bike, bill, blanket, block*. All twelve went through the full pipeline (format check, word forms, pronunciation check, review by two outside language models, my reading of every objection, a check of the words used in definitions, a check of links) and are marked `reviewed`. The dictionary now has 1,312 entries.

The largest new entry is the verb *block*, with five senses (stopping things from getting through, with a sub-sense for standing in someone's way; blocking a view or light; stopping a plan; blocking in sport; blocking a caller or website on a phone) and a short note on choosing between *block*, *prevent*, and *stop*. *Setting* has five senses, from the place where something happens to the position of a control on an oven or phone. *According to* has three (as said by; following a plan; depending on) and three learner errors, including *according to me*, which English speakers do not say.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 35 points, 27 marked blocking. I accepted 24 and rejected 11; each reason is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. The useful objections:

- Three of my "learner errors" were not errors. *The ground was blanketed by snow* is grammatical, only less common than *in snow*; *bill the repairs to me* is standard; *I go to school by biking* is unusual but not wrong. All three were removed or replaced (the *bike* entry now warns against *I ride bike to school*, without *my*).
- *Go bananas* had been defined with *crazy*, which can suggest illness; it now reads *become very excited or angry*.
- *Visitor*'s summary sentence said a visitor does not belong to the place, which is not always true; it now just says someone who comes for a short time.
- The *block* definitions were tightened: *way in* was awkward, and the sport sense was too narrow.

The main rejection: reviewer-a said *bike* cannot mean carrying something by bike (*I'll bike the papers over to your office*). The open dictionary Wiktionary, checked at run time, lists a transitive sense "to transport by bicycle", so the sense stays; but I dropped my "British" label, since nothing I could check supports it. Other rejections were the usual house rules: the American transcription of *tube* has no length mark, a tree that *blocks the road* is a transitive use, and *go biking* is a real collocation.

## Pronunciation

The panel agreed with eleven of the twelve. *According to* came back "disputed", but when I looked at the panel's answers, two of the three gave exactly my sounds; they only wrote the gap between the two words as a period instead of a space. The check counts that as a difference. The entry carries the automatic flag, the owner's queue (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`) has a line about it, and a new note, `wiki/notes/pronounce-check-word-break.md`, proposes the fix for a later run.

## Links and spend

Four fit; the one from *blanket* to *cover* landed on *cover* in the sense "put something over to hide it", and I moved it to "lie or spread over the surface", which is what snow does.
