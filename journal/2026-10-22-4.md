## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 35 issues, almost all from reviewer-a; seven entries drew none. I made 35 decisions: 14 applied and 21 rejected.

Applied, among others:

- *sister*: the first sense said *a girl or woman who has the same parents as you*, while its own explanation discussed half-sisters. It now says *the same parents as you, or one of the same parents*, the summary sentence matches, and the explanation adds that people often just say *sister*. The British hospital sense now says *an experienced nurse with a high position, often one who is in charge of a part of a hospital*.
- *sign*: the star-sign sense called a sign *one of the twelve parts of the year*; it now says *one of twelve names given to periods of the year*.
- *silk*: *thin* was dropped from the cloth sense, and the grammar note on the plural now says that **silks** can mean kinds of silk or silk clothes.
- *smell*: *have a smell of this milk* is mainly British; that sense now carries a British label.
- *soap*: the note *Short for soap opera* is a claim about where the word comes from, which a house rule keeps only in the etymology field. It now says *The full form is soap opera*.
- *situation* and *slice*: two notes for translators were made less absolute.

Rejected, among others:

- Four objections that *go to sleep*, *get to sleep*, *put something to sleep*, and *lose sleep over something* belong under the verbs. The house rule puts an idiom under its first noun, which here is *sleep*.
- Merging the two senses of *sky*. *Clear skies* is plural and about the weather; the splitting rule allows a new sense for different grammar.
- Several objections that ordinary nouns should not have an explanation field. The style guide asks for a grammar exception (*a heavy snow*, *soaps* for kinds of soap) to be stated in words.
- The claim that *scale* is not a synonym of *size* (the *scale* entry's first sense is *the size or level of something*), and that *sister school* makes *sister* an adjective (the entry already records that the noun is often used before another noun).
