# Run report, 2026-10-29 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from how long each kind has waited) chose **build**: writing new entries from the queue of headwords still to be written. Its reason was that build had the highest "debt", meaning it had waited longest relative to its share of the work. The defining vocabulary (milestone 1) is finished, so the queue offers band-2 words; band 2 is the second most important of five editorial frequency bands. The twelve rows at the top were all verbs that other entries already point to ("crossref" rows): *accuse*, *address*, *adjust*, *admit*, *advise*, *aid*, *announce*, *applaud*, *assist*, *attach*, *attempt*, and *attract*. Several fill obvious gaps: the dictionary had the noun *address*, *advice*, and *attempt* but not the verbs, and entries such as *help*, *try*, *charge*, and *clap* already named these verbs as synonyms.

All twelve went through the full pipeline: schema check, inflection check, the pronunciation panel, both reviewers, my decision on every issue, a second schema check, the defining-vocabulary lint (which checks that definitions use only the plain words of the defining list or words that have entries), and the cross-reference tool. All twelve are now `reviewed` and will appear on the site at the next build. The dictionary has 3,269 entries, none in draft.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model, and reviewer B, a Google model) raised 61 issues, 9 of them blocking. I applied 47 and rejected 14.

- **A circular definition.** The letter sense of *address* (*address an envelope*) was defined with the noun *address*. It now says "write on a letter or package the name of the person it is for and the place where it should go".
- **A definition that contradicted its own example.** I defined the "get used to" sense of *adjust* with *slowly*, then gave the example *Most children adjust quickly*. The word *slowly* is gone, and the definition now also covers the body (*your eyes adjust to the dark*).
- **Definitions with the wrong object.** In *attach importance to something*, the object of the verb is the quality (*importance*), not the thing judged; in *attract attention*, it is the reaction, not the person. Both definitions were rewritten.
- **Examples that did not fit.** *What attracted you to this job?* sat under a sense defined only for people. I widened that sense to "a person or thing" and kept the example. Two examples for the physical sense of *attract* (*A magnet attracts iron*) were science claims, which the examples policy forbids; they are now everyday scenes.
- **Added on review:** a fourth sense of *announce*, "be a sign that something is coming" (*a loud bell announced the start of the lesson*); *adjust for* (*adjusted for inflation*); the intransitive *the lens attaches to the camera*; *admit* with a *that*-clause.
- **Rejected,** with reasons logged: a request to merge the "agree something is true" and "admit a wrong act" senses of *admit* (they take different objects and are often translated differently); a request to mark *aiding* by hand in *aid and abet* (the site already marks listed forms); calls to drop *advise someone to do something* and *attempt to do something* as collocations (house practice keeps the full wording); *enclose* as a synonym of *attach* (the existing entry *enclose* already pairs them).

**Cross-references.** Three entries named one of the new verbs as a synonym while I had named that entry under "compare" (*charge*, *recommend*, *try*). I moved them to synonyms so the two sides agree. Seven back-links that the cross-reference tool could not place on a sense (it no longer guesses) were placed by hand, in *suggest*, *inform*, *praise*, *fasten*, *enclose*, *deal with*, and *alter*.

**Pronunciation and inflections.** All twenty-four transcriptions were verified by the panel and, for American English, by the open CMU pronouncing dictionary, consulted at run time and not stored. All inflections came from the rules, including the doubled consonant of *admitted*.

**Spend.** US$0.37 this cycle; US$3.22 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The definition-word lint caught five words outside the defining vocabulary in my first drafts (*envelope*, *remark*, *complaint*, *reaction*, *sexual*); each definition was reworded with plainer words.

**What is next.** The queue continues with band-2 verbs. The lint pass is due in one run.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle. The standing items are in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner".

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose **review**: a second reading of entries that are already published. No entries were in draft and no entry reports (problem reports filed by readers through the site) were open, so the cycle continued the coverage pass the owner asked for in October (ruling B1). That pass reads the most important band-1 content words, the ones with the most senses first, and asks whether a learner would find every common use. Ninety-three entries have now been read this way. This cycle read four: *sit* (verb), *short*, *rough*, and *right* (adjectives).

**What I added.** For *sit*: a sense for posing for a painter or photographer (*sit for a portrait*), and the phrase *sit well with someone* (*the new rules did not sit well with the staff*). For *short*: the phrase *nothing short of* (*nothing short of a miracle*) and the informal *short on* (*short on patience*). For *rough*: a sub-sense for things made simply and quickly (*a rough shelter*), one for people without polite manners, and a sense for an unpleasant sound (*a rough voice*). For *right*: the angle sense (*a right angle*, American *right triangle*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 53 issues, 7 of them blocking. I applied 44 and rejected 9.

- **My own mistake, caught.** I had added a *short* sense meaning "not quite reaching" (*the ball landed just short of the line*). Both reviewers pointed out that *short* there describes the verb, so it is the adverb, not the adjective. I removed it; *short* the adverb is already on the queue.
- **Names outside the house list.** Two older examples in *short* used *Samantha* and *Ravindra*; examples may use only nine fixed names. Both were replaced (*"Fridge" is short for "refrigerator"*; *Mathematics is usually called math for short*).
- **A misleading usage note.** The note on *short* said to use *thin* or *slim* for "a person who is not fat", which has nothing to do with height. That sentence is gone.
- **Notes that broke the house rules.** Three learner errors were invented rather than real (*I have short of money*, *It costs rough fifty dollars*, *He sitted*), and three adaptation notes (notes for future translators) only said "translate each sense separately" without naming a trap. All six were removed. One fact that appeared in three fields of *sit* now appears once.
- **Smaller fixes.** *Do you have the right time?* became *Is that clock right?*; an example about which side of the road people drive on (a real-world fact) was replaced; several definitions were reworded.
- **Rejected.** Requests to add idioms that the keyword rule puts under another word: *fall short* and *cut short* (under the verbs), *sit on the fence* (under *fence*), *rough around the edges* (under *edge*), and *a short temper* (under *temper*). I also rejected *sit on* meaning "delay", which is a phrasal verb and gets its own entry.

**Spend.** US$0.23 this cycle; US$3.45 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The adverb sense I drafted for *short* was a real error of mine, and the review caught it.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *raise*, *private*, *order*, and *open*. The lint pass, a full health check of the dictionary every ten runs, is now due.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: lint

**What the cycle did.** The selector forced **lint**, the health check of the whole dictionary that runs every ten runs (ten had passed). A lint pass runs every mechanical check over all 3,269 entries, settles what they report, and reads the knowledge base (the `wiki/` pages that hold the project's rules and notes) for contradictions and gaps. This was the ninety-third such pass.

**The mechanical checks.** The word caps on the project's files, the links between pages, the schema check, and the unit tests all passed. The cross-reference tool keeps two-way links between entries (if *A* lists *B* as a synonym, *B* should list *A*). It added six missing back-links on its own. It could not place nineteen others because it no longer guesses which sense of the target entry they belong to. I placed fourteen by hand; for example, *recognize* names *admit* as a synonym, so *admit* now names *recognize* on its "agree is true" sense. The other five I left alone, because the original reference points at a sense that does not exist: *also* and *too* list *well* as a synonym, but they mean the phrase *as well*, which *well* the adverb does not cover. Those five are now listed in `NEXT.md` for the cross-reference audit (the owner's ruling C1). Six spent claim files (records of which run is writing which word) were pruned.

**The band vote.** Every new queue row starts "unbanded" until three word-list models vote on its frequency band. Forty-six rows were waiting, most of them family words queued by today's builds (*accusation*, *adjustment*, *assistant*, *attachment*). The vote cost under one cent. Where the three votes spread over more than one band, I settled five by hand: *European* (adjective) and *Ms.* went to band 2 rather than band 1, as common words outside the core vocabulary. The others kept the lower median.

**A tool fix.** A known bug was marked "fix due": a second band vote on the same day overwrote the first day's file, losing its record. It happened twice before, and each time a session restored the file by hand. The vote now merges with an existing file of the same name, with new votes winning. Its file option also accepts a path from the repository root. A unit test covers the merge. The tool only records votes, never entry content.

**Reviewer precision.** Precision is the share of a reviewer's issues that I applied. Reviewer A (the Claude model) stands at 0.83 over 2,948 decisions and reviewer B (the Google model) at 0.82 over 1,202. A family of issues is switched off for a reviewer when it falls under 30 percent over twenty or more decisions. None crossed the line this time. Reviewer B's "phrase" family is at 0.22 but over only 18 decisions; it mostly proposes idioms that the keyword rule files under another word.

**The reports.** The circular-definition report lists 327 definitions that use their own headword. Most are harmless (*walk a dog* for *walk*); a review judges each. The missing-sense report lists 773 cross-reference pairs to check by hand. Six "promises" remain: usage notes that point to an entry nobody has queued, such as the British spelling *storey*. These are unchanged in kind from the last pass.

**Upkeep.** The wiki was 11 words over its 30,000-word cap after this cycle's notes, so I condensed two older sections of the reviewer-precision note.

**Spend.** US$0.007 this cycle; US$3.45 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What is next.** Review continues with the coverage pass; build continues with band-2 verbs. The next lint is due in ten runs.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose **build** again. The top twelve queue rows were a mix: four band-1 words that other entries already pointed to (*little* as a pronoun, *cent*, *September*, *sir*), the adjective *due*, two prepositions (*across from*, *aside from*), and five verbs (*advance*, *back*, *bark*, *bet*, *blame*). Band 1 is the most important of the five editorial frequency bands. Each fills a gap: *October* pointed to *September*, *dollar* to *cent*, *Mr.* to *sir*, and *blame* the noun had no verb. *Bark* the verb now pairs with the dog's *bark* (`bark-n-2`), as `NEXT.md` asked.

All twelve went through the full pipeline and are now `reviewed`. The dictionary has 3,281 entries, none in draft. All transcriptions were verified. The irregular *bet, bet, bet* came from the inflection exceptions table.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 59 issues, 17 of them blocking. I applied 50 and rejected 9.

- **A sense that was really a negative.** I gave *blame* a second sense, "think someone was right" (*I don't blame you for being angry*). Both reviewers pointed out that *blame* never means that on its own; only *not blame* does. That use is now a phrase, *not blame someone*. *Be to blame* (*nobody is to blame*), which I had tucked into an example, is now a phrase too.
- **A definition that did not fit its grammar.** *The hotel is backed by mountains* means the mountains stand behind the hotel. My definition of that sense of *back* said the opposite. It now reads "be or stand directly behind something". I dropped the British *our garden backs onto a river*, which goes the other way. The betting sense of *back* got a British label and a clearer example.
- **Examples that did not fit.** *Dear Sir or Madam* sat under a sense defined for letters to a man; it is now *Dear Sir, thank you for your letter*. An awkward *add a little of the sauce at a time* was replaced.
- **Added on review:** a sense of *advance* for moving an event earlier (*the meeting has been advanced to Tuesday*); a *bet* sub-sense for taking a risk on a plan (*the company is betting on its new phone*); *the amount due* and *the due date*.
- **One fact, one field.** The difference between *little* and *a little* appeared in four fields of the new entry. It now lives in one explanation.
- **Rejected.** A request to move *put in your two cents* to the verb *put*. The house keyword rule takes the first noun, which is *cents*. Also rejected: *in due course* (it belongs under *course*), and *due to* meaning "caused by" (the entry `due-to-prep` already covers it).

**Cross-references.** *Opposite* named *across from* under "compare", while I had named *opposite* as a synonym. I moved it to "compare", with a note that *opposite* is the usual British word, so the two sides agree.

**Spend.** US$0.38 this cycle; US$3.83 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. My first draft of *blame* was the main error of the run, and both reviewers caught it.

**What is next.** The run ends here: four cycles is the most a run may take. The next run picks its own mode. The coverage pass continues with *raise*, *private*, *order*, and *open*, and the queue has fourteen new rows waiting for the next band vote.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this run.
