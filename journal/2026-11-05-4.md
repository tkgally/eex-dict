## Cycle 1: closure

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each run's kind of work) chose *closure*: building entries for words the dictionary already uses or points to but has no entry for yet. Its reason was that closure had the most "debt", meaning it had gone longest without a turn compared with how often it should run. The `closure` source itself (words used inside definitions) is empty for bands 1 and 2, the two most important frequency bands we are allowed to build from right now, so the claim fell through to the `family` source: nouns that existing entries list as relatives in their word family. Twelve were built, the most a cycle may take: **curse**, **cycling**, **daddy**, **dancing**, **daylight**, **daytime**, **dealer**, **defeat**, **delivery**, **deposit**, **designer**, and **detective**, all nouns.

**Deposit** has four senses: money put into a bank, a first payment that holds something for you, money you get back after renting something, and an amount of a substance such as oil or coal in the ground. Many languages use different words for the second and third, so the entry carries an adaptation note (a note for future translators) saying so. **Defeat** can mean losing or beating someone, and *their defeat of the champions* means they won; that trap also has its translator note. **Daylight** carries four phrases: *in broad daylight*, *daylight robbery* (British), *scare the living daylights out of someone*, and *daylight saving time*.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** Every entry went to the two-reviewer panel (two language models from different companies, each checking every field). They raised 64 issues, 15 of them marked *blocking*, which means the entry cannot be published until I rule on them. I agreed with 57 and fixed the entries myself; I rejected 7 with a recorded reason; none needed the owner.

The most useful catches:

- **designer**: both reviewers objected that I had squeezed the use in *designer jeans* (expensive clothes from a famous fashion company) into the "person who designs things" sense through an example note. They were right: it is a different meaning and arguably a different part of speech. I removed it and queued a separate entry, `designer-adj`, linked from the noun.
- **detective**: my private-detective subsense said such a person is "paid by the public". That is wrong; they are paid by people or companies who hire them. Fixed.
- **daytime**: an example said owls sleep in the day and hunt at night. That is a checkable claim about animals, which house rules forbid in examples, and not true of every owl. Replaced. A forecast example with "thirty degrees" (a Celsius figure in an American-English entry) also went.
- **delivery**: one reviewer pointed out two common uses I had missed: *the delivery arrived* (the goods themselves) and the formal *the delivery of health care* (providing a service). I added a subsense and a fourth sense. The "way of speaking" sense was also marked both uncountable and "usually singular", which contradict each other; it is now "singular only".
- **cycling**: *mountain cycling* is not what people say (it is *mountain biking*). Removed.

The seven rejections: four repeated a reviewer's long-standing objection to the grammar code *often before another noun* on nouns, which is a valid code in our closed list; two asked for extra **daylight** senses (dawn, *see daylight*) that are marginal at this level; one objected to *the person who gives out the cards* in **dealer**, but a card game has one dealer, so *the* is right.

Three new cross-references could not be mirrored automatically because the tool could not tell which sense of the other entry they belong to; I placed them by hand (*blessing* as the opposite of *curse* in its "cause of trouble" sense, *dancing* compared with *dance*, *defeat* as a synonym of the "losing a game" sense of *loss*). All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and the open CMU dictionary; all plurals came from the inflection rules.

## Cycle 2: originality

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler forced an *originality check*, which it does every twentieth run (this was run 700). The check asks one question: does any of our wording look copied from a published dictionary? The project never consults commercial dictionaries while drafting, and this periodic test is how we catch any accidental resemblance.

A script (`tools/originality_check.py`) picked ten prose fields at random from the whole dictionary: the phrase definition *suddenly go completely wrong* under **crash** (verb); *a sudden loud noise, like the noise of something exploding or hitting something hard* (**bang**); *an activity or a time that is fun* (**laugh**, noun); the definition of *write your own ticket* under **ticket**; *if something happens* (the phrase *in the event of* under **event**); *make a word or a grammar pattern from its parts* (**form**, verb); *the thin threads that grow on the body of a person or an animal* (**hair**); *catch, hit, or punish someone* (**get**); *the end of something such as a plan, a business, or a way of life* (**death**); and the usage note on **murder**.

For each, I ran an exact-phrase web search and looked only at whether a dictionary or word list shows the same wording. I also put all ten to reviewer B (the Google model on our two-reviewer panel) in a single call, asking whether each reads as copied and requiring it to quote the source if it says yes.

**What was found.** No search turned up a published match. Two fields are what the checklist calls *generic overlap*: short, plain definitions that any dictionary would write in much the same way. Two dictionaries give *if (something) happens* for *in the event of*, and our *if something happens* is the same obvious wording. Dictionaries describe hair as *threadlike strands growing from the skin*, the same idea as our *thin threads* in different words. Neither needs rewriting; the style guide says short plain definitions will resemble others by necessity. The other eight are *original*. Reviewer B answered *original* for nine and *cannot tell* for **event**, and named no source, so the searches decided every verdict. The completed checklist is in `reviews/originality/2026-11-05.md`.

**Spend.** Half a cent for the reviewer call; US$3.46 of the US$15 daily cap used so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing. Many search results were spam or aggregator pages, which is normal for exact-phrase searches and does not affect a verdict.

**What is next.** The scheduler picks the next cycle's work. Lint is due in six runs and the weekly digest on 2026-11-07.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The scheduler chose *build*: new entries taken in order from the main queue of headwords waiting to be written. I took six of the twelve allowed: **currency**, **cushion**, **customs**, **cycle**, **cyclist**, and **dairy**, all nouns. Six, not twelve, because this was the third cycle of a long run, and the rule is that a later cycle's entries must be as full as the first's; fewer entries done well beat more done thinly.

A few points of interest. **Customs** looks like the plural of **custom** but is a separate word for the border department and the place where bags are checked; it takes a singular or a plural verb (*Customs has* or *Customs have*), and learners do write *go through custom*, so that error is recorded. **Dairy** carries a learner error for the real spelling slip *dairy* for *diary*. **Cycle** has three senses: a series of events that repeat (*the life cycle*), one run of a washing machine or dishwasher, and (mainly British) a bicycle, as in *cycle path*. **Currency** has a formal second sense, *the idea gained currency*, meaning it became widely accepted.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 30 issues, 2 of them blocking. I applied 25 and rejected 5 with reasons; none needed the owner.

- **cycle**: I had defined the British sense as "a bicycle or a motorcycle". A reviewer pointed out that a *cycle path* or *cycle lane* is for bicycles and every example showed only bicycles. Fixed to "a bicycle". The reviewer also doubted my label calling *a vicious cycle* American; it is common in both varieties, so the label went.
- **currency**: a collocation, *exchange rates*, did not contain the headword. Fixed to *currency exchange rates*. A reviewer also noted that *digital currency* is not "the system of money that a country uses"; the definition now begins "a kind of money, especially...".
- **customs**: I had stated the *custom*/*customs* difference three times (a comparison note, a learner error, and a translator note). The house rule is one fact, one field, so it now lives only in the learner error.
- **dairy**: the farm sense joined two different things (a farm building and a company); it is now one sense, "a farm or a company that produces milk and makes or sells food from it", and *dairy cows* moved there from the food sense.
- **cyclist**: *a keen cyclist* is mainly British; the American-English entry now says *an avid cyclist*.

The five rejections: three repeated objections to the closed-list code *often before another noun*; one claimed that *plural only* contradicts *singular or plural verb* (the first describes the word's form, the second its verb agreement); one asked to regroup collocations by word order, which our collocation types do not distinguish.

One definition word outside the defining vocabulary (*yogurt*) was removed. One cross-reference mirror was placed by hand (*customs* compared from the tradition sense of *custom*). All 12 pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and the CMU dictionary.
