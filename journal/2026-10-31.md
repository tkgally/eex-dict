# Run report, 2026-10-31

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to write. It claimed twelve band-2 verbs (band 2 is the second most important frequency group) in queue order: *interpret*, *interrupt*, *investigate*, *joke*, *justify*, *knit*, *label*, *leap*, *line*, *link*, *load*, and *loan*. All twelve were drafted from scratch, checked, reviewed by both reviewers, corrected, and marked `reviewed`, which means they will appear on the public site. The weekly digest was also due, so the cycle wrote `journal/digest-2026-10-31.md` first: the week's numbers, ten entries for you to sample, and the questions that wait on you.

**Checks before review.** The inflection tool generated every verb form from its rules and the exceptions table (*knitted*, also *knit*; *leaped*, also *leapt*; American *labeled*, British *labelled*). The pronunciation check, a panel of three models plus the open CMU pronouncing dictionary consulted at run time, agreed with every transcription but one: the American form of *interrupt* was first written with a doubled r sound; it was corrected to ˌɪn.təˈrʌpt, which then had every vote and the CMU dictionary on its side.

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, a Google model) raised 84 issues, 10 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be marked reviewed). Every one was decided by hand: 66 applied, 18 rejected, none escalated to you. The main changes:

- *leap*: the sense *my heart leaped* was defined as if a person felt happy; both reviewers pointed out that the heart is the subject. It is now defined as the heart seeming to jump because of a strong feeling, fear included, and lost a *literary* label it did not need. *Leap at the chance* (accept eagerly), flagged by both, is now its own subsense.
- *link*: the sense *link arms* now covers hands too, and the computer sense (*this page links to...*) moved ahead of it because learners meet it far more often.
- *load*: two examples in the sense *give someone a lot of something* did not fit it. One, *the sauce is loaded with salt*, is really the adjective *loaded*; the other, about bags in a car, belonged to the first sense. Both were replaced.
- *loan*: both reviewers said the museum use (lending a painting) is a special case of the main sense, not a sense of its own; it is now a subsense. A usage note explains that American English uses *loan* for lending almost anything, British English mainly for money.
- *joke*: the surprised reply *You're joking!* / *You must be joking* is now a subsense.
- *interpret*: the note contrasting it with *translate* appeared twice; it now lives in one place, the learner-error note.

**What was rejected, and why.** Three requests to add idioms were refused under the keyword rule (an idiom goes under its first noun, else its first verb): *knit your brows* belongs under *brow*, *line your pockets* under *pocket*, *look before you leap* under *look*. Reviewer B's requests to hand-mark forms like *joked* in examples were refused: the site marks listed forms itself. Its objection to the corrected American *interrupt* transcription was refused against a unanimous panel and the CMU dictionary.

**Cross-references.** The cross-reference tool adds back-links automatically when it can tell which sense of the other entry to attach them to. In five cases it could not, so they were placed by hand: *join*, *disturb*, *research*, and *examine* now point back to the new verbs, and *loan* now treats *borrow* as a word to compare rather than an opposite, matching how *lend* already does it. One new queue row was added, the phrasal verb *load up*, which a reviewer asked for.

**Spend.** US$0.38 of the day's US$15 budget (reviewer B and the pronunciation panel; reviewer A runs at no charge).

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. Three definitions first used words outside the defining vocabulary (*complaint*, *needles*, *gallery*); the final check caught them and they were reworded.

**What is next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order; the next whole-dictionary lint pass is due in one run.

**What needs you.** Nothing new from this cycle. The digest lists six standing items, among them the two open questions and the pronunciation disputes.

## Cycle 2: lint

**What the cycle did.** The selector forced a *lint* pass because ten runs had passed since the last one. A lint pass is the periodic whole-dictionary check. It runs the mechanical checks over every entry and every wiki page, and then a reading for things a script cannot see. This was the ninety-fifth pass. It made no new entries and changed no definitions.

**The mechanical checks.** The word caps on the project's own files, the links between wiki pages, the validator, the unit tests, and the definition-vocabulary gate all passed. One cap was briefly breached: the wiki as a whole came to 26 words over its 30,000-word limit once this pass's precision note was added. The fix was to condense an older section of `wiki/notes/reviewer-precision.md`, the page that tracks how often each reviewer is right. Six claim files left by finished runs were pruned. A claim file is the record of which words a run had reserved.

**Cross-references.** Every cross-reference in the dictionary should have a matching link back. `crossref.py` adds those back-links when it can tell which sense of the other entry to attach them to. Across the whole dictionary it wrote back-links to four entries and left 16 pairs it could not place. Eleven of these came from older entries that already pointed at the verbs written this morning. Examples are *border*, *code*, *decode*, *read*, *take*, *sew*, and *tie*. These were placed by hand on the right sense of each new verb. Two other pairs from earlier runs were placed the same way: *exemplify* to *illustrate*, and *fall over* to *trip*. The remaining five were left alone. They are forward references that point at an idiom instead of a sense, such as *also* pointing at *well* for *as well*. They belong to the cross-reference audit you asked for in ruling C1, and nothing about them has changed.

**The band vote.** Since the last vote, tools had added 77 new rows to the queue with no frequency band. The three word-list models voted on them for US$0.012. Each row takes the lower median of the three votes. Two rows were split by more than one band, and both were settled at the middle vote: *kid* (verb) in band 2 and *knitwear* in band 4. The record is `experiments/band-vote-2026-10-31/README.md`.

**Reviewer precision.** A reviewer's precision is the share of its issues that turn out to be right. Any issue family that falls under 30 percent over twenty or more decisions gets switched off. Nothing new crossed that line. Reviewer A (the Claude model) is right 83 percent of the time overall. Reviewer B (the Google model) is right 81 percent of the time. Reviewer B's spelling-and-format flags are right only 24 percent of the time, but they stay on under your ruling. Its pronunciation flags (6 of 16 right) and inflection flags (3 of 9) are the closest to being switched off, but they do not yet have twenty decisions.

**Reports.** These reports are queued for review runs. None needs action in a lint pass:
- 330 definitions use their own headword. Most of these are harmless, for example *water* in *give an animal water to drink*.
- 813 cross-reference pairs may name the wrong sense.
- 7 "promises" are notes saying a word "has its own entry" where none exists yet, for example *corn* the foot problem and *butter up*. These are the same kinds of items as at the last pass.

**The reading.** Every wiki page is listed in the index. The open questions are still current. The curator file (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`) has no duplicate lines.

**Spend.** US$0.012 this cycle; US$0.39 for the day.

**What is next.** Build again: band-2 verbs in queue order. The next lint pass is due in ten runs.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. This cycle claimed ten band-2 verbs instead of the allowed twelve, to keep each entry full: *exceed*, *kid*, *lower*, *maintain*, *march*, *matter*, *mistake*, *murder*, *nod*, and *note*. All ten were drafted, checked, reviewed, corrected, and marked `reviewed`. Every verb form came from the rules or the exceptions table; *mistake* takes *mistook* and *mistaken*. The pronunciation panel and the CMU dictionary agreed on all twenty transcriptions on the first pass.

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewers raised 44 issues, 12 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be marked reviewed). Of the 44, 33 were applied and 11 rejected; none was escalated to you. The real problems were:

- *kid*: the adaptation note (advice for translators) said the verb "has nothing to do with children". That is a claim about the word's history, which the house rules keep out of these notes. It was removed. The everyday reply *No kidding!* was missing and is now a phrase.
- *maintain*: an example for the sense *pay for what someone needs to live*, *maintain two homes*, was really about keeping buildings in good repair. It became *works two jobs to maintain her children*. The repair sense now also covers computer systems, with a website example.
- *march*: *Students marched for cheaper schools* was unnatural; it is now *Nurses marched for better pay*.
- *murder*: the very common informal *Mom is going to murder me* had no sense. It is now sense 2, *be very angry with someone and punish them*. That matches the same sense already in *kill*, and the two entries now point to each other.
- *nod*: the sense about a sleepy person's head dropping had the signpost *fall asleep*. A signpost is the short label that helps a reader pick a sense. This one clashed with the separate phrasal verb *nod off*, so it is now *almost asleep*.
- *mistake*: reviewer A pointed out that the formal sense *understand something wrongly* overlapped the main sense. It is now a subsense of it.
- *matter*: one of two learner-error notes, *I don't matter* for *I don't mind*, was judged not to be a mistake learners really make, and was removed. The other, *It's not matter* for *It doesn't matter*, stays.

**What was rejected, and why.** Reviewer B raised four blocking issues asking for *mistook* and *mistaking* to be marked by hand in the examples. All four were rejected: these forms are in the entry's own inflections table, so the site marks them automatically. Two idioms went to other entries under the keyword rule (an idiom goes under its first noun): *lower your guard* belongs under *guard*, and *time marches on* under *time*. *I could murder a pizza* was left out as mainly British and not yet a need for intermediate readers.

**Cross-references.** In six places the cross-reference tool could not tell which sense to link back to, so the links were placed by hand. *Fool*, *keep*, *support*, *kill*, *shake*, and *write down* now point back to the new verbs.

**A tool problem noted.** The band-vote tool writes the date "2026-10-24" on every row it settles, whatever the real date. The *kid* row settled this morning shows the wrong date, though its band is right. This is recorded in `wiki/notes/band-vote-settled-date.md` for a later run to fix, as the rule against changing tools on the spot requires.

**Spend.** US$0.30 this cycle; US$0.69 for the day.

**What is next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: a fresh reading of entries that already exist. There were no draft entries and no open entry reports. The work therefore went to the coverage pass under your ruling B1. The coverage pass rereads the most important band-1 entries, the ones with the most senses first, and asks whether any common use is missing. This cycle read four seven-sense content words that had not been read since 2026-10-24: *deep* (adjective), *card* (noun), *drive* (verb), and *control* (noun). That brings the coverage pass to 121 entries.

**What was added.** These are the uses an advanced learner would expect to find and did not:

- *deep*: a new sense, *deep in thought* or *deep in conversation*. It means so involved in something that you notice little else, and it is used only after a verb such as *be*.
- *card*: *play cards*, the game, was hidden in an explanation; it is now a subsense marked plural only. A second subsense covers a card as an advantage, as in *her strongest card* or *play the sympathy card*. There is also an old-fashioned sense, *your grandfather is a real card*, for an amusing person. Gift cards and store cards now count as payment cards.
- *drive*: *driving rain* is now its own sense, for rain or wind with great force. Hitting a ball hard is a subsense, and *drive someone up the wall* is listed with *drive someone crazy*.
- *control*: a new sense for the people who direct an activity, as in *air traffic control* and *mission control*.

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewers raised 60 issues, 7 of them blocking. Of the 60, 53 were applied and 7 rejected; none was escalated to you. The blocking ones:

- *deep in debt*: this cycle's own addition put it under the new *deep in thought* sense, but being in debt does not mean noticing nothing else. It was removed.
- *card*: one example, *Two yellow cards in one game mean a red card*, stated a real sports rule. The house rules ban checkable facts in examples, so it was replaced. *He mixed the cards* became *He shuffled the cards and dealt them*.
- *control*: *hand* was listed as a synonym, which it is not. It was removed together with the matching link back from *hand*, as the cross-reference rules require. The loose synonym *rule* went the same way.

Several notes repeated themselves, which the "one fact, one field" rule forbids:
- *drive* stated the difference between *drive* and *ride* three times. It now lives in one note.
- *control* had a grammar note repeating its learner-error note; the grammar note was removed.
- *card* and *deep* had the generic "translate each sense separately" advice for translators that the notes rules ban. *Card* now has a specific false-friend warning: in some languages the look-alike word means a map, a menu, or a ticket. *Deep* now names its actual trap: many languages use *low* for a deep voice.

**What was rejected, and why.**
- *Often before another noun* was called "not a grammar code"; it is in the closed list of grammar codes.
- *Drive a hard bargain* belongs under *bargain* by the keyword rule.
- Moving *deep down* to the adverb entry was refused under house rule 7, which keeps idioms under their keyword entry.
- *Report card* is a compound noun, so it was queued as its own entry, `report-card-n`. The phrasal verb *drive at* (*what are you driving at?*) was queued as well.
