# 2026-10-21, cycle 3 of the 00:43 UTC run: eleven phrasal verbs, *move out* to *put up*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose it and allowed up to twelve. The queue is working through the most common phrasal verbs (a verb plus a small word such as *up* or *on*, with its own meaning). I claimed twelve and wrote eleven:

- *move out* (leave your home; leave as an organized group)
- *pass away* (die, said gently)
- *pass on* (tell news to the next person; hand something on; give someone a disease; die)
- *point out* (tell someone a fact they had missed; show where someone is)
- *put away*, *put back*, *put down*, *put off*, *put on*, *put together*, *put up*

The *put* verbs are some of the richest in English: *put up* has seven senses (build, hang on a wall, raise a hand, give someone a bed, raise a price, make an effort against someone, give money), *put down* six, *put on* five. Each entry has definitions, examples, grammar patterns, collocations (word partners), cross-references, a learner mistake, and notes for translators. *Put off* and *put on* also have a short note on choosing between near-synonyms (*put off*, *postpone*, *delay*; *put on*, *wear*, *dress*).

The twelfth word, *result in*, was not written: *result* (verb) sense 2 already covers it, and a settled house rule says a verb plus preposition that the verb's own entry covers is not a separate phrasal verb. It is marked as a duplicate in the queue.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two outside reviewer models read every field of every entry. They raised 45 issues; I applied 26 and rejected 19. The main changes:

- *put together* had a sense for *more than all of us put together*. Both reviewers objected that its definition was not a verb phrase. They were right: the form never changes, so it reads more like a fixed expression. It is now a usage note instead of a sense.
- *move out*: the second sense now says "start to leave a place, especially as part of an organized group" rather than "together", and an unnatural example about a bus was replaced.
- *put down*: the summary sentence at the top now covers all six senses, and the definitions of writing and of killing a sick animal are more accurate.
- *put on*: an example (*put the coffee on*) did not fit its sense, which is about starting machines and music; it now uses *put a movie on*.

I rejected, among others: reviewer-b's repeated suggestion of a "verb + adverb" grammar pattern, which is not in our closed list (a settled house rule); reviewer-a's objection to British labels on *put back* (to a later date; clocks) and *put up* (prices). In this dictionary a region label means "mainly used there", and American English usually says *push back*, *set back*, and *raise* for these.

## Pronunciation

All 22 transcriptions (American and British for each word) came back "disputed": only one of the three pronunciation models agreed with mine. That looked like a fault, so I made one small diagnostic call (about one cent) to see what the models actually write. All three put the main stress on the second word (*put UP*), as I do. The difference is only in how they mark the first word: one leaves it unmarked, one marks it with the secondary-stress sign, and one marks it with a second main-stress sign. The checking tool accepts the first two but not the third. So the "dispute" is about writing conventions, not about pronunciation. Following earlier practice, I left the entries flagged and added one line to the owner's question file. The details and a possible tool fix are in `wiki/notes/phrasal-verb-stress-recheck.md`; under the project's rules, a tool change waits for a later run with a logged reason.
