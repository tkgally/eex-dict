## What this cycle did

A **build cycle** writes new entries from the work queue and takes each one through the full review pipeline. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build again because it was furthest behind its usual share. The claim tool handed out the next twelve band-1 adjectives in queue order (band 1 is the most common words): *economic*, *effective*, *elderly*, *electric*, *electronic*, *emotional*, *empty*, *equal*, *exact*, *excited*, *expensive*, and *fair*.

I wrote each from my own knowledge, following the house style guide, and tried to make them as full as the first cycle's entries. Some points of interest:

- *economic* and *electric* each have a short guide to a pair that learners confuse: *economic* (about trade and money) against *economical* (saving money or fuel), and *electric* against *electrical*. *economic* also has the learner error *This car is very economic*.
- *effective* has three senses: working well, true in practice (*in effective control*), and officially starting (*effective from Monday*). Its guide compares *effective* with *efficient*.
- *elderly* has a usage note on polite ways to talk about older people.
- *empty* has four senses, from "nothing inside" to feeling that life has no purpose, plus a guide to *empty* versus *vacant* and the error *I have some empty time* (should be *free time*).
- *excited* has a note on the prepositions that follow it and the error *I'm exciting about the party*.
- *expensive* warns that a thing is expensive but a price is *high*, not *expensive*.
- *fair* has six senses (equal and right, reasonable, average, quite large, light in color, and pleasant weather) and three phrases: *fair enough*, *to be fair*, and *fair and square*.

## What the checks and reviewers found

The pronunciation check verified all 24 transcriptions, and the inflection tool produced *emptier* and *fairer*; the other ten words compare with *more* or not at all.

The two reviewer models raised 43 issues; only *emotional* came back with none. I accepted 25 and rejected 18. Nothing went to the owner.

Accepted:

- *exact*: reviewer-a said the sense "careful about small details" is not really a meaning of *exact* (words such as *precise* or *careful* do that job), and that its examples showed the ordinary "correct" sense instead. I agreed and removed it. Reviewer-b said that in *the exact same dress*, *exact* works like an adverb in front of *same*; I replaced that example with *the exact place where we first met*. The entry now has two senses.
- *economic*: the guide said "do not use *economic* to mean cheap to run", but the entry's own sense 2 (making enough money to be worth doing) is close to that. The guide and the translator's note now say exactly where the line is.
- *fair*: the summary sentence wrongly called fair weather "light or bright"; the weather sense lost a "formal" label it did not need; *to be fair* is now defined as showing the other side, not only softening a criticism; and the learner error *It's not fair for me* was removed, because *It's not fair for me to decide* is correct English.
- *elderly*: the notes now say the word can sound formal or distant, not only polite.
- *electric*: sense 2 now reads "made of electricity, carrying it, or caused by it", which fits *electric current* and *electric shock*.
- *expensive*: *costly* no longer "means that something costs too much"; it is used when a cost is a loss or a problem.

Rejected, each with a reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`: reviewer-b called the summary sentence "padding" four times, against the house rule that it is a full sentence; it said three times that *comparative with more* is not a valid grammar code, but it is on the closed list; and it wanted *fair and square* moved to an adverb entry, against the settled rule that idioms stay under their keyword. A request to drop the note that *fair* describes hair and skin was also rejected: such restrictions belong in that note by house rule.

After review, the cross-reference tool added three back-links to existing entries. One landed on the wrong sense: *fair* was listed as the opposite of *dark* meaning "with little light". I moved it to *dark*'s color sense.
