## What I changed

- ***lot***: the note said *you lot* and *the lot* (meaning *everything*: *He ate the lot*) are British. The reviewer pointed out that Americans also say this. I rewrote it: *you lot* is British; *the lot* is mainly British, and American English more often says *the whole lot* or *all of it*.
- ***love***: the sense used to speak to someone in a friendly way (*Thanks, love*) said that "shop staff and older people often use it to strangers". That was a generalization about a group of people. It now says that in some parts of Britain some people, such as shop staff, use it to strangers; it is meant to be friendly, though some people do not like it. The matching note for translators was rewritten the same way. *A person that you love* became *a person who you love*.
- ***lift***: the ski-lift sense described only seats hanging from a wire; it now also covers small cars.
- ***level***: the example *What level are you in your English class?* was unnatural; it is now *What level is your English class?* Two collocation lists were tidied (*at government level* moved into the phrase list).
- ***look***: the example *From the look on his face, the news was bad* was under the sense *the way someone or something appears*; it belongs to the face-expression sense and was moved there.
- ***loss***: the money sense listed *at a loss*, which is also a separate phrase meaning *not knowing what to do*. It now lists *sell something at a loss*, which makes the money use clear.
- ***line***: *go over the line* was replaced with *cross the line* in the border sense, and an example for *read between the lines* now says *she seems to want to leave*, because the reader is guessing.
- Smaller fixes on *lesson* (the *class* note now covers colleges and clubs), *letter* (*a lowercase letter*), *lid* (a pen has a *cap*), and *limit* (*a limit of ten people*).

## What I rejected

- That *story* is not a synonym of *lie*: our own *story* entry has a sense *an explanation that is not true, told to trick someone*.
- That *thumb a lift* belongs under the verb: the project puts a phrase under its first noun, here *lift*.
- That *give someone a lift* should not be labeled British: the project's labels mean *mainly used there*, and Americans mostly say *ride*.
- reviewer-b's two flags on the *lot* phrases (*draw lots*, *throw in your lot with someone*): their definitions are already in the required form.
- Two learner errors that the reviewer called "possible English" (*the limit of speed*, *the link of the website*): both are forms learners produce and native speakers do not.
- That *a bunch of* and *a mass of* are not synonyms of *a lot*; that the *informal* labels on *lot* (a group) and *Love* (closing a letter) are wrong; and that the term-of-address use of *love* cannot be marked uncountable.
