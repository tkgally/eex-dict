# 2026-09-26 (eleventh run): ten more verbs, *marry* to *name*

## What this run did

This was the fifth cycle of this session and a **build** run, which writes new dictionary entries. Earlier cycles today built thirty verbs (pull requests #76, #77, #78) and gave twenty older prepositions and pronouns a second reading (pull request #79). The scheduler (`next_mode.py`, the script that picks each run's kind of work) returned to building, because the queue of words still to write is the longest backlog.

This cycle drafted the next ten verbs in band 1, the most basic band of the word list: **marry, mean, measure, meet, melt, mention, mix, move, multiply, name**. The largest are *mean* and *move*.

- *Mean* has six senses (separate meanings): what a word means, what a speaker means, being serious (*I mean it*), intending (*I didn't mean to*), having a result (*it means moving*), and being important to someone. It also has two phrases, *I mean* and *mean well*.
- *Move* covers changing position, changing your home, making progress, feelings (*the story moved me*), games, and taking action.

The learner-error boxes show a common wrong sentence beside the correct one. This cycle's include *married with a doctor* → *married a doctor*, *what means this word?* → *what does this word mean?*, *mentioned about the problem* → *mentioned the problem*, and *I moved to here* → *I moved here*. The adaptation notes (short notes for future translators) point out where other languages often use a preposition that English does not (*marry*, *mention*), and where one English verb covers ideas that other languages split (*meet*, *mean*, *move*).

## Checks and reviews

All ten entries went through the full pipeline:

- **Format and inflections.** All ten passed the format check. The inflection tool took *meant* and *met* from the table of irregular forms and spelled *multiplies*/*multiplied* by rule.
- **Pronunciation.** All twenty American and British transcriptions were verified. This included *marry*, which has a different vowel in the two varieties, and *multiply*.
- **Reviewers.** One reviewer-b reply, on *name*, could not be read and was run again. The two reviewers then raised 31 issues, only 10 of them blocking (they must be settled before publication), fewer than in earlier cycles. Every issue got a logged decision: 18 fixes applied and 13 rejected.

**What was fixed.** *Meet* in the sense *meet a deadline* had been defined as "do or have what is needed or wanted", which is too loose. It now reads "reach the level that a need, a rule, or a plan asks for". *Meet*'s usage note had also said that *meet* alone is for social or chance meetings; that was wrong, and it now says bare *meet* is used for every kind of meeting. Two things were taken out of *move*: an example with *move on*, which is a phrasal verb with its own entry, and *move house* in its word combinations, which is British and is already explained in the usage note. In *mix*, the example *he mixed us some drinks* was not covered by the definition and was replaced. *Thanks for the lift*, which is British, became *thanks for the ride*. Four lists of word combinations had the wrong type heading (*move slowly* is verb + adverb, not adverb + verb) and were corrected.

**What was rejected.** Reviewer-a questioned the slug `mix-up-phrv` for *mix up*; that name was settled in an earlier run. Reviewer-b asked for capital letters on short example phrases inside notes, which the house style keeps lowercase. It also asked to change the pattern label on a passive example, and the house practice is to name the active pattern. Its usual objection to the quoted headword in adaptation notes was rejected as before.
