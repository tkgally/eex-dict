## What was done

The cycle drafted twelve nouns: *exchange, excitement, exercise, exit, experience, experiment, explanation, eye, fabric, face, fact, factory*. Together they hold 35 senses (numbered meanings) and 33 phrases. Two of the twelve are large entries. **Eye** has four senses (the body part, *an eye for color*, the eye of a needle, the eye of a storm) and thirteen idioms, including *keep an eye on*, *see eye to eye*, *turn a blind eye*, and *with the naked eye*. **Face** has five senses and twelve idioms, including *face to face*, *lose face*, *save face*, *keep a straight face*, *on the face of it*, and *take something at face value*. **Fact** has six phrases, including *in fact*, *know for a fact*, and *a fact of life*.

There are 18 learner errors (common mistakes shown with their correction). Several come from words that look alike in other languages: *My father works in a fabric* (for *factory*), *I could see the excitation on their faces* (for *excitement*), *We made a wonderful experience* (for *had*). The adaptation notes for translators flag the same traps, along with *experience* against *experiment*.

**The keyword rule.** An idiom lives under the first noun in it. `NEXT.md` had a standing instruction: when *eye* and *face* were written, move the idioms that had been waiting under verbs. *Cry your eyes out* moved from `cry-v` to `eye-n`, and *laugh in someone's face* moved from `laugh-v` to `face-n`. Both verbs now point to the nouns. A search of every entry found no other idioms waiting for these two words. (*Have egg on your face* stays under *egg*, its first noun.)

All 24 American and British transcriptions were confirmed by the pronunciation check. *Exit* and *factory* record their second common pronunciations (*EK-sit*, and *FAK-tree* in fast speech).

## What the reviewers found and what was decided

The two reviewer models raised 37 issues, 20 of them blocking. I decided each one by hand: 24 applied and 13 rejected, all logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The applied fixes were mostly cases where I had been too absolute or too narrow:

- Three idioms under **fact** were defined too tightly. *In fact* can correct something said earlier, not only make it stronger. *A fact of life* is not always unpleasant. *After the fact* does not always mean too late. All three were reworded.
- **Exchange**: the money sense was marked uncountable, but *an exchange of dollars for euros* is countable. *Foreign exchange* was removed from its word partners, because it names a wider financial idea.
- **Exit**: I had called *exit door* a learner error. It is a normal term, so the error was removed.
- **Experience**: a claim that British English prefers *experience of* while American English prefers *with* was too strong. The note now lists all four patterns without a regional claim.
- **Exercise**: *take exercise* is British, so the American example now uses *get exercise*.
- **Fabric**: an unsupported claim that *fabric* is the shop word was replaced. The example now measures cloth in yards, as American English does.
- **Explanation**: the "reason" sense was made countable or uncountable, to match *without any explanation*.

Rejected: the objection that an eye is not "part of the face" (the plain wording is right for learners), objections to definitions that begin with an *-ing* word (they are noun phrases, and the house style bans *the act of*), and the usual flags against the quoted headword that opens an adaptation note.

Six definition words fell outside the defining vocabulary (the list of simple words definitions are written from) and were reworded. The eye of a needle is now described as a hole in a thin, sharp tool used to sew. The words *needle* and *thread* have no entries yet, so the definition could not use them.
