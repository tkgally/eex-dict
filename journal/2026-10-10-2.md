## What this cycle did

A **closure cycle** writes entries for words the dictionary already uses but has not yet defined. There are two kinds. **Closure rows** are words that appear in our own definitions and are outside the defining vocabulary (the limited word list that definitions are meant to use). **Family rows** are related words that an existing entry names in its word-family box (*device* names *devise*, for example). The scheduler chose closure because it was furthest behind its share of cycles.

There were only four closure rows. Two had the wrong part of speech, because the tool that adds them has to guess: *windy* had been queued as a noun and *clicking* (from the new *web* entry's "by clicking links") as a verb form. I marked both rows as duplicates and drafted the real words, the adjective *windy* and the verb *click*. The other two were *carrot* and *castle*. I then took eight family verbs in queue order: *desert, detail, devise, differ, dine, direct, disable,* and *disapprove*. All twelve are now `reviewed`.

## The entries

- *click* has four senses: on a computer, a short sound (*the door clicked shut*), suddenly becoming clear (*then it clicked*), and two people getting on well (*we clicked at once*).
- *desert* (verb) has three senses and a usage note that separates three easily confused words: *desert* the verb (*dih-ZURT*), *desert* the dry land (*DEZ-urt*), and *dessert* the sweet food, which sounds like the verb.
- *direct* (verb) has five senses, from managing a team and directing a film to showing someone the way.
- *differ* has two learner errors: *Our opinions are differ* and *differs than*.
- *devise* warns against spelling it *device*, the noun.
- *carrot* includes the phrase *the carrot and the stick*.

## Pronunciation

Twenty-three of the 24 transcriptions were confirmed at once. The British *direct* was disputed: I had written the first part like *die*, and only one of three models agreed. I switched to the form with the first part like *did*, which matches our existing *direction* entry, and kept the *die* forms as listed variants. The recheck confirmed it 3 to 0.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 33 issues, 23 of them blocking. I agreed with 22 and fixed the entries myself, and rejected 11 with logged reasons. None needed the owner.

The most useful corrections:

- *castle*: I had included the chess piece (often called a castle; officially a rook). The reviewer objected to my definition, which said "a piece in a board game" without naming chess. It was right that this was vague, but *chess* is outside the defining vocabulary and has no entry, so the definition could not name it. I removed that sense and put one sentence about it in the usage note instead. I did not accept the reviewer's claim that *castle* is not a normal name for the piece; it is.
- *detail*: the job-giving sense is used for police and work too, not only soldiers; the note calling the verb formal now covers only the describing sense.
- *dine*: I had said *dine in* means eating at home or in the restaurant, which was muddled; it is gone.
- *disable*: the note had said it means the same as *turn off*; it now says the two are close, since a disabled feature can stop working while the machine stays on.

Rejected, with reasons: three requests from reviewer-b for an intransitive "verb" pattern, which does not exist in our closed list of verb patterns; a request to label the reward sense of *carrot* informal (it is common in news and business writing); and a claim that *Can you direct me the way to the station?* is natural English (the standard form is *direct me to*).

Reviewer-b raised only five issues across the twelve entries, and three of them were wrong; this matches its low yield noted earlier (`wiki/notes/reviewer-b-low-yield.md`).
