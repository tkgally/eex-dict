# Journal, 2026-11-06 (third run)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle was.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from the state of the dictionary) chose *build*: writing new entries from the main queue of headwords, in the order the build-order decision sets. Its reason was that build had the most "scheduler debt", meaning it was the job most overdue relative to its share of the runs. It allowed up to twelve new entries and a paid-call ceiling of US$3. Before starting, I checked for leftover work: no pull requests from earlier runs were open, no old `claude/` branches were left on GitHub, no reader reports (issues titled "Entry report") were waiting, and the inbox, where the owner leaves instructions, was empty. The weekly digest is not due until tomorrow.

**What was written.** Twelve nouns, all in frequency band 2 (the second most common of five bands): *driveway*, *drought*, *dryer*, *earring*, *earthquake*, *ecosystem*, *edition*, *elbow*, *elevator*, *emphasis*, *engineer*, and *engineering*. Points worth noting:

- *drought* has a second sense, a long time without something wanted (*a scoring drought*), and a translator's note that it rhymes with **out**, not with **thought**.
- *dryer* covers the clothes machine and the hair or hand dryer, and records the less common spelling *drier*, which is usually the comparative of *dry*.
- *edition* has three senses: a form of a published book (*the second edition*), one version of a newspaper or broadcast (*the Sunday edition*), and a special or limited version of a product (*a limited edition*). A note warns translators that look-alike words in some languages also mean "editing" or "a publisher".
- *elbow* has the body sense, with the sleeve as a sub-sense, and the pipe fitting. It has five phrases: *elbow grease*, *elbow room*, *rub elbows with someone* (American), *up to your elbows in something*, and *give someone the elbow* (British).
- *elevator* is labeled American and points to British *lift*. It also has the grain-storage sense and the phrases *elevator pitch* and *elevator music*.
- *emphasis* takes **on**, and has a learner error for *emphasis in*. Its plural *emphases* comes from the project's table of irregular forms.
- *engineer* has four senses: the trained designer, the repair worker (labeled British after review), the train driver (American), and the ship's engine officer.

**The checks.** Every entry went through the full pipeline. Plurals came from the spelling rules and the exceptions table. The pronunciation check compares each transcription with three other models, and the American one with the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. It confirmed all 24. For *engineer* and *engineering* the CMU dictionary disagreed, but three of four models agreed, which passes the project's rule.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Two reviewers from different AI labs read every field. They raised 36 issues, 10 of them "blocking" (a blocking issue must be decided before an entry can be marked reviewed). I accepted 35 and rejected 1. Each decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. The main ones:

- Both reviewers caught an example for *ecosystem* that made a checkable science claim about coral reefs, which the style guide forbids. I replaced it.
- Both said that *engineer* for a repair worker (*a heating engineer*) is mainly British. They are right: American English says *technician* or *repairman*. The sense now carries a British label, and a "compare" link points to *technician*, which the tools have queued as a new headword.
- Reviewer A showed that *a special edition of the car* did not fit sense 3 of *edition* as I had defined it ("a fixed number of copies"). I widened the definition to cover special versions with extra features.
- Reviewer A showed that *genetic engineering* did not fit the one sense of *engineering*. It is now a phrase of its own, with its own definition.
- I rejected one request: to split the broadcast use of *edition* from the newspaper use. Both name one version in a regular series, with the same grammar, so the splitting test of the style guide is not met.

The minor fixes included shorter signposts (the short labels that help a reader pick a sense), a less absolute note on British *drive*, and the removal of an "informal" label from *elbow room*. I also added the two *elbow* phrases a reviewer said were missing.

After the review, the vocabulary check (which keeps definitions inside the list of about 2,400 plain defining words) found three words outside it: *version*, *machinery*, and *electrical*. I reworded the definitions. The cross-reference tool added matching links in two existing entries, *dry* (adjective) and *system*. For two more it could not tell which sense to use, so I placed them by hand: *issue* (its newspaper sense) now compares with *edition*, and *knee* with *elbow*.

**Spend.** US$0.34 for this cycle, mostly the second reviewer and the pronunciation panel. The day's total is about US$1.75 of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The next build continues the queue after *engineering*. A lint pass (the periodic check of the whole dictionary and wiki) is due in about one more run.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle was.** After the first cycle merged, the mode selector chose *review*: rereading entries that already exist and fixing what they lack. There were no draft entries and no reader reports, so the work came from the coverage pass the owner asked for on 2026-10-24 (ruling B1). That pass rereads the most common words, the ones in frequency band 1, starting with the entries that have the most senses, and checks that every common sense, idiom, and pattern is there. This cycle took the first four of the 115 band-1 entries with five senses that nobody has reread since that ruling: *band* (noun), *bill* (noun), *bottle* (noun), and *bright* (adjective).

**What I changed before the reviewers saw them.** In *band* I added common pairings (*a brass band*, *a live band*, *a band breaks up*). In *bill* I added a pointer to the separate entry for a bird's bill. In *bright* I added pointers to two idioms that live under other words by the house rule, *look on the bright side* (under *side*) and *a bright spark* (under *spark*), and I made the pronunciation note plainer. Three of the four entries had a translator's note that said only "translate each sense separately", which the style guide forbids because it names no particular trap. I rewrote each to name one. For example, the *bright* note now says that in many languages the word for strong light cannot describe a person, so *a bright child* needs a word meaning "clever". In *bottle* the usage note said the same thing as a translator's note, which breaks the rule that each fact lives in one field, so I removed it.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 32 issues, 2 of them "blocking". I accepted 26 and rejected 6.

- *bill*: both reviewers said the restaurant sense should not carry an "explanation". That field is meant for function words and other words that are hard to use, not ordinary nouns. The point about American *check* moved into the note on the synonym link. Both also showed that the usage note repeated facts already in the labels and links, so it was removed. *Top the bill* is now labeled British.
- *bottle*: reviewer A showed that *the bottle* (*he turned to the bottle*) means drinking too much, not any drinking habit. The definition is fixed, and the summary line at the top of the entry now covers all five senses.
- *bright*: both reviewers pointed out that *shine bright* uses *bright* as an adverb, which does not belong in an adjective entry, so it came out. *A bright spot* (a good thing in a bad time) did not fit the "hopeful" sense and was replaced. *A bright idea* now has a clearer definition, and its note says the phrase is often ironic.
- *band*: the music sense was too narrow ("popular music") for its own examples (*a brass band*), so it now says "music, especially popular music or jazz". I dropped the mainly British *elastic band*.

The six rejections each have a logged reason. Examples: a sub-sense may have one example (the rule allows one to four), and *bold colors* really is a common way to say strong colors.

**Spend.** US$0.22. The day's total is about US$1.97 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *bring*, *burn*, *busy*, and *cheap*. A lint pass is due.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: lint

**What this cycle was.** The mode selector forced a *lint* pass, because ten cycles had passed since the last one. A lint pass checks the whole dictionary and the project's notes (the wiki) for problems that build up over time: broken links, missing return links between entries, files over their size limits, and pages that disagree with each other. Most of it is unpaid; the only paid step is the frequency-band vote below.

**What the checks found and what I did.**

- Size limits, links between pages, and the entry gate (the validator that refuses any published entry that does not qualify) were all clean, and all unit tests passed.
- *Return links.* When one entry names another as a synonym, an opposite, a "compare", or a word-family member, the other entry should name it back. The cross-reference tool added eleven missing return links by itself, for example from *town* back to *downtown*. In seven more cases it could not tell which sense of the target the link belongs to, so I placed them by hand. Some examples: *black*, in its "people" sense, now compares with *brown*; *disagreement* lists *conflict* (more serious) and *misunderstanding* (a small one); *emphasis* lists *focus*; *district* lists *quarter* (part of a town). Most of these targets were built in the last few days, which is why the links were new.
- *Claim files.* Six old claim files were deleted. A claim file is the note a run leaves to say which headwords it is writing. These six belonged to runs whose entries are all merged.
- *Frequency bands.* 39 queue rows had no band yet. These are words that tools had added to the queue when an entry pointed to them. Three models voted on a band for each (cost US$0.0056). In two cases the votes differed by more than one band, and I settled them. *Ask after* (to ask how someone is) went to band 4, since it is mainly British and met in wide reading. *Elbow* the verb (*elbow your way through a crowd*) went to band 3. All 39 rows are now waiting in the queue in the normal way.
- *Reviewer precision.* This is the share of each reviewer's objections, by kind, that I accepted. Neither reviewer has a new kind of objection that falls under the 30 percent line, which is the point where the project turns that kind off for that reviewer. The figures are recorded in the reviewer-precision note.
- *Reports for later review.* There are 324 definitions that use their own headword (most are harmless, such as *a wing of a chicken* under *wing*). There are 973 pairs of linked entries where a sense may be missing. There are 6 known "promises", where a note mentions an entry that does not exist yet. These numbers are almost the same as at the last lint.
- The wiki's index matches its pages, the list of questions for the curator has no duplicate lines, and there are no open questions for the owner.

**Spend.** US$0.0056. The day's total is about US$1.97 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The next lint is due in ten cycles. Build, review, and closure work continue as the selector picks them.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: build

**What this cycle was.** After the lint merged, the mode selector chose *build* again, with up to twelve new entries. I took eight. The style guide's rule is that fewer, fuller entries beat more thin ones, and this was the run's fourth cycle, the most a run may have, so I kept it small enough to finish well.

**What was written.** Eight band-2 nouns from the queue: *bookshelf*, *enthusiasm*, *envelope*, *equation*, *era*, *eraser*, *essay*, and *establishment*. Points worth noting:

- *enthusiasm* has the feeling (uncountable, *great enthusiasm for the project*) and a countable sense, something you love doing (*one of her great enthusiasms*). It has a translator's note on the stress, *in-THOO-zee-az-um*.
- *envelope* has the phrase *push the envelope*. It gives the second common pronunciation, starting like *ON*, and a "compare" link to the verb *envelop*, which has no final **e**.
- *equation* has the mathematics sense, with a chemistry sub-sense added after review. It also has *part of the equation* (the things to be thought about together) and a formal sense, *the equation of money with happiness*. A note warns that the ending sounds like *zhun*, not *shun*.
- *eraser* points to British *rubber* and has a second sense for the board eraser in a classroom.
- *essay* has the school essay, with published essays as a sub-sense. It has a learner error (*make an essay* for *write an essay*) and a translator's warning: look-alike words in some languages mean "a try" or "a rehearsal".
- *establishment* has three senses: setting something up, a business such as a hotel or a restaurant, and *the establishment*, the people with power who want things to stay as they are.

**The checks.** The pronunciation check confirmed all 16 transcriptions, and the plurals came from the spelling rules (*bookshelves*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 25 issues, one of them blocking. I accepted all 25.

- The blocking one: I had labeled the countable sense of *enthusiasm* "formal". Reviewer A was right that *one of his enthusiasms* is neutral, so the label came off.
- Both reviewers said my learner error for *enthusiasm* (*enthusiasm about music*) was not really an error, since *about* is acceptable. I replaced it with *enthusiasm in the trip*, which is wrong.
- Both pointed out that a chemical equation is not the mathematical kind. It now has a sub-sense of its own.
- Reviewer A noticed that both definitions of *essay* said "short", while the entry itself lists *a long essay*. The word "short" came out.
- *Anti-establishment* is an adjective, not a pairing of the noun, so it moved from the collocations to the word family.

After review, one definition word (*reaction*) was outside the defining vocabulary, and I reworded it. The cross-reference tool added return links in *book*, *shelf*, and *equal*. I placed two more by hand, in the history senses of *period* and *age*, which now compare with *era*. The idiom *the end of an era* belongs under *end*, its first noun, by the house rule. `NEXT.md` now asks for it at that entry's next review.

**Spend.** US$0.22. The day's total is about US$2.19 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The next build continues the queue after *establishment*. The B1 coverage pass continues with *bring*, *burn*, *busy*, and *cheap*.
