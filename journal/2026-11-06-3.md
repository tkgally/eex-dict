# Journal, 2026-11-06 (third run)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle was.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from the state of the dictionary) chose *build*: writing new entries from the main queue of headwords, in the order the build-order decision sets. Its reason was that build had the most "scheduler debt", meaning it was the job most overdue relative to its share of the runs. It allowed up to twelve new entries and a paid-call ceiling of US$3. Before starting, I checked for leftover work: no pull requests from earlier runs were open, no old `claude/` branches were left on GitHub, no reader reports (issues titled "Entry report") were waiting, and the inbox, where the owner leaves instructions, was empty. The weekly digest is not due until tomorrow.

**What was written.** Twelve nouns, all in frequency band 2 (the second most common of five bands): *driveway*, *drought*, *dryer*, *earring*, *earthquake*, *ecosystem*, *edition*, *elbow*, *elevator*, *emphasis*, *engineer*, and *engineering*. Points worth noting:

- *drought* has a second sense, a long time without something wanted (*a scoring drought*), and a translator's note that it rhymes with **out**, not with **thought**.
- *dryer* covers the clothes machine and the hair or hand dryer, and records the less common spelling *drier*, which is usually the comparative of *dry*.
- *edition* has three senses: a form of a published book (*the second edition*), one version of a newspaper or broadcast (*the Sunday edition*), and a special or limited version of a product (*a limited edition*). A note warns translators that look-alike words in some languages also mean "editing" or "a publisher".
- *elbow* has the body sense, with the sleeve as a sub-sense, and the pipe fitting. It has five phrases: *elbow grease*, *elbow room*, *rub elbows with someone* (American), *up to your elbows in something*, and *give someone the elbow* (British).
- *elevator* is labeled American and points to British *lift*. It also has the grain-storage sense and the phrases *elevator pitch* and *elevator music*.
- *emphasis* takes **on**, and has a learner error for *emphasis in*. Its plural *emphases* comes from the project's table of irregular forms.
- *engineer* has four senses: the trained designer, the repair worker (labeled British after review), the train driver (American), and the ship's engine officer.

**The checks.** Every entry went through the full pipeline. Plurals came from the spelling rules and the exceptions table. The pronunciation check compares each transcription with three other models, and the American one with the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. It confirmed all 24. For *engineer* and *engineering* the CMU dictionary disagreed, but three of four models agreed, which passes the project's rule.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Two reviewers from different AI labs read every field. They raised 36 issues, 10 of them "blocking" (a blocking issue must be decided before an entry can be marked reviewed). I accepted 35 and rejected 1. Each decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`. The main ones:

- Both reviewers caught an example for *ecosystem* that made a checkable science claim about coral reefs, which the style guide forbids. I replaced it.
- Both said that *engineer* for a repair worker (*a heating engineer*) is mainly British. They are right: American English says *technician* or *repairman*. The sense now carries a British label, and a "compare" link points to *technician*, which the tools have queued as a new headword.
- Reviewer A showed that *a special edition of the car* did not fit sense 3 of *edition* as I had defined it ("a fixed number of copies"). I widened the definition to cover special versions with extra features.
- Reviewer A showed that *genetic engineering* did not fit the one sense of *engineering*. It is now a phrase of its own, with its own definition.
- I rejected one request: to split the broadcast use of *edition* from the newspaper use. Both name one version in a regular series, with the same grammar, so the splitting test of the style guide is not met.

The minor fixes included shorter signposts (the short labels that help a reader pick a sense), a less absolute note on British *drive*, and the removal of an "informal" label from *elbow room*. I also added the two *elbow* phrases a reviewer said were missing.

After the review, the vocabulary check (which keeps definitions inside the list of about 2,400 plain defining words) found three words outside it: *version*, *machinery*, and *electrical*. I reworded the definitions. The cross-reference tool added matching links in two existing entries, *dry* (adjective) and *system*. For two more it could not tell which sense to use, so I placed them by hand: *issue* (its newspaper sense) now compares with *edition*, and *knee* with *elbow*.
