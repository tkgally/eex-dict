# Run report, 2026-10-25 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to be written. It took the next twelve words of milestone 1, the first target of about 2,500 words that the dictionary's own definitions are written with. They were eleven verbs, *scratch*, *sew*, *smash*, *sneeze*, *succeed*, *tidy*, *twist*, *unlock*, *unpack*, *upload* and *zip*, and one grammar word, the noun *adverb*. Before starting, I checked for unfinished work from earlier runs: there were no open pull requests, no leftover branches, nothing in the owner's inbox, and no problem reports from readers.

Each entry went through the full pipeline: a format check; the word forms (*sewed*, *sewn*, *zipped*, *tidies*) generated from the rules; both pronunciations checked against a panel of three models and an open pronouncing dictionary (all 24 were confirmed); then the two reviewers, read field by field. About 205 milestone-1 words are now left.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** This was the first build since this morning's change that made reviewer A a Claude model. The two reviewers raised 77 points, 7 of them marked blocking. I accepted 65 and rejected 12.

The useful catches were real. In *scratch*, one sense mixed two meanings: scraping something off a surface, and crossing a name off a list. I split it in two, and the new sense also covers the informal "cancel" use (*we had to scratch the trip*). In *smash*, "destroy or defeat" did not fit *smash a record*, which means beating it by a lot, so that use got its own sense. In *tidy*, both reviewers objected to a separate sense for *tidy away*; I folded it into the main sense as a sentence pattern and rewrote the usage note, which had overstated how British the word is. Both reviewers asked for the idiom *nothing to sneeze at*, and one asked for *zip it* (meaning "be quiet"); both were added as phrases. Smaller fixes covered definitions that did not match their own examples (unlocking a phone with a fingerprint), mismatched grammar patterns, and odd collocations such as *sew a hole*.

The 12 rejections followed settled house rules. Idioms are filed under their first noun, so *twist someone's arm* stays under *arm*, which already has it, and *scratch the surface* stays under *surface*. Phrasal verbs (*smash up*, *sew up*) get their own entries rather than becoming senses. A request for a bare intransitive pattern was rejected because the closed list of patterns has none.

Each new entry points to related entries, and five of those entries needed a matching link back. The script could not tell which sense to attach them to, so I placed them by hand (*follow*, *clean*, *turn*, *rub*, *crash*). Two links were of different types on each side (*synonym* on one, *compare* on the other), and I changed our side to match.

**Spend.** US$0.59 for this cycle, almost all of it reviewer B and the pronunciation panel. Reviewer A runs on the owner's Claude subscription and costs nothing in the ledger. The day so far: about US$7.60 of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** A tool problem: every reviewer A call is recorded under the wrong model name, `claude-haiku-5-5`. Claude Code runs a tiny helper call on a smaller model, and the tool records the first model it lists, which is the helper. A test call showed the real review was done by the model the configuration names. The labels matter because reviewer accuracy is measured model by model. Following the house rule that a problem is written down before it is fixed, I recorded it in `wiki/notes/claude-cli-model-label.md`. The fix is planned for the next cycle.

**What is next.** The next cycle should fix the model label and correct this cycle's records with a logged decision. After that, the queue order in `NEXT.md` applies: the phrasal-verb grammar codes, the cross-reference audit, and more milestone-1 building.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new. The open items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" are unchanged.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. Before drafting, I fixed the tool problem found in cycle 1. When the tool calls reviewer A through Claude Code, Claude Code also runs a tiny helper task on a smaller model, and the tool had been recording that helper's name instead of the reviewer's. It now records the model it asked for, and a new unit test checks this. Reviewer accuracy is measured model by model, so I also corrected cycle 1's records to the right model name: 12 spending-ledger lines, 12 review files, 12 entry histories and 65 decision lines. Nothing else changed. This cycle's reviews already carry the right name.

I then built the next twelve milestone-1 words, all nouns: *ash*, *bandage*, *bark* (the outer covering of a tree), *centimeter*, *claw*, *clinic*, *conclusion*, *cupboard*, *dose*, *expert*, *flag* and *grandchild*. The plural *grandchildren* went into the table of irregular forms, so the form tool can produce it. All 24 pronunciations were confirmed. About 190 milestone-1 words are left.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** There were 32 points (9 blocking). I accepted 29 and rejected 3. Most of the blocking points were about *ash*. I had marked the powder sense as uncountable, but the plural *ashes* is real (*the house was in ashes*), and the entry contradicted itself: it said "no plural" while discussing *ashes*. I changed the grammar so the plural is recorded, made the usage note agree, widened the definition to cover ash from a volcano, and added the phrase *rise from the ashes*. In *claw*, the definition of the tool sense used the word *claw* itself, and I rewrote it. In *cupboard*, the usage note claimed that British English uses *cupboard* for every kind of storage space; that ignored *wardrobe*, so I rewrote it. Several idioms whose key noun is the headword were added: *jump to conclusions*, *in conclusion*, *a foregone conclusion*, *a red flag*, *a white flag*, *fly the flag*, *get your claws into someone*, *the cupboard is bare*, and the American sports phrase *put on a clinic*. The rejections: a request for a radiation sense of *dose* (a specialist use); one reviewer calling the *bark* translation note generic (it names a specific trap, though I removed a claim about word origin from it); and a request to drop an explanation that house rule 15 requires.

*Bark* and *ash* each have an unrelated twin with the same spelling: the sound a dog makes, and the tree. These need separate entries, and the queue cannot list a second entry with the same spelling and part of speech (a known problem with a note in the wiki). I listed both in `NEXT.md` so a later run claims them by hand, and queued the verb *bark*.

**Spend.** About US$0.47 for this cycle, and about US$8.08 of the US$15 daily cap so far.

**What did not work.** Nothing new failed.

**What is next.** More milestone-1 building. The lint pass (the periodic whole-dictionary check) is due in four runs.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*, which means rereading existing entries rather than writing new ones. There were no drafts waiting, so I took two items from the owner's rulings of 2026-10-24, in the order `NEXT.md` gives.

The first was ruling B4 (i). Phrasal verbs now have two grammar labels. *Separable* means the object can go between the verb and the particle, and a pronoun must go there (*give it up*). *Inseparable* means the object always comes after the whole verb (*go on about it*). The rule says the two labels enter the dictionary's fixed list of allowed values together with the first entries that use them. Those entries were *give up* and *go on*. Each also gained a missing use: *give someone up* (to the police) and *have nothing to go on* (no facts to base a decision on). Other phrasal verbs will get the labels as they are reread.

The second was the start of the coverage pass. This pass rereads the most important common words to check that no current meaning is missing. It begins with the content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs) that have the most senses. I took the six largest that had not been read since the reviewers started checking coverage: *take*, *go*, *line*, *break*, *see* and *cut*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 122 points on these eight entries, 13 of them blocking. I accepted 97 and rejected 25.

Missing meanings were added. *Go* now has *stay in a state* (*go hungry*, *go unnoticed*), split off from the "become" sense where it did not fit. *Break* gained *end a silence*, *of waves*, and *make someone lose hope* (*the wait broke him*). *Cut* gained *remove someone from a team*, the film use (*the scene cuts to the kitchen*), and the American *cut someone a check*. *See* gained *visit a place* (*see the sights*). *Line* gained the phrases *a fine line between* and *down the line*, and *go* gained *the same goes for*.

Several fixes made the entries agree with themselves. A false claim in *break* said that only *break*, not *beat*, works in the passive with a record. The definition "begin suddenly" was wrong for *dawn breaks*. *See fit* had a definition that added a meaning the phrase does not have. Two learner errors were the kind the house rules ban: *breaked* and *cutted*, which are made-up regular forms rather than mistakes learners are known to make. Both were removed. Many facts were stated in three or four fields at once, and the extra copies were cut.

Most rejections followed the keyword rule: an idiom is filed under its first noun. *Take place*, *take part*, *take care of* and *take advantage of* are already under those nouns, and *let me see* is under *let*. Four objections called "not used in continuous tenses" an invalid pattern, but it is a value on the dictionary's own fixed list.

**Spend.** About US$1.00 for this cycle; about US$9.08 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. These big entries take the reviewers several minutes each.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *strong*, *time*, *stand*, *sharp*, *set* and *keep* (listed in `NEXT.md`). Building milestone 1 goes on alongside it. The lint pass is due in three runs.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build*. The next twelve milestone-1 words were all nouns: *granddaughter*, *grandparent*, *grandson*, *greeting*, *guilt*, *harvest*, *herb*, *hint*, *honesty*, *inch*, *injection* and *jewelry*. *Jewelry* records the British spelling *jewellery* as a variant. Milestone 1 now has 172 words left.

The pronunciation check disagreed with two of my British transcriptions, for *grandson* and *grandparent*. I had written the *d* of *grand*, and British speakers normally drop it in these words. I rewrote both without the *d*, and the panel then confirmed them. The pronunciation notes I wrote cover two real traps: the silent *h* of *honesty*, and the American *herb* said without the *h*, which is why Americans write *an herb*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** There were 20 points (6 blocking). I accepted 19 and rejected 1. The blocking points were sound. In *hint*, my example *Can you give me a hint? I can't guess the answer* did not fit the sense I had put it under (showing what you mean without saying it). It is really a separate meaning, a clue that helps you find an answer, so *hint* now has that sense. In *injection*, the example *an injection of jobs* did not match a definition about money, and the figurative use is wider anyway (*an injection of energy*), so the definition was widened. In *inch*, the definition carried conversion facts: 12 inches in a foot, about two and a half centimeters. The 12-to-a-foot fact now lives in the usage note, and the phrase *every inch of something* moved out of the "small distance" sense, where it did not fit. Two idioms both reviewers asked for were added: *give someone an inch and they will take a mile* and *within an inch of your life*. The one rejection was *season's greetings*, which by the keyword rule belongs under *season*.

*Centimeter*, written in cycle 2, still has the same kind of conversion fact inside its definition. The reviewers did not raise it there, but for consistency I listed it in `NEXT.md` for a later review.

**Spend.** About US$0.43 for this cycle. The whole run used about US$2.49, and the day stands at about US$9.50 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** Milestone-1 building, the coverage pass (*strong*, *time*, *stand* and others), and the lint pass in two runs.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this run. The standing items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" are unchanged.
