# Report to the owner: 4 November 2026, evening run

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The automatic selector, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work by weighing how long each kind has waited, chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words still missing. It handed out twelve common nouns from band 2 (the second of five frequency bands, "common: words most intermediate learners know or need soon"), and all twelve were written, checked, and published: *chip*, *cigarette*, *cinema*, *circuit*, *circumstance*, *citizenship*, *clap*, *classmate*, *cleaner*, *cleaning*, *clerk*, and *click*. The dictionary now has 3,734 entries, every one of them reviewed. Before starting, the cycle checked for unfinished work from earlier runs (open pull requests, leftover branches, messages in the owner's inbox, and "Entry report" issues filed by readers) and found none.

**How each entry was checked.** Each entry went through the full pipeline. The validator checked the file's shape and house rules; the inflection tool generated the plural forms from rules; the pronunciation panel, three outside models plus the open CMU pronouncing dictionary, agreed with all 24 American and British transcriptions; and two reviewers from different companies (reviewer A, a Claude model, and reviewer B, a Google model) read every field. The vocabulary check confirmed that every definition uses only words from the dictionary's defining vocabulary or words that already have entries. Three first drafts failed that check and were reworded: *cookies* in a definition of *chip*, *source* in *circuit*, and *dentist* in *cleaning*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 60 issues in all. I applied 47, rejected 13, and sent none to you. Only two were "blocking", meaning the entry could not be published until they were settled, and both were the same problem: in the computer sense of *chip*, defined as a small part inside a machine, one example described a chip put under a dog's skin. The reviewer was right. I replaced it with an example about chips made for cars and phones.

The most useful minor findings:
- *chip*: the American sense was defined as a slice of potato, but the examples showed tortilla chips. The definition now covers other foods too. The reviewer also spotted that *bargaining chip* was missing, so it is now a subsense under the gambling sense.
- *circuit*: a sense for a court district, and the regular trip a judge makes around it, was missing and has been added.
- *cleaning*: *a good cleaning* (one time, countable) was not covered. The definitions also no longer use the word *clean*, which made them partly circular.
- *clerk*: *paper work* is now avoided altogether, and the American shop sense says *store*.
- *citizenship*: an example that described a school subject, not good behavior in a community, was replaced.

What I rejected, and why: reviewer B wanted *cigarette* defined with the word *tobacco*. *Tobacco* has no entry yet, so the vocabulary gate would refuse it; *dried leaves* is plain and accurate, and the entry links to *tobacco* for later. Reviewer B also asked for single rather than double asterisks around expressions such as **go to the movies**. The style guide uses double asterisks for any expression named as a word, so that was rejected. Reviewer A said the American/British usage note on *chip* only repeated the region labels. I kept the note, because the labels alone do not tell a learner that the two varieties swap the words (*chips* versus *crisps* and *French fries*).

**Links between entries.** The cross-reference tool adds a reverse link whenever a new entry names an existing one, but it could not decide which sense to attach three of them to, so I placed them by hand: *cinema* on *film* (the art and business sense), *circuit* on *track* (the racing sense), and *circumstance* on *condition* (the living-conditions sense). The tool also noticed that *assistant* lists *clerk* as a word to compare while *clerk* listed *assistant* as a synonym. I changed *clerk*'s link to "compare" so the two agree. Nine words named in the new entries have no entries yet (among them *crisp*, *French fry*, *tobacco*, *cigar*, *applause*, and *nationality*). They are now in the queue without a band, waiting for the next band vote.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: a second, closer reading of entries that have already been published. There are no unpublished drafts and no open reader reports, so the cycle went on with the coverage pass you ordered on 24 October. That pass rereads the most important band-1 entries (band 1 is the most common words), starting with those that have the most senses, and checks two things: that every current meaning a learner will meet is there, and that each example really fits its definition. This cycle read four six-sense entries: *fair* (adjective), *exercise* (noun), *escape* (verb), and *down* (adverb). That brings the pass to 192 entries.

**What was added.**
- *fair*: a subsense for following the rules of a game (*a fair tackle*); the old literary sense *beautiful*, labeled old-fashioned and literary, so the usage note no longer has to carry it alone; and the phrase *fair's fair*.
- *down*: four new senses. They are behind in a game (*two goals down*), finished from a list (*two exams down, one to go*), including everyone or everything (*down to the last detail*), and from an earlier time to a later one (*passed down from her grandmother*).
- *escape*: the "not noticed or remembered" sense now covers "not understood" (*the point of the joke escaped me*). Reviewer B pointed out that the first sense is also used with a direct object (*she escaped the burning car*), and that has been added.

**Duplicates cut.** The notes rule from 24 October says that one fact lives in one field. *Exercise* stated its countability trap in four places and its stress pattern in two, and now states each once. *Escape* and *down* each lost a note that only repeated another field. *Fair* lost a translator's note that only said the word has several meanings. Both reviewers rightly called that generic.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 51 issues. I applied 44, rejected 7, and sent none to you. All four blocking issues were applied:
- In *exercise*, "an exercise in patience" did not fit a definition about planned activities. The definition now covers an activity or situation that tests or shows a quality.
- *Exercise* also had an unnatural example (*leaders need the exercise of judgment*), which is now *requires*.
- *Fair* had the generic translator's note, now removed.
- *Escape* now records that the "get free" sense can be transitive.

Other fixes: an invented learner error in *escape* was removed; a learner error in *exercise* was replaced, because the old "incorrect" sentence was actually correct; the British spelling *half-time* became *halftime*; and an example making a health claim about fair skin was replaced. *Exercise* no longer lists *problem* as a word to compare, and the matching link on *problem* was removed too.

The rejections, with reasons logged: *fair game* already lives under *game*, because idioms go under their first noun. *Get down to work* is a phrasal verb, so it was queued as its own entry instead of becoming a sense of *down*. "Not used in the passive" is in the closed list of verb patterns, so the objection to it was mistaken. Two cross-references on *fair* (*clean*, *right*) were kept because they hold in ordinary use.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. I took eight band-2 nouns from the queue, fewer than the limit of twelve, so that each entry stayed full: *cliff*, *climbing*, *clip*, *clothing*, *clue*, *coach*, *coding*, and *coffee shop*. All eight went through the full pipeline (format check, generated plurals, the pronunciation panel, both reviewers, my adjudication, and the vocabulary and cross-reference checks) and are published as reviewed. The dictionary now has 3,742 entries. The pronunciation panel agreed with all 16 American and British transcriptions.

**One judgment call worth knowing about.** *Clip* is two unrelated words in English. One means to cut, and gives *a video clip*, *a quick clip* for a hedge, and *at a good clip* for speed. The other means to fasten, and gives *a paper clip* and *a hair clip*. The house rule is that unrelated words with the same spelling and part of speech get separate entries, never shared senses. This entry, `clip-n`, holds the cutting senses, because the existing *video* entry already links to *clip* as "a short video". The fastener is to be built later as `clip-n-2`. The queue cannot yet hold a second-homograph row, so it is listed in `NEXT.md` with the other homographs still to build. `clip-n` already points to it.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 26 issues. I applied 23, rejected 3, and sent none to you. Three were blocking, and all three were right:
- In *clip*, two blocking issues were the same example: a ball "gave him a clip on the head", which does not fit a sense defined as a hit with the hand. It is now *he got a clip round the ear for being rude*.
- In *clothing*, reviewer B noticed that the learner-error note told learners to fix *three new clothings* by using *clothes*. That would lead them to *three new clothes*, which is also wrong. The note now says only that you count it with *items of clothing*.

Smaller fixes: British phrasings in examples were replaced with American ones (*could do with*, *work out the clue*, *I haven't a clue*). The second sense of *coding* is now worded as an activity and no longer uses the word *code*. The definition of *clothing* no longer leans on *clothes*. The core idea of *coach* now names all six senses.

The three rejections:
- Reviewer A asked twice for **fell** to be marked in the *cliff* phrase examples (*sales fell off a cliff*). The headword is *cliff*, which already appears in the sentence, so no mark is needed.
- *Clip* keeps *video* as a synonym, because the *video* entry already links to *clip* with a note explaining the difference.

**Links between entries.** The cross-reference tool could not decide which sense of *car* should link back to the train-carriage sense of *coach*. I placed that link by hand on *car*'s "on a train" sense.
