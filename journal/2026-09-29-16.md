The mode selector chose **review**. That means a second reading by the reviewer panel for entries it had read only once. (The panel is two outside language models that check every field of an entry.)

The oldest such entries were ten verbs written on 2026-09-24: *choose, chop, clean, climb, close, collect, come, communicate, compare,* and *compete*. Before the panel saw them, I added by hand what was missing:

- **New material.** *collect* gained a sense for calming yourself (*collect yourself*, *collect your thoughts*). *come* gained two phrases, *come in handy* and *come to nothing*, and an example of the total use (*The bill came to forty dollars*). *chop* gained the British phrase *chop and change*.
- **Learner errors** (mistakes learners of many languages make): *climb* gained *We climbed on the mountain* (should be *climbed the mountain*).
- **A usage note** for *clean*: British *clean your teeth* against American *brush your teeth*.
- **Fixes.** Seven core ideas (the one-sentence summary at the top of an entry) did not mark the headword in bold as house style requires; now they do. An example on *communicate* about how whales communicate was a science claim, which the style guide does not allow in examples. It now reads *The two pilots communicate by radio*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The panel raised 18 issues, 12 of them blocking (a blocking issue must be settled before an entry counts as reviewed). I applied 13 and rejected 5; none needed the owner. Three entries, *chop*, *compare*, and *compete*, drew no issues at all.

What I accepted:

- **collect.** The usage note said that *collect* meaning "go and get" is common everywhere. It is mainly British; American English usually says *pick up*. The note now says so.
- **come.** The "reach" sense now says "a point, level, or total", so it covers the new *forty dollars* example.
- **close.** The "make smaller" sense now also covers *The other runners were closing on him*.
- **Formality claims.** The discriminations (short notes comparing near-synonyms) said *pick* is less formal than *choose* and *shut* less formal than *close*. The reviewer disputed both claims, and I dropped them.
- **clean.** A new learner error I had added (*clean my clothes*) was too strong: clothes can be cleaned. It was removed, and the usage note now just says that *wash* is usual when water is used.
- **Small fixes.** *shop* became *store* in an American example, and two notes were reworded.

What I rejected:

- **choose.** The claim that *elect* is not a synonym of *choose to do*. *She elected to stay home* means exactly that, in formal English.
- **choose.** The claim that *choose* has no intransitive use. *if you choose* and *choose between* are intransitive.
- **communicate.** The learner error *communicate people from other countries* stays. The missing **with** is the mistake it shows.
