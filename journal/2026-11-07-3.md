# Run report, 2026-11-07 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** Each cycle of a scheduled run starts by asking the selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks the kind of work most overdue) what to do. It chose `build`: write new entries from the queue of words still waiting for one. The limit it set was twelve entries and US$3 of paid review calls. Before that, the pre-flight checks found nothing stranded: no open pull request, no leftover branch other than `main`, nothing in the owner's inbox, and no open "Entry report" issues from readers.

The twelve words taken from the queue were all band-2 nouns (band 2 means "common": words most intermediate learners know or need soon). Each had been queued because an existing entry already points to it: *bookmark* (named by *favorite*), *ex-husband*, *fire truck*, *fireman*, *first* (the noun: *a first for me*, first gear, a British top-grade degree), *first aid*, *fist*, *fitness*, *flat* (the noun: a British home, a flat tire, the flat of the hand, mud flats, flat shoes, the music sign), *flaw*, *flexibility*, and *flour*. All twelve were written from scratch, checked, reviewed, and published as `reviewed`.

**The checks.** Every entry went through the full pipeline: the validator (schema and house-style rules), the inflection tool (plurals such as *firemen*, and no plural for uncountable nouns like *flour* and *first aid*), the pronunciation check (three outside models plus the open CMU pronouncing dictionary must agree; all 24 transcriptions were verified), and then the two-reviewer panel, in which two models from different companies read every field and return a closed verdict.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 29 issues, 4 of them "blocking" (the entry may not be published until each is settled). I applied 24 and rejected 5, with a reason logged for each in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`; none needed to go to the owner.

The real catches were on *flat*. I had defined the car sense as "a wheel whose rubber cover has lost its air"; the reviewer rightly said that a flat is the tire, not the wheel, and the definition now says so. The entry's one-sentence summary of how its senses connect (the "core idea") claimed the home sense was about being level, while an adaptation note (a note for future translators) said the two meanings were unrelated; the summary was rewritten and the translator note, which was generic anyway, removed. Smaller fixes: an example that paired British *flat* with American *elevator* was replaced; two collocations were filed under the wrong type; *the flat side of something* used the headword in its own definition and now says *level side*. Elsewhere, the old steam-engine sense of *fireman* was put into the present tense as house style requires, an example saying *driver* for a train became *engineer* (the American word), *health* was moved out of the synonyms of *fitness* into "see also", and *flour*'s note on sounding like *flower* now marks *flower* as a word.

I rejected: adding *at first* to the noun *first* (it is already a phrase in the adverb entry); moving the British home sense of *flat* below the American senses (it is the meaning learners meet most); calling a *toy fire truck* example a misfit; objecting to the grammar code "often before another noun" on *fitness*, which is a valid code; and calling *from the first* dated.

**What did not work.** The queue row for *ex-husband* had lost its hyphen (*ex husband*), a known tool fault recorded in `wiki/notes/` as `queue-hyphen-loss`; the entry was written with the correct spelling *ex-husband*. The tool that maintains two-way links reported that *flaw* listed *weakness* as a "compare" word in one sense and a synonym in another, while *weakness* lists *flaw* as a synonym; I removed the compare link.

**Spend.** US$0.34 for this cycle; the day's total is US$2.05 of the US$15 cap.

**What is next.** The selector decides the next cycle. New words used in examples or notes that have no entry yet (for example *heel*, *tire*, *clench*) were added to the queue as candidates for later.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle. The standing items are unchanged: pronunciation disputes and the ten-entry samples in the weekly digests are waiting for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** After cycle 1 merged, the selector chose `build` again, with the same limits: twelve entries and US$3. It took the next twelve words from the queue, all band-2 nouns: *fluid*, *follower*, *forecast*, *foreigner*, *format*, *formula*, *fortune*, *foundation*, *fountain*, *framework*, *freezer*, and *French fry*. All twelve went through the full pipeline and are published as `reviewed`. The inflection tool took the irregular plural of *formula* from the exceptions table (*formulas*, also *formulae*). All 24 pronunciations were verified by the panel.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 39 issues, 2 of them blocking. I applied 36 and rejected 3; nothing went to the owner.

Both blocking issues were example sentences that stated a science fact, which house rules forbid: *Air behaves like a fluid...* and *The chemical formula for water is H2O*. Both were replaced with neutral sentences.

The most useful finds were gaps in coverage, meaning senses a learner would meet that the entry did not have. *Follower* now has a sense for someone who keeps up with a sport, team, or subject (*a follower of the local team*), separate from a follower of a leader or a religion. *Formula* gained a subsense for a pattern used so often that the result is predictable (*the movie follows the usual formula*), labeled disapproving. *Fountain* gained *a fountain of stories* (a rich source of something), and *fortune* gained the phrase *seek your fortune*, alongside *a small fortune*, which the baton (`NEXT.md`, the file that passes tasks from one run to the next) had asked for. The reviewers also caught British wording in examples where American English is the house variety: *at the weekend*, *tins* of baby formula, and *a pack of cards* became *this weekend*, *cans*, and *a deck of cards*. Smaller fixes: several collocations were filed under the wrong type, *framework* now takes *provides* rather than *gives*, two countability codes were widened to "countable or uncountable" (*in digital format*, *liquid foundations*), and *french fry* with a small *f* is now recorded as a spelling variant instead of only being mentioned in a note.

I rejected adding a rare sense of *formula* (a fixed form of words, as in *a legal formula*), which a learner at this level seldom needs. I also rejected a request to mark *French fries* by hand in examples, because the plural is already listed and the site marks it automatically. And I kept *fluid* in the "drink plenty of fluids" sense as a countable noun with the code "usually plural".

**What did not work.** The tool that keeps cross-references two-way could not place two back-links by itself. The *follower* sense for a fan pointed at *fan*, but the existing *fan* entry is the machine that moves air. The enthusiast sense of *fan* is an unrelated word, so it needs its own entry, `fan-n-2`, which does not exist yet. I pointed *follower* at that future entry and added it to the hand-claim list in `NEXT.md`. The other back-link, from *leader* (sense 3, a company that is more successful than others) to *follower* as its opposite, I placed by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.37 for this cycle, US$2.42 for the day so far, against a US$15 daily cap.

**What is next.** The selector picks the next cycle's work. New words used in examples and notes that have no entries yet were queued as usual.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose `review` this time: a second reading of entries that have been reviewed only once. The owner's ruling E2 sets the order. Drafts come first (there were none), then open entry reports (none), then the "coverage pass" over the most important band-1 words. That pass rereads each entry asking one question: does it cover every sense, phrase, and use that a learner will meet? It takes the entries with the most senses first. This cycle took the next four five-sense entries in alphabetical order: *day* (noun), *detail* (noun), *direction* (noun), and *dirty* (adjective).

**What I added before the reviewers saw them.** *Day* gained three common idioms, *at the end of the day*, *win the day*, and *someone's days are numbered*, plus the pattern *twice a day*. *Detail* gained the decorative use (*leather details on the pockets*). *Dirty* gained a sense for dull colors (*dirty blond hair*, *a dirty yellow coat*). Three definitions that said "you" now say "someone", as house style requires.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The panel raised 48 issues, 8 of them blocking. I applied 44 and rejected 4; nothing went to the owner.

Three of the blocking issues were example sentences that stated facts about the world: owls sleeping by day, birds' sense of direction, coal as a dirty fuel. House rules forbid examples that make checkable claims, so all three were replaced. Two more were definitions of *day* that used the word *day* itself: the working-hours sense and the special-day sense. Both were rewritten, the second as "a particular date on which something happens or is celebrated". The *day off* example sat under the working-hours sense, which it contradicts, so it moved to the twenty-four-hour sense. A reviewer also noticed that *What day is it today?* asks for a day of the week, which no sense covered, so *day* now has a subsense for the days of the week.

Other changes: *direction* now gives directing a film or play its own subsense, apart from managing a group. *Detail*'s decorative use became a sense of its own, because it does not follow from "a part of a picture". In three entries, a usage or adaptation note repeated a fact already given in a learner-error item. The project's rule is one fact, one field, so the repeats were cut. One learner error on *day* (*in the other day*) looked invented and was removed. The reviewers also found that *pure* is not a real opposite of *dirty* in the not-clean sense, so that link was removed from both entries.

I rejected four objections. One reviewer wanted the special-day sense of *day* merged into the first sense; I kept it because it has its own set of word partners (*wedding day*, *on the day*, *the big day*). A request to move *someday*'s spelling out of the phrase explanation was rejected: a phrase has no other place to hold it. A request to turn *a dirty look* into a phrase was rejected: it is a use of the adjective with its own grammar. And *play dirty* and *do someone's dirty work* belong under *play* and *work*, by the rule that files an idiom under its first noun, or else its first verb.

**Spend.** US$0.22 for this cycle, US$2.64 for the day.

**What is next.** The coverage pass goes on from *divide* (verb). The selector decides whether the next cycle reviews or builds.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
