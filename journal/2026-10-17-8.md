Points of note:

- ***today*** and ***tomorrow***: the adverb entries cover saying when something happens (*I'm busy today*). Their usage notes point to the noun entries for *Today is Monday* and *today's news*, matching the existing *yesterday* pair. Both warn about common errors: *on tomorrow*, and *today morning* for *this morning*.
- ***too***: the entry separates four uses: *more than enough* (*too hot to drink*), *also* (*me too*), *not too bad*, and the American reply *I did too!* It explains the choice between *too*, *also*, and *either*.
- ***therefore***: the entry warns that it is not a conjunction. *It was late, therefore we went home* should be *so*, or use a semicolon.
- ***through***: the entry lists the informal American spelling *thru* as a variant. Its usage note points to the preposition, and to an adjective entry (*Are you through?*), which is now queued.
- ***under***: the entry pairs *twelve and under* with *eighteen and over* in the existing *over* entry.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 22 issues and reviewer-b 3. Two entries drew none (*therefore*, *unfortunately*). I applied 14 and rejected 11.

Applied, among others:

- ***too***: my notes said *too* with an adjective is negative in meaning. reviewer-a gave five issues on this, and it was right that the claim was too absolute (*too good to miss*). The notes now say *too* **usually** means more than is good. The learner error *The food was too good; I loved it* stays, because using *too* for *very* is a very common mistake. Its note now explains the intended meaning rather than calling *too good* wrong.
- ***under***: *twelve and under* includes twelve, while the definition said *less than*. The explanation now says *twelve and under* means twelve or less. *Over* was removed as the opposite of the *below* sense (it stays for *and over*), and an *informal* label came off the medical sense (*go under*).
- ***truly***: sense 1 now reads *very, or really*; both reviewers objected to the longer wording.
- ***through***: the phone sense now covers *get through* as well as *put someone through*.
- Smaller fixes: the usage notes of *today* and *tomorrow* were reworded, *length* was added to *twice the size*, and the note on *totally* as a reply no longer says *among young people*.

Rejected:

- Region labels where the reviewer said the use is found everywhere: *go through to the final* stays British (American English says *advance*), and *yours truly* as a letter ending stays American. A region label here means *mainly used there*.
- *Until today* stays a noun use, matching the existing *today* and *yesterday* noun entries.
- *Together* meaning *at the same time* keeps that plain definition. *Totally* keeps *completely*.
- *Double* (verb) stays under *see also* for *twice*.
- reviewer-b suggested a predeterminer entry for *twice the size*; our part-of-speech list has no such value. It also suggested moving *go under* to a phrasal verb; house practice keeps verb + adverb pairs in the adverb entry.

## My own checks

Three definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (*romantic*, *sincere*, *beneath*), so I reworded them. The cross-reference tool adds back-links on the target's first sense, and two landed wrong:

- *truly* went under *really* meaning *in fact*. I moved it by hand to *really* meaning *very*.
- *thus* went under *so* meaning *very*. *So* (adverb) has no *in this way* sense, so I removed the synonym and its back-link.

Three pairs disagreed about the kind of link (synonym, opposite, or word to compare); I changed mine to match the older entries. The queue guessed *beneath* as a noun from my first wording; that row is marked as a duplicate of the preposition.
