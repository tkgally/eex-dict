# 2026-10-03 (04:43 UTC run, cycle 2): twelve new nouns, *disease* to *dream*

## What this run did

This was the second cycle of the run that started at 04:43 UTC. The first cycle, a second review of twelve older entries, merged as pull request #198. The mode selector (the script that chooses each cycle's kind of work) then picked **build**: new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). It allowed twelve, and the queue gave the next twelve common nouns in alphabetical order: *disease, dish, display, distance, divorce, doctor, dog, door, doubt, download, drawer,* and *dream*.

I wrote each entry from my own knowledge and the house style guide. Each has a definition for every sense, examples, collocations (typical word combinations), a usage note (a short paragraph on a point of use), and learner errors (common mistakes shown with a correction) where they help. In all, the twelve entries have 27 senses, 25 idioms or fixed phrases, and 18 learner errors.

Some of the content:

- **Phrases**, each placed under its keyword by the house rule (the first noun in the phrase): *on display*, *keep your distance*, *within walking distance*, *go the distance*, *just what the doctor ordered*, *let sleeping dogs lie*, *work like a dog*, *next door*, *behind closed doors*, *show someone the door*, *no doubt*, *without a doubt*, *cast doubt on something*, *a dream come true*, *in your dreams*, and others.
- **A synonym discrimination** (a paragraph on how to choose between near-synonyms) for *disease*, *illness*, and *sickness*.
- **Learner errors** such as *I saw a dream* (in English you *have* a dream), *How much distance is it to the station?* (say *How far...?*), *He has a heart's disease* (say *heart disease*), *Someone knocked the door* (*knocked on*), and *I have a doubt about the homework* for a question, which some varieties of English allow but most do not.
- **Notes on use**, for example that *no doubt* on its own usually means only "probably", that *drawer* is one syllable for most American speakers and rhymes with *door*, and that people usually call their own dog *he* or *she*, not *it*.

Before review, all 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the panel of models and, for American English, by an open pronouncing dictionary.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 21 issues. Nine were *blocking*, which means I had to decide each one before the entries could be marked as reviewed. I applied 14 decisions and rejected 7. None needed to go to the owner. Every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The reviewers were right about these:

- I had listed *meal* as a synonym of *dish* in its food sense, while the usage note said that a dish is not a whole meal. The synonym is gone.
- *Doctor* sense 2 said "the highest degree that a university gives". That is not true everywhere, and the translators' note repeated it. The definition now describes the degree by the research it takes, and the note says *Dr.* "may be" used for such a person.
- Two of my learner errors were sometimes correct English: *I'll wash the dish* (right when there is only one) and *She closed the door with the key*. Both were removed.
- My synonym paragraph called *sickness* less formal than *illness*, which is not reliable; that claim is gone.
- A *download* can come from any network, not only the internet.

I rejected the rest. Most were formatting points the house style already settles: a phrase named as a word, such as *How far...?*, takes the bold mark, and quoted fragments in prose stay lowercase. I also kept the label "American" on *dog* meaning a poor product; a region label in this dictionary means the use is *mainly* found there, not only there.

Two of my definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions should use): *separation* in *divorce* sense 2 and *advanced* in *doctor* sense 2. The vocabulary check caught both, and I reworded them.
