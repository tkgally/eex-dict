# 2026-10-17, cycle 3 of the 00:44 UTC run: the thirty-eighth originality check

## What this cycle did

This was an **originality** cycle. Every tenth run, the scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) forces a check that our wording has not drifted toward a published dictionary's. This was run 450, so the check was due. The tool (`tools/originality_check.py`) picked ten fields at random from finished entries: eight definitions, one phrase definition, and one usage note. For each, I searched the web for the exact wording and looked at whether any published dictionary or word list shows the same or nearly the same text. I also asked one outside reviewer model (reviewer-a) whether each definition reads as copied. The verdicts go in `reviews/originality/2026-10-17.md`.

## What the searches found

Five fields are **original**: no published wording was close (*heavy* sense 3, *other* the pronoun, *love* in the sense *want very much*, *a change of scene*, and the usage note on *weigh*).

Three are **generic overlaps**: short, plain definitions that any dictionary would write in much the same way. *Show* as *a program on television or the radio* is about as plain as English allows. *Examine* as *test what someone knows by asking questions* and the *fence* idiom *become friendly again with someone after an argument* are similar to other dictionaries' plain frames but not their distinctive wording.

Two needed a **rewrite**:

- ***supper***, sense 1, read *the meal that people eat in the evening, especially a simple one eaten at home*. A learner's dictionary defines it as *the evening meal; especially, an informal meal eaten at home*. Ours had the same shape (*especially ... eaten at home*) with one word changed. It now reads *the meal that you eat in the evening*. The point about home and formality already lives in the entry's note comparing *supper*, *dinner*, and *tea*, written in our own words.
- ***rule***, sense 1, read *an instruction that says what people are allowed or not allowed to do in a game, a place, or an organization*. Two learner's dictionaries frame a rule as an *instruction* that *tells you what you are allowed or are not allowed to do* in *a particular game or situation*. Ours followed that frame. It now reads *something that people in a game, a place, or a group must do or must not do*.

A rewrite counts as a review of the entry, so both went to the two reviewers again.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

On the rewrites, reviewer-a raised 14 issues and reviewer-b none. I applied 3 and rejected 11.

Applied: my first rewrite of *rule* said *everyone ... must*, but a rule can apply to only some people; *everyone* is gone. On *supper*, I had added a short explanation that the reviewer rightly said this ordinary noun does not need; it is removed. The note comparing meal names now says that in some families and regions *dinner* is the midday meal.

Rejected: the reviewer said British *supper* is never a light snack before bed; it is (*hot milk and toast for supper*), so that sense and its British label stay. It also said *during his rule* contradicts the uncountable grammar of that sense, but uncountable nouns take a possessive freely (*his leadership*, *his rule*). Smaller objections on *rule* (a cross-reference to *government*, the family link to *ruler*, a learner error) were rejected with reasons.

## What reviewer-a said about copying

reviewer-a answered *copied* for all ten fields, nine times naming the same learner's dictionary. Most of its "quotes" could not be found by search. For *rule* its quote was close to a real frame, which search confirmed independently; for *supper* the real match was in a different dictionary from the one it named. This is the pattern recorded in `wiki/notes/reviewer-noise.md` (updated with this check): search alone decides the verdicts.
