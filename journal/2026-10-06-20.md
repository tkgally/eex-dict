## What this cycle did

Every tenth run, the scheduler forces an **originality check**. The dictionary is written without consulting published dictionaries. This check looks for any wording that ended up matching one anyway. It takes ten random pieces of prose from the dictionary and searches the web for each as an exact phrase. It also asks one of the outside reviewer models whether the text reads as copied.

Each field gets one of three verdicts:

- **original**: no match.
- **generic overlap**: a short, plain wording that any dictionary would write the same way. Left alone.
- **rewrite**: the field matches a published dictionary's distinctive wording. It is rewritten.

This time the sample held seven definitions, two usage notes, and one idiom definition:

- the usage note of *oven* (oven, stove, and British *cooker*);
- *throw*: "confuse or surprise someone so that they do not know what to say or do";
- *platform* at a train station;
- the usage note of *pedal* (*gas pedal* and *accelerator*);
- the idiom *out to lunch* (under *lunch*);
- *hour*: "a particular time of the day or night";
- *driver*, the computer program;
- *edge* in someone's voice;
- *class*: all the students who finish school in the same year;
- *cut*: stopping an engine or a supply of power or water.

## What the searches found

Six came back **original**: the searches found only shopping guides, forums, and blogs.

Three are **generic overlaps**:

- *throw*: dictionary pages for *nonplus* describe the same idea in other words.
- *hour*: one dictionary has the near frame "any specific time of day".
- *cut*: learner's pages for *shut off* use the plain frame "stop a supply of", but not our wording.

None of the three is distinctive enough to change.

One needed a **rewrite**: *out to lunch*. Our definition read "not paying attention, or acting in a strange way". Two searches both brought up dictionary-style pages, and the search summaries gave nearly our exact wording. I did not open the pages. As in earlier checks, a match in the search was reason enough to change it. It now reads "with your mind somewhere else, so that you do not notice what is going on around you". A one-line explanation adds that the phrase can also mean someone seems a little crazy, and a second example shows the common school use (*I was out to lunch in class and missed the homework*).

A rewrite counts as a review of that entry, so *lunch* went through the panel again. One reviewer raised one issue, about a translators' note that was already there. The note said that *dinner* for the midday meal is British, while "most of the English-speaking world" uses *lunch*. In fact the midday *dinner* is also heard in some other places, rural American speech among them. I applied the point: the note now says "in parts of Britain and in some other places", and drops the claim about most of the world.
