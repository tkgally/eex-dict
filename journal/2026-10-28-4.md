# Run report, 2026-10-28 (fourth run of the day)

## Cycle 1: review

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from what is most overdue) chose *review*: a second reading of entries that already exist. There were no unreviewed drafts and no reader reports open on GitHub, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That pass, which you asked for on 2026-10-24, rereads the most important everyday words (band 1, the most frequent of the five frequency levels), starting with those that have the most senses, and asks one question of each: does the entry cover every meaning a learner will actually meet? Four entries were read: **position** (noun), **part** (noun), **out** (adverb), and **open** (adjective). Seventy-seven entries have now had this reading.

**What was added.**

- **position**: a new sense for a person's importance or rank in a society or a group (*a position of power*, *a special position in the family*); a military sub-sense (*the enemy positions*); and the common negative *be in no position to do something*.
- **part**: a sub-sense for a numbered section of a book or series (*the story continues in part two*); *do your part*; two phrases, *the better part of something* (*the better part of an hour*) and the informal *in these parts*.
- **out**: a sub-sense for reaching away from the body (*hold out your hand*), and three new senses: giving or sending to many people (*hand out the tests*), removing something (*cross out the wrong words*), and doing something completely (*clean out the garage*, *the concert sold out*).
- **open**: sub-senses for "not covered" (*an open fire*, *an open wound*), "spread out" (*the book lay open*), and "shown without hiding it" (*open anger*); and two new senses, "available" (*Is the job still open?*) and "at risk" (*open to criticism*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Each changed entry went to the two automated reviewers (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, a Google model). They raised 50 points, 2 of them marked blocking, both about the same example in **position**: *She was in the lead position for most of the race*, which is not how people talk. It now reads *in first position*. Of the 50 decisions, 38 applied the reviewer's point and 12 rejected it with a written reason. Applied changes included removing the "formal" label from the job and opinion senses of **position** (both are ordinary language), tightening the opinion definition to a view stated in public, a clearer definition of an acting part, and removing notes that repeated another field in all four entries (the house rule is one fact, one field). In **out**, the note about *out of* before a noun had been said two different ways; it now lives once, in the learner error, which also names *out the door* and *out the window* as the normal exceptions. The American label on the "outdoors" sense of **out** was removed, since *out in the garden* is used everywhere. Rejected points included requests to add idioms whose keyword is another noun (*with open arms* and *keep an open mind* are already under **arm** and **mind**), to move *jockey for position* to a verb entry (its first noun is *position*), and to mark **part** in *part of the money* as uncountable (it is a use without an article, which the usage note already explains).

Three cross-references that a script could not place automatically were placed by hand: **open** now appears as the opposite of **private** (sense 1) and as a synonym of **available** (sense 1) and **free** (the "not used" sense).

## Cycle 2: originality

**What this cycle did.** The selector forced an *originality check*, which runs every twentieth run. Its purpose is to make sure our wording has not drifted toward a published dictionary's. A script picked ten fields at random from across the dictionary (mostly definitions, one usage note), and each was searched on the web as an exact phrase. Reviewer B was also asked whether each one reads as copied; it is told to answer "copied" only if it can quote the matching published text.

**What was found.** Six fields matched nothing and are original. Two overlapped with published wording only in the way any plain definition of the same thing would: the phrase *global village* and the sense of **feel** meaning to search with your hands in the dark. Two were close enough that I rewrote them:

- **spell** (verb), the sense in *spell trouble* and *spell disaster*. Ours said *be a sign that something, usually something bad, will happen*; a learner's dictionary defines a different word, *portend*, with the same frame. It now reads *mean that a particular result, usually a bad one such as trouble or failure, is likely to follow*. That also fits the examples better, since *spell the end* is about a result, not a warning.
- **make a practice of something**, a phrase in **practice** (noun). Ours said *do something regularly, as a habit*, which a learner-dictionary site paraphrases almost word for word. It now reads *do something often because you have decided it is a good thing to do*.

Reviewer B answered "original" six times and "cannot tell" four times and quoted nothing, so, as in earlier checks, the web searches decided.

**What the reviewers found in the rewritten entries.** Both entries went through the full two-reviewer panel, which read every field, not only the rewritten ones. There were 19 minor points; 18 were applied and 1 rejected. In **spell**, the fact that the British past form is *spelt* was stated twice in different words and now lives only in the inflection note; the second sense ("the letters spell a name") got a clearer definition with its subject named in the explanation, and the summary at the top of the entry now says that the result is usually bad. In **practice**, *habit* was moved from "synonyms" to "compare", with a one-line difference, and the matching link in **habit** was changed too; an example about tipping in restaurants (a custom that differs between countries) was replaced; the doctor-and-lawyer sense now covers the work itself (*the practice of medicine*), not only the business; the "formal" label on *make a practice of* was removed; and *My French is out of practice*, which people do not say, became *The team is out of practice after the long break*. I rejected the claim that *spell* in this sense is often used in continuous forms: it is rare, and the "not continuous" code helps learners.
