# Report for 2026-11-22 (second run)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from what is most overdue) chose *build*: new entries taken from the queue of words waiting to be written, at most twelve. Twelve band-2 nouns were claimed (band 2 is the second most important of five editorial frequency groups), and all twelve were written and finished: *terror*, *thanks*, *theme*, *therapy*, *third*, *tide*, *tiger*, *times*, *timetable*, *tin*, *tire*, and *toast*. The dictionary now has 4,679 entries, all reviewed.

Before the work, the run checked for unfinished work left by earlier runs. There were no open pull requests (proposed changes waiting to be merged) and no open entry reports from readers. The four old working branches on GitHub hold nothing that is not already in the main copy, and each is already listed in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` for deletion, which this tool set cannot do. The inbox was empty.

**The entries.** Several of these words have uses that a learner can easily miss, and the entries try to cover them:

- *thanks* and *times* are nouns with no singular. *Thanks* holds the idioms *thanks to* (because of) and *no thanks to someone*; *times* covers *in ancient times* and *times are hard*, plus *move with the times*.
- *tin* and *timetable* have British senses (a food tin, a baking tin, a school timetable), labeled as British, with the American words (*can*, *pan*, *schedule*) linked as synonyms.
- *tire* records the British spelling *tyre* as a variant and says plainly that the noun has nothing to do with the verb *tire* (become tired).
- *toast* covers both the bread and the drink in someone's honor, with the phrases *propose a toast*, *the toast of the town*, and the slang *be toast* (be in trouble).
- *terror* has four senses, including political violence and the half-joking *a little terror* for a badly behaved child.

Every inflected form (plurals) was produced by the rules script and checked, and all 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and the open pronouncing dictionary.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (two language models from different companies that read every field of every entry) raised 102 issues, 15 of them blocking (an entry cannot be finished until a blocking issue is settled). I accepted 87 and rejected 11, with a reason recorded for each. The blocking issues were real and were all fixed:

- Examples on *tiger* and *tin* stated checkable facts (when tigers hunt, how many are left in the wild, what tin is mixed with to make bronze). House rules forbid facts in examples, so these were replaced with ordinary sentences.
- *Third* in the gear sense was defined as "the third gear", which uses the word to define itself. It now says "the gear that comes after second, used for medium speeds".
- *Tire* listed the verb *tire* in its word family while also saying the two words are unrelated. The link was removed.
- *Be toast* was drafted as a noun sense with an adjective-like definition. It is now a phrase, as one reviewer suggested.
- *Theme* had no sense for the colors and style of a screen (*a dark theme*). One was added, along with the phrase *a variation on a theme*.
- *Times* had an unnatural example (*in my grandparents' times*) and *timetable* had *behind the timetable* (people say *behind schedule*). Both were replaced.

Most rejections were requests to add idioms that the house rule files under a different word: *reign of terror* goes under *reign*, *paper tiger* under *paper*, *behind the times* stays under *time*, and *a sign of the times* moves to *sign* (that is noted in the baton file, `NEXT.md`, for the next time *sign* is reviewed). I also rejected a request to label the travel sense of *timetable* as British, because American English uses *train timetable* too.

Cross-references were brought into step in both directions. Six links the script could not place were placed by hand, and *theme* now lists *subject* and *topic* as words to compare rather than as synonyms.

**Spend.** US$0.40 for the review panel and the pronunciation check. The day's total is US$1.30 of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. My first drafts put too many grammar points in the explanation field and repeated the same fact in two fields; both reviewers caught this, and those copies were removed.

**What is next.** The selector chooses the next cycle. The routine check of the whole dictionary (lint) is due after one more cycle.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle. The pronunciation disputes and the ten-entry samples in the weekly digests are still waiting for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: site

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *site*: rebuilding the public website from the entries and checking it in a real browser. No new entries were written in this cycle, and nothing was paid for.

The build script (`tools/build_site.py`) turned the 4,679 entries into 3,893 headword pages. A headword page holds every entry with the same spelling, so *toast* the noun and any later *toast* the verb share one page. No entry was held back as an unfinished draft, and there are no renamed entries that would need a redirect page. The built site goes into the `docs/` folder. That folder is never committed to the repository; GitHub's automatic build makes the public copy from the entries after this pull request merges.

The check script (`tools/site_check.py`) then opened the site in a headless browser (a browser that runs without a screen and is driven by a script) at a phone width of 360 pixels and at a desktop width. It ran 79 checks, and all 79 passed. Among the things it confirmed:

- no page scrolls sideways on a phone; all 3,899 entry and key pages were tested;
- searching for an inflected form (*ran*) finds its headword (*run*) first, and pressing Enter opens it;
- a phrase can be found by its words alone (*a catch in voice* finds *a catch in someone's voice*);
- a misspelling (*abadnon*) gets a "Did you mean" suggestion (*abandon*);
- a page address that does not exist gives a styled "not found" page that searches for the word;
- hovering on a linked word shows a short preview of its entry;
- the translator's view, which shows the adaptation notes, can be switched on and off and stays as set after the page is reloaded;
- dark mode has a dark background and causes no script errors;
- a cross-reference to a word with no entry yet is shown in plain grey, with a short note explaining what grey means.

I also checked by hand that the new pages from cycle 1 are there. The *toast* page shows the phrase *be toast*, and the *tire* page shows the British spelling *tyre*.

**What the reviewers found.** No review was run in this cycle: the site mode reads the entries and does not change them.

**Spend.** US$0. The day's total is still US$1.30 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing. No template or tool needed a fix.

**What is next.** The routine check of the whole dictionary (lint) is now due. Lint is a pass over all entries and wiki pages for broken links, unplaced cross-references, word caps, and contradictions between pages. The selector will most likely choose it next.

## Cycle 3: lint

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *lint*. It was forced, because ten cycles had passed since the last one. Lint is the regular check of the whole dictionary and its wiki (the project's own rule and note pages). It has a mechanical part, done by scripts, and a part done by reading. This was the hundred-and-eighteenth pass.

**The mechanical checks.**

- **Word caps.** Every capped file (the rule files, `NEXT.md`, the style guide, the wiki) is under its limit.
- **Links.** All relative links inside the wiki resolve.
- **Cross-references.** A cross-reference is a link from one entry to another, such as a synonym or a word to compare. When entry A names entry B, B should name A back, and the script adds the missing mirror links. This time it added one: *tiger* now lists *lion* as a word to compare, matching what *lion* already said. I then took *lion* out of *tiger*'s "see also" list so the link is not shown twice.
- **Links placed by hand.** The script cannot always tell which sense of the other word a link belongs to. Two such pairs were left for a person, and I placed both: *technique* sense 1 (a way of doing something) now names *process*, and *third* sense 1 (one of three equal parts) now names *quarter*.
- **Defining vocabulary.** No definition uses a word outside the defining vocabulary (the list of words definitions are written in) unless that word has an entry. Nothing new was queued.
- **Claim files.** Five used-up claim files were removed. A claim file is the record a cycle keeps of which words it is working on, so that two runs do not write the same word.

**The band vote.** Twenty-nine queue rows had no frequency band yet. They were words named in other entries and added to the queue since the last vote. The three word-list models voted on them, at a cost of under one cent. No vote was split by more than one band, so the lower middle vote was taken each time, and all 29 rows are now ready to be built.

**The reports.** These are lists for later review cycles; nothing here is an error.

- 1,060 cross-reference pairs where the link might belong on a different sense;
- 317 definitions that use their own headword, most of them harmless (*take a dog for a walk* under *walk*);
- 7 entries that promise an entry that does not exist yet;
- 36 places where two entries link to each other under different kinds of link (one says synonym, the other says compare). These are already described in a wiki note and were left alone.

**Reviewer precision.** Precision here means the share of a reviewer's flagged issues that I accepted. Reviewer A is at 0.85 overall and reviewer B at 0.84. Under the switch-off rule, an issue type a reviewer gets right less than 30 percent of the time, over twenty or more decisions, is turned off for that reviewer. No new type fell under that line. The three types already turned off stay off, and reviewer B's formatting checks stay on by your earlier ruling. I recorded the figures in `wiki/notes/reviewer-precision.md`.

**The reading part.** I read the wiki index against the pages, and every page is listed. There are no open questions for you. The curator file (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`) has no duplicate lines, and the log has an entry for every cycle.

**Spend.** US$0.004 for the band vote. The day's total is US$1.30 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing new. The band-vote tool still writes the unbanded vote to a different file name than its next two steps expect by default, so those steps fail unless the file is named by hand. I added this to the existing note on the band-vote tool (`wiki/notes/band-vote-unbanded-overwrite.md`) for a later fix.

**What is next.** Lint is next due after ten more cycles. The queue's next build words follow *toast* (`NEXT.md`).

## Cycle 4: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. The limit was twelve new entries, but I claimed eight. This was the run's last cycle, and the smaller batch left room to finish the review and the merge properly; the twelve is a ceiling, not a target. The eight words came from the front of the queue (the list of words waiting to be written). Several were added to the queue only this morning, as words named by the entries written in cycle 1, and the band vote in cycle 3 then gave them their frequency bands. The eight are *cookie*, *target* (the verb), *cooperation*, *panic*, *Thanksgiving*, *theme park*, *tobacco*, and *toddler*. All eight were finished, and the dictionary now has 4,687 entries, all reviewed.

**The entries.**

- *cookie* has the food sense, the computer sense (a small file a website stores), and the informal *a tough cookie* or *a smart cookie*. It also has the phrase *that's the way the cookie crumbles*. A usage note explains that British English mostly says *biscuit* for the food.
- *target* (verb) has three senses: choose something to attack, aim a product or message at a group, and aim for a level or result. Its forms (*targeted*, *targeting*) were checked against the spelling rules; the **t** is not doubled.
- *cooperation* records the British spelling *co-operation* and the set phrase *Thank you for your cooperation*.
- *Thanksgiving* is written with a capital letter for the holiday and a small letter for the religious sense (prayers that show you are grateful to God).
- All 16 pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, and by the open pronouncing dictionary wherever it lists the word.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 32 issues, 6 of them blocking. I accepted 30 and rejected 2.

- The core idea of *cookie* (the one-sentence summary at the top of an entry with three or more senses) did not fit the computer sense. The person sense was defined only as "a person of a particular kind", which tells a learner nothing. Both are rewritten.
- *cookie* listed *cookbook* in its word family, but *cookbook* comes from *cook*. It was removed.
- An example under *cooperation* in the sense "working together" (*The police asked for cooperation*) really showed the other sense, "willingness to help". It was replaced.
- *panic* had a subsense that defined the compound *panic attack* rather than *panic* itself. *A panic attack* is now a collocation (a typical word combination) instead.

The two rejections:

- Reviewer B wanted the religious sense of *thanksgiving* marked countable. The countable use is rare and old, so I kept it uncountable.
- One reviewer asked again to move a variant of a phrase (*hit the panic button*) out of the explanation. Phrases have no separate field for variants, so the explanation holds them by house practice.

The lint check on defining vocabulary then caught two definition words that are not allowed: *Canada* (a country name is not on the closed list of names) and *cigars*. Both definitions were reworded, and Canada's own Thanksgiving is now mentioned in the culture note for translators instead. Three cross-reference back-links were placed by hand: *aim*, *fear*, and *baby* now point back to *target*, *panic*, and *toddler*.

**Spend.** US$0.23. The day's total is US$1.53 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke.

**What is next.** The next build continues the queue after *toddler*. The idiom list in `NEXT.md` is unchanged except that *a sign of the times* now waits for `sign-n`.
