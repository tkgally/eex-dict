# 2026-09-29 (8) — Build: twelve verbs, *stick* to *suppose*

## What this run did

This was the fourth and last cycle of the scheduled run that began at 06:43 UTC. A run has at most four cycles. The earlier cycles merged as pull requests #130 (review of twelve conjunctions), #131 (lint pass), and #132 (twelve verbs, *smell* to *steal*).

The mode selector chose **build** again, so I wrote the next twelve band-1 verbs from the queue: *stick, stir, stop, strengthen, stretch, strip, study, suffer, suggest, supply, support,* and *suppose*. Each entry has:

- senses with signposts;
- grammar and verb patterns;
- examples and collocations;
- learner errors;
- adaptation notes for translators;
- where the choice is a real problem, a note on how to choose between near-synonyms.

Highlights:

- ***stop*** has six senses. They run from *stop talking* to *stop someone from going* and *stop at the bank on the way home*. It has the phrases *stop at nothing* and *stop short of something*. Its usage note explains the difference between *He stopped smoking* (he gave it up) and *He stopped to smoke* (he paused so that he could smoke).
- ***support*** has seven senses. They include holding up a roof, supporting a family with money, the British sense of supporting a sports team, and a computing sense (*this phone doesn't support the app*). It has a warning for learners whose languages have a word like *support* meaning *put up with*: *I can't support this noise* should be *I can't stand this noise*.
- ***suppose*** covers *I suppose so*, the polite *I don't suppose you could...*, and *be supposed to*. It notes that *was supposed to* usually means the thing did not happen. It also points out that the **d** of *supposed to* is written but usually not heard.
- ***suggest*** warns against *I suggest you to go*, which is one of the commonest learner errors in English.
- ***study*** explains the difference from *learn*.
- ***suffer*** explains *suffer from* an illness versus *suffer* a loss.
- ***stick*** and ***stretch*** each list the range of meanings that other languages often split among several verbs.

## Pronunciation

All transcriptions were verified. One needs a note. The first check of the British transcription of *strengthen* got only one vote out of three. A re-run got two out of three, which meets the house rule (at least two agreeing votes). The panel is split because *strengthen* is often said with a **k** sound before the **th**. Our transcription leaves the **k** out, and so does the American one, which the open CMU pronouncing dictionary also supports.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewers raised 35 issues, 21 of them blocking. I applied 31 and rejected 4. This was a cleaner batch than the previous cycle.

What I accepted:

- **Absolute claims.** Several of my notes said "always" or "only" where the language allows exceptions:
  - *suffer* is "always" followed by *from*;
  - *suffer from* is only for lasting illnesses;
  - *be supposed to* is the "only" form;
  - *learn* always means you succeeded.
  Each was softened or rewritten.
- ***I don't suppose he'll come* and *I suppose he won't come*.** My usage note treated these as a matter of style. They differ a little in meaning (doubt versus conclusion), and the note now says so.
- **Two unclear definitions for *stretch*.** A job that "stretches" you tests what you can do, and someone who "stretches" a rule uses it more widely than was meant.
- ***Come to a stop*.** This is a phrase with the noun *stop*, not the verb, so I removed it from the collocations.
- **Smaller fixes.** More collocations for the computing sense of *support*, and for *suffer* meaning "become worse" (*your health will suffer*).

What I rejected:

- Two objections to grammar values that are on the house's closed list (*not used in continuous tenses*).
- A claim that phrasal-verb links should end in `-v`. The house uses `-phrv`.
- A flag that an example was redundant.

The vocabulary check then found six definition words outside the defining vocabulary (*slightly*, *straighten*, *property*, *defeat*, *indirect*, *indirectly*). I rewrote those definitions in plainer words. The cross-reference tool added three back-links (*put*, *mix*, *give*), all on the right sense.

Reviewer-b's reply could not be read for two entries. Re-running that reviewer alone fixed both.
