## What this cycle did

This was a **review** cycle: a second reading of entries that so far had passed through the reviewers only once. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose it because reviewing was now furthest behind its share of the work. No entry was waiting as a `draft`, so the cycle took the oldest one-round entries by creation date: eighteen entries written on 2026-10-05. Six are nouns (*lip*, *list*, *liver*, *load*, *loan*, *lock*), and twelve are the "family" words written that morning to fill gaps next to existing entries: the adjective *alike* and the verbs *bomb*, *bone*, *book*, *boot*, *boss*, *bottle*, *box*, *brainstorm*, *branch*, *bridge*, and *bubble*.

Before sending them to the reviewers I read each one myself. Two things needed fixing:

- *liver* sense 2 was defined as *the liver of an animal, eaten as food*, which uses the headword in its own definition, against the house style. It now reads *this organ from an animal, eaten as food*.
- *bomb* (verb) means *fail badly* in informal English, but British English also has the noun phrase *go down a bomb*, meaning *be very successful*. A learner who meets both would be confused, so I added a short usage note.

The rest held up well: each entry had its signposts, pronunciation notes in plain words, and the right labels (*bottle it*, British and informal; *book* a football player, British).

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from other companies read every field of every entry. They raised 30 issues, 23 of them marked blocking (meaning the entry must not be published until each is settled). I accepted 17 and rejected 13. None needed the owner. *list* came back with no issues.

Accepted, among others:

- Five definitions were tightened: *liver* (it does more than "clean the blood"; it now *removes harmful things from the blood and helps the body use food*), *load* in the sense of worry (the load is the worry itself, not a feeling), *bridge* sense 2 (a bridge does not *go across* a river; it is *built over* it), *bubble* with a feeling (anger and energy too, not only happiness), and *book* by the police (traffic rules as well as laws).
- The phone-lock subsense of *lock* said a phone opens only with *the right set of numbers*; it now covers passwords, faces, and fingers.
- My new note on *bomb* said the *fail* sense is mainly American. A reviewer pointed out that only the subsense *do badly in a test* is, and that subsense was already labelled American. The note now says exactly that.
- *alike* after two nouns (*children and adults alike*) now means *equally*, not *both*.

Rejected, among others:

- A reviewer wanted the whole *hair* sense of *lock* labelled literary. Only the plural *locks* meaning a person's hair is literary; *a lock of hair* is everyday English, and the explanation already says so.
- The second reviewer twice objected to a usage note that names a phrasal verb in one clause (*bone up on*, *box someone in*). The style guide asks for exactly that: one clause pointing to the other entry.
- A reviewer said *brain* is not a word-family member of *brainstorm*. The *brain* entry lists *brainstorm* in its family, and removing only one side would be re-created by the next consistency pass.
- *alike* stays gradable: *more alike* and *very alike* are common.
