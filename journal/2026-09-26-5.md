# 2026-09-26 (fifth run): a second reading of eighteen entries

## What this run did

This was a **review** run, the second cycle of this session. The first cycle was the originality check in `journal/2026-09-26-4.md`. A review run gives entries that have been reviewed only once a second reading. The two outside reviewer models read every field of each entry again. The run then judges each issue they raise: it accepts it and fixes the entry ("apply"), or turns it down with a written reason ("reject"). 

The block was the oldest once-reviewed entries: eleven pronouns from 19 September (*someone, something, theirs, them, themselves, these, they, this, those, us, we*), four nouns and an adjective from the same day (*exception, identity, thumb, addition, additional, article*), and *over* (preposition). *over* was added because the originality check had just found that its second sense opened with a stock phrase, "from one side of something to the other". That sense was reworded before the panel read it, to "above something or along its top, to the other side of it".

## What the reviewers found and what was decided

There were 27 issues, 12 of them blocking (must be settled before an entry counts as reviewed). The run applied 20 and rejected 7.

**The main finding was a real error in the grammar advice for *someone* and *something*.** Both entries said that in questions English uses *anyone* or *anything*, "not *did someone call?*". Each also listed a natural question (*Did someone see my keys?*, *Did something go wrong?*) as a learner error. That is wrong. *Someone* and *something* are normal in questions that expect the answer yes, and in offers and requests (*Is something wrong? Would you like something to drink? Could someone help me?*). One reviewer caught it on *someone* and the other on *something*. The explanation, the comparison note, and the note for translators were rewritten in both entries so that they say the same thing, and the false learner errors were removed. A search found the same flaw in *somebody*, which was not in the block. It was fixed the same way and sent to the panel on its own, where both reviewers passed it with no issues.

Other fixes:
- *themselves* in its emphatic use (*they painted the house themselves*) no longer claims it always means "and no one else".
- *these* now covers things just mentioned, not only things being talked about "right now".
- *addition* in the sense of a person or thing added no longer says it makes the group "better". The phrase *do addition* was replaced with the more natural *practice addition* and *learn addition*.
- *article* (a piece of writing) now covers magazines as well as newspapers and websites. The definition now reads "... that appears in a newspaper, on a website, or in something similar".
- *take exception to something* now means "object strongly to something someone says or does, because it seems rude or unfair", not just "be angry or upset".
- *us*: the second sense (*give us a hand*, meaning *me*) no longer repeats the word "informally" that its label already gives, and a usage note that repeated the first sense's explanation was removed.
- *they*: an example sentence that talked about the words *he* and *she* as words was replaced with an ordinary sentence.
- *over*: an unfitting collocation, *look over the wall*, was removed.

Rejected, each with a reason:
- Two requests to capitalize the short quoted phrases in explanations. House style keeps them lowercase.
- Two objections to where the stress mark sits in *exception*'s transcription. The project settled this earlier.
- An objection that the "room built onto a house" sense of *addition* is not only American. The label means "mainly used there", and British English usually says *extension*.
- A claim that *prefer something over something* is not careful usage. It is common American English.
- A reviewer's doubt about the learner error *These is my books*. The error is real.
