## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's job from what is most overdue) chose *build*: new entries from the waiting list of words, the queue. I wrote eleven new entries and all eleven passed the full checking pipeline and are now `reviewed` (published on the site at the next build). Nine came from the queue in its usual order: *grown-up*, *guidance*, *guideline*, *guitar*, *gum*, *haircut*, *hairdresser*, *half hour*, and *hallway*. Two were homographs, that is, separate entries for words with the same spelling but an unrelated meaning and origin. *Count* as a noble rank (*Count Rossi*) had been waiting in the baton file `NEXT.md` as a word to claim by hand; its entry is `count-n-2`, beside the existing *count* that means a number reached by counting. *Gum* turned out to be two words: the sweet you chew (and the sticky substance from trees) and the pink flesh around your teeth. They come from different roots, and the house rule is that unrelated homographs are separate entries, never senses of one entry, so I wrote `gum-n-2` for the second.

The queue row read *grown up* without a hyphen; as `NEXT.md` asked, I wrote the noun as *grown-up*. The adjective row is still waiting in the queue.

**What the reviewers found.** Every entry goes to two automated reviewers from different companies, who give each field a verdict of "ok" or "issue". This time they raised 31 issues. Only 3 were blocking (serious enough to stop an entry from being published until settled), and all three were the same mistake: one example for the tree-substance sense of *gum* said *a thick gum*, treating as countable a sense the entry itself marks uncountable. Both reviewers caught it; I rewrote the example as *Thick gum was coming out of a cut in the tree.*

Of the 31 issues I applied 24 and rejected 7, each with a logged reason. The useful fixes:
- I had used *sweet* to mean a piece of candy, which is the British word; the dictionary's main variety is American, so the *gum* definition now reads *a soft, sweet substance that you chew*.
- *Grown-up* is now defined simply as *an adult*; the fact that children use the word, and adults use it when they talk to children, moved to the explanation field, where the style guide puts limits on who uses a sense.
- *Guidance* now covers official advice from an organization or a government, not only personal help, with a new example.
- The shop sense of *hairdresser* used the headword in its own definition (*a shop where a hairdresser works*); it now reads *a shop where people go to have their hair cut*.
- Small tidying: collocation types corrected on *grown-up* and *half hour*, a repeated fact removed from *half hour*, a misleading note on the link from *count* to *lord* corrected.

The rejections: one reviewer argued that *play guitar*, with no article, makes *guitar* uncountable; it does not, as instruments commonly drop the article after *play*. Both reviewers wanted the note that the shop sense is usually said *the hairdresser's* moved out of the explanation; the style guide says that a limit to one form belongs exactly there. I also kept a learner-error note on *haircut* (*I cut my hair at the hairdresser's* when someone else did it), because it is a real mistake learners make when talking about haircuts.

**Cross-references.** When a new entry names another entry as a synonym, an opposite, or a word to compare, the target entry should point back. The tool could not decide which sense of five target entries should hold the link, so I placed those by hand: *lord*, *adult*, *child*, *rule*, and *glue* now point back to the new entries.

## Cycle 2: originality

**What this cycle did.** Every twentieth run is forced to be an originality check: a test of whether any of our wording looks copied from a published dictionary. The tool picked ten fields at random from across the dictionary (eight definitions, one phrase definition, and one usage note). For each, I ran a web search for the exact wording in quotation marks, and I asked one of the two reviewers, reviewer B, whether each one reads as copied or as original.

**What the searches found.** Nine of the ten searches found no dictionary with our wording; they returned unrelated pages (software help for "start a recording", pages on predators for the big-cat sense of *cat*, self-help pages for *out of your control*). One, the sense of *snow* meaning a fall of snow, is close to how dictionaries put it, but it is short and plain and any dictionary would say it that way; that verdict is "generic overlap", which needs no change.

**The one rewrite.** Reviewer B said the first definition of *twin* (*one of two children born to the same mother at the same time*) was copied, naming three learner's dictionaries. In past checks this reviewer has often quoted our own wording back as if it were the published one, so its "copied" answers do not decide anything by themselves. Its quotation was in fact our own sentence. But a second search, for the headword with the opening words, found a well-known learner's dictionary with *one of two children born at the same time to the same mother*: the same words with two phrases swapped. That is too close, so I rewrote it.

The new definition is *either of two children that a mother gives birth to at one time*. The rewrite went through the full review: both reviewers read the entry three times as I revised it. Along the way they found my first attempt (*born in the same birth*) awkward, which it was, and I replaced it. I also took three smaller fixes: the sense's signpost (the short label that helps a reader pick a sense) changed from *child* to *born together*, since twins can be adults; the usage note no longer says identical twins look "exactly" the same, or that *fraternal twins* is mainly American; and the example *She has a twin brother* was replaced, because *twin* before another noun works like an adjective, and that use belongs in a separate entry. I rejected one point: reviewer B wanted *twin bed* and *twin room* covered here, but those uses belong to the *twin* adjective entry, which is already waiting in the queue. One of the first reviewer's suggestions was to go back to the old, copied wording; I did not.

**Spend.** US$0.08 for the cycle; US$0.38 so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. One lesson, which the previous checks also showed: an exact-phrase search misses a published definition that uses the same words in a different order. The second search, the headword with its opening words, is what caught it. `NEXT.md` already lists both searches.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. I wrote ten new entries, the next ten rows in the queue: *ham*, *hammer*, *handbag*, *handful*, *handwriting*, *happiness*, *harbor*, *hardware*, *hatred*, and *hazard*. All ten went through the full pipeline and are `reviewed`.

Several have more than one meaning, so they needed real work on the senses (the separate meanings in an entry). *Ham* is the meat, and informally also an actor who overacts and a person who uses a radio as a hobby. *Hammer* is the tool, a part inside a piano, and a heavy ball thrown in sport. *Handful* is an amount you can hold in one hand, a small number (*only a handful of people came*), and a child who is hard to control. *Hardware* covers computers, household tools, and an army's weapons. *Hazard* has the golf sense and the phrase *an occupational hazard*. The plain defining vocabulary has no entries for *piano*, *golf*, or *auction*, so definitions avoid those words; *piano* and *golf* appear only in signposts (the short labels for picking a sense) and in examples.

**What the reviewers found.** Across two readings they raised 40 issues. I applied 38 and rejected 2. Only one was blocking: I had put the sport use of *the hammer* in an explanation field, which is meant for words that are hard to define, not for a second meaning. The sport is now its own subsense (a smaller sense under the main one), marked as used only in the singular. The reviewers also found real gaps, which I filled:
- *hammer* now has the part of a gun that makes it fire, and the idiom *go at it hammer and tongs* (labeled British);
- *handwriting* had two senses that were almost the same. The second is now the skill children practice at school, and the entry has the American idiom *the handwriting on the wall*;
- *ham* now has *ham radio*, the usual American collocation (a word that typically goes with the headword), next to the British *radio ham*;
- *hardware* used the British *shop* in an example, and its weapons sense carried a "technical" label that it does not need;
- *hazard* had *Smoking is a serious health hazard*, a health claim of the kind the example rules forbid, and the unnatural *sand hazard*. Both were replaced.

Because *hammer* and *handwriting* changed so much, I sent those two back for a second reading. That reading found the gun definition slightly wrong: the hammer hits a small part at the back of the bullet, not the bullet itself. I fixed it.

I rejected two points. One reviewer wanted the plural *hatreds* removed, but it is a real form (*old hatreds*), and plurals here come from rules, not from hand. The other wanted *hammer and tongs* rewritten with its verb; but the phrase also follows *argue* and *fight*, and the style guide allows a phrase to carry a short explanation of how it is used.

**Cross-references.** I placed three back-links by hand where the tool could not choose the sense: *joy*, *love*, and *risk* now point to *happiness*, *hatred*, and *hazard*.
