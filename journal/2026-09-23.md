# 2026-09-23 — Twelve conjunctions added to the dictionary

## What this run did

This was an unattended scheduled run of the TKG English Learner's Dictionary. A small program, the selector, chose its mode: **build**, writing new entries from the queue of words still to be written. The run was allowed up to twenty new entries and US$1.25 of paid model calls.

There was no leftover work from earlier runs and nothing in the owner's inbox.

The previous run (a maintenance pass) had freed sixteen common conjunctions that were stuck in the queue. This run took twelve of them: **after, although, before, but, if, nor, once, or, since, though, unless, until**. It left out *as, so, than,* and *that*, which have many uses each and need more room. Each entry has between one and four senses (distinct meanings). The three largest (*but*, *if*, *or*) open with a "core idea", one sentence that says what all the senses share.

The entries concentrate on what learners actually get wrong with these words: using **will** after *if*, *when*, *until*, and similar words when talking about the future (*if it will rain*); using *although* and *but* together in one sentence; adding a second *not* after *unless*; using the present tense instead of the present perfect with *since* (*I live here since I was ten*); and word order after *nor* (*nor do I*, not *nor I do*).

## How the entries were checked

Every entry went through the full checking pipeline:

- **Format and word forms**: all twelve passed the validator; conjunctions have no inflected forms.
- **Pronunciation**: all 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by a panel of models and agreed with the Carnegie Mellon pronouncing dictionary, an open data set consulted at run time and not stored.
- **Two outside reviewers**: two models from other companies (called reviewer-a and reviewer-b) read every field of every entry.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 19 issues, 18 of them marked blocking. All 19 came from reviewer-a; reviewer-b found nothing to object to in any of the twelve entries. I read each one, agreed with 18, and rejected 1. The main real problems:

- **before**, second sense (*It was weeks before the house was ready*): I had defined it as "until", which is wrong. The sentence says how much time passes before something happens. Rewritten, and the claim that *until* is a synonym was removed.
- **after** and **before**: the notes on learner mistakes mentioned the **-ing** form (*after finishing*), which belongs to the preposition, not the conjunction. They now say plainly that the **-ing** form is the preposition use.
- **if**, the formal sense in *a good, if rather long, meeting*: my definition made it sound like a guess ("and maybe"). The phrase actually states that the meeting was long. Definition corrected.
- **unless**: my usage note claimed that *unless* is not used for imagined past situations. That is too strong. It was replaced with a contrast grammar books agree on (*I'll be sad if you don't come*, which sounds better than *unless you come*).
- Two weak learner-mistake examples were replaced or dropped. One, on *or*, became a real mistake that some learners make: ending a statement with *or?* as a question tag.

The one rejection: reviewer-a said that *whether*, not *if*, is required when a question includes *or not*. In fact *I don't know if he's coming or not* is ordinary English. Only *whether* can come directly before *or not*, and the entry already says so.

## What did not work

Reviewer-b's first reply on *unless* came back unreadable, so I re-ran that one reviewer. The re-run added a duplicate record of reviewer-a's review to the entry's history and left the empty record in place. I cleaned it by hand (it is tool bookkeeping, not dictionary content). The problem is written up in `wiki/notes/review-panel-rerun-duplicate-records.md` for a later run to fix in the tool.
