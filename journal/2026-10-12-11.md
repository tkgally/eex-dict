# 2026-10-12, run at 08:44 UTC, cycle 3: build, twelve adjectives *perfect* to *popular*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words the dictionary still needs). The scheduler chose it because building had fallen furthest behind its usual share. I took the next twelve band-1 adjectives in queue order. Band 1 is the most common words, which are also the words definitions are written with. The twelve are *perfect*, *permanent*, *personal*, *physical*, *pink*, *plain*, *pleasant*, *pleased*, *polite*, *political*, *poor*, and *popular*. All twelve went through the full pipeline and are now `reviewed`, which means published. The dictionary now has 2,203 entries.

Each entry has every sense a learner needs, with examples, collocations (the words a word usually goes with), and grammar codes. Most also have a learner-error box and a usage note. Some highlights:

- *perfect* has four senses: without faults; exactly right (*the perfect place*); complete, as in *a perfect stranger*; and the grammar term. It warns against *very perfect*.
- *permanent* separates *lasting* from *constant*, a common confusion (*constant noise*, not *permanent noise*). It also carries a translator's note on languages where a similar word means *all the time*.
- *personal* has five senses and a box that separates it from **private**. It warns against *personal department* for **personnel department**, and adds the phrase *nothing personal*.
- *pleased* has a box on *pleased*, *glad*, and *happy*, and the learner errors *pleased of* and *pleasing to meet you*.
- *poor* separates the money sense from the "I feel sorry for them" sense (*the poor dog*, *Poor you!*), which many languages express with a different word.

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel on the first try.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 47 issues, 44 of them blocking. I applied 32 and rejected 15. Most came from reviewer-a. reviewer-b raised eight issues.

Applied:
- *perfect*: the grammar sense said a perfect form shows "a result now". That is true only of the present perfect (*I have eaten*), not the past perfect (*I had eaten*). It now says "complete before a particular time".
- *permanent*: three notes said the word means lasting "for always". It also means lasting a very long time, so the notes now agree with the definition.
- *polite*: I had given *polite with older people* as a learner error. *Polite with* is uncommon but correct, so the error box now shows a real mistake, *polite for us*. *Polite society* and *polite company* (people with good manners) were removed from the sense about manners shown without real feeling, which they do not fit.
- *political*: the sense *done to win power* carried a "disapproving" label and said the reasons were not "good or fair". A political decision is not always a bad one, so the label is gone and the definition is neutral.
- *poor*: the "feel sorry" sense was defined as "used to show that you feel sorry". House style allows "used to" only for function words, so it now reads "unlucky or unhappy, so that you feel sorry for them".
- Smaller fixes: a noun example in *pink* (*a light pink*) was replaced; the *personal computer* compounds were moved to the right collocation type; the core ideas (one-sentence summaries) of *popular* and *perfect* were tightened.

Rejected:
- reviewer-b said four times that the grammar code *comparative with more* is not a valid code. It is in the dictionary's closed list.
- reviewer-a said *plain* meaning "not beautiful" is not a gentler word than *ugly*. It usually is, so the "euphemistic" label stays.
- *I'm very pleasing to meet you* stays as a learner error. Mixing up *-ing* and *-ed* adjectives is one of the commonest learner mistakes.

## Cross-references

When a new entry names an existing one as a synonym or opposite, a script adds the mirror link to the existing entry, but it puts the link on sense 1 when it cannot tell which sense is meant. Three of those mirror links were on the wrong sense, and I moved them by hand. *perfect* now sits on the *total* sense of `complete-adj`. *plain* now sits on the *saying clearly what you mean* sense of `direct-adj` and on the *saying what you really think* sense of `honest-adj`. My first attempt to move them with line-number edits did not work, so I redid them by hand and checked the result. New words linked from these entries were added to the queue, among them the American noun *a physical* (a medical check), *personnel*, and *pleasing*.
