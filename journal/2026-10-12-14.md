# 2026-10-12, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 2: review, second reading of *hotel* to *income*

## What this cycle did

This was a **review** cycle. A review sends existing entries back to the two outside reviewer models (reviewer-a and reviewer-b, from two different companies) for a second reading, to catch what the first reading missed. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job from how long each kind of work has waited) chose it after this run's first cycle, a build. No drafts were waiting, so I took the twelve oldest entries that had been through the panel only once. All of them are nouns written on 4 October: *hotel*, *hour*, *humor*, *husband*, *ice*, *idea*, *illness*, *image*, *impact*, *importance*, *improvement*, and *income*. Following the advice left by the last review cycle, I started the panel in the background so that the ten-minute limit on a single command could not cut it off. It finished in about nine minutes.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 31 issues, 24 of them "blocking" (an entry cannot stay marked as reviewed until each is decided). I applied 18 and rejected 13; none needed escalating to the owner. *hotel* and *improvement* came back with no issues. Every decision is logged, with a short reason, in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

Applied (each fix written in my own words):

- *ice*: sense 2 is the frozen sweet made without milk (*a lemon ice*). Its definition now names sugar as well as fruit juice. It is now labelled American, since British English usually says *water ice* or *sorbet*, and it now has a "compare" link to *ice cream*, which is already in the queue. The note for translators had said learners should never write *an ice* or *ices*. That contradicted sense 2, so it now says the countable use belongs only to sense 2. An example with the awkward plural *lemon ices* became *each had a lemon ice*.
- *illness*: the learner-error box (a note on a mistake learners often make) had marked *He has an illness of the heart* as wrong. It is unusual but grammatical, so the box now shows a real error: *She has been illness for a week*, corrected to *She has been ill for a week*. The note explains that *illness* is a noun and the adjective after *be* is *ill* or *sick*. The note comparing *illness* and *disease* also no longer says a disease usually has a known cause.
- *importance*: sense 1 had been "the quality of mattering a lot, or of having a big effect". Something can matter without having an effect, so it is now "the quality of mattering a lot". Sense 2 is now "the quality of having a high position or a lot of influence".
- *income*: the word family listed *come*, which a learner would not see as related. Both reviewers flagged it, and it was removed. The note on *wage* no longer ties wages to physical work. A second learner error (*a big income*) repeated the usage note, so it was removed.
- *hour*: the example for *after hours* about a bar serving drinks suggested illegal late opening. It now describes an office that is locked after hours.
- *impact*: the note on the verb *impact* now says it is common especially in business and news writing, not only in business English.

Rejected:

- reviewer-a said British English spells the adjective *humourous*. It does not: *humorous* is the spelling everywhere, which is exactly what the usage note says. I rejected this and the related "consistency" complaint.
- reviewer-a said *no idea* and *any idea* show *idea* used as an uncountable noun. *No* and *any* take singular countable nouns (*no car*, *any idea*), so the note is right.
- *make little impact on prices* is standard English, not an error.
- The *husband* pronunciation note ("the **s** sounds like a **z**") follows the house rule for notes written for intermediate learners, which compares letters with familiar sounds instead of using technical symbols.
- *make a good image* is a common learner error, and the note already says that *make an image* suggests drawing a picture.
- reviewer-b wanted the note about the verb *impact* moved out of the noun entry. One sentence pointing to the verb helps a learner who has looked up the noun, so it stays.

No back-links (the mirror cross-references the tools add when a new link is made) were created this cycle.
