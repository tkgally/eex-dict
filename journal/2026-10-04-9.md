Some notes on the entries:

- **ground** is the largest: nine senses (the surface you stand on, soil, an area of land, land used for an activity, *the grounds* of a building, *grounds* meaning reasons, a subject of discussion, the American electrical ground, and coffee grounds) and ten idioms, such as *stand your ground*, *common ground*, *get something off the ground*, and *hit the ground running*. The idiom *break new ground* used to sit in the verb *break*. The house rule files an idiom under its first noun, so it moved to *ground*, as the to-do list asked.
- **god** separates the one God of religions such as Christianity and Islam (capital G, no article) from the gods of many religions and from a person someone admires too much. It has six phrases (*thank God*, *my God*, *God knows*, *for God's sake*, *God willing*, *play God*).
- **goods** is plural only. Its learner errors cover *the goods was* and *a goods*.
- **government** and **governor** cover the British and American differences: singular or plural verb after *government*, and the British school and prison governors.
- **grain**, **grass**, **grammar**, **grave**, and the two grandparent words have learner errors where learners really go wrong: *a rice*, *grammer*, *grand father*, *grave yard*, and *grasses* meaning a lawn.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two outside models review every field of every entry. We call them reviewer-a and reviewer-b, and neither is from Anthropic. Between them they raised 37 issues, 26 of them "blocking" (they must be settled before an entry can be published). I applied 29 and rejected 8, each with a logged reason.

The useful catches:

- The electrical sense of *ground* is the connection to the earth, not a wire.
- *Coffee grounds* covers ground coffee in general, not only what is left after coffee is made.
- The third sense of *government*, *in government*, means holding power. My example *twenty years in government* read as working for the state, so the examples were replaced.
- *Damaged goods* means a person seen as less valuable because of their past. It does not mean their value was actually harmed.
- The second sense of *grass* was defined through the first ("this plant"). It now stands on its own.

Rejections included the literal woodworking phrase *against the grain*, which is not only the idiom; the British *grammar school* note, which is a real trap for translators; and *take a secret to the grave*, which is as common as *to your grave*.

A fourth tool, the pronunciation check, asks three models and an open American pronouncing dictionary. It confirmed every transcription except the British forms of *grandfather* and *grandmother*. There only one of three models agreed with the drafted form; most likely the others gave the common form without the *d*. Both forms are in normal use. I kept the drafted form as the main one, added the *d*-less form as a British variant, flagged both entries, and added a line to the curator file (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`, the list of questions for the owner).

One linking problem: *earth* already lists *ground* as a synonym, so a "compare" link from *ground*'s electrical sense back to *earth* would have made the pair two link types at once. That sense now names the British word *earth* in its explanation instead.
