# 2026-10-03 (00:43 UTC run, cycle 1): second review of twelve verbs, *look* to *move*

## What this run did

This was the first cycle of the run that started at 00:43 UTC. There was no stranded work: no open pull requests and no leftover branches. The mode selector (the script that chooses each cycle's kind of work) picked **review**. A review is a second reading of entries that have already passed one round of the two outside reviewer models. I add missing senses, idioms, learner errors, and notes by hand, and then the entries go through the reviewers again.

There were no unreviewed drafts, so the rule was to take the oldest entries that had been read only once. These were twelve verbs drafted on 2026-09-26: *look, lose, love, manage, marry, mean, measure, meet, melt, mention, mix,* and *move*. Several of them were thin. *Measure*, *melt*, and *mix* had no learner errors, and most had no usage note.

Before the reviewers saw them, I added by hand:

- **Ten new senses**: *look at* meaning to examine something (*can you look at my bike?*); *lose* meaning to confuse a listener (*you've lost me*, informal); *manage* meaning to be able to do, carry, or eat something (*can you manage that box?*); *marry* meaning to join two things successfully (formal); *be meant to* under *mean*, meaning to be expected to (*you're not meant to park here*); *meet* meaning to get a reaction such as opposition; *melt* meaning to disappear (*melted into the crowd*); *mix* meaning to make a drink or a material; and two for *move*: to change the time of an event, and to suggest something formally at a meeting.
- **Seven idioms** (fixed phrases), each placed under its keyword by the house rule (the first noun in the phrase, or the first verb when it has no noun): *look who's talking*, *never look back*, *not look yourself*, *lose it*, *lose yourself in something*, *nice to meet you*, and *mix and match*.
- **Eight usage notes** (a short paragraph on a point of use), such as *look* followed by an adjective, not an adverb; *lose* against *miss*, and *lose* against *loose*; *managed to* rather than *could* for one success in the past; *melt* against *dissolve*; and *mention something to someone*.
- **Eleven learner errors** (common mistakes, shown with a correction), such as *I looked TV* (use *watch*), *I very love this city*, *Nice to meet you again* (use *see*), *The sugar melted in my tea* (use *dissolved*), and *She mentioned me that...* (use *mentioned to me*).

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 57 issues. Thirty-two were *blocking*, which means I had to decide each one before the entries could stay marked as reviewed. I applied 29 decisions and rejected 26. None needed to go to the owner. Every decision is logged with its reason in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The most useful findings:

- My new learner error for *measure* (*I measured my weight*) was too strict. *Measure* is normal for weight in science and medicine. I took the error out and softened the usage note: *weigh* is the usual everyday verb.
- *Melt*: I had written a core idea (the one-sentence summary of what the senses share) saying that melting happens "slowly". That is not always true, so it was rewritten. The feelings sense and the new "disappear" sense were also made more exact.
- *Meet* sense 6 (*meet a need*) spoke of "reaching a level", which did not fit *meet a deadline*. It now names a time limit.
- The music sense of *mix* now says that sounds are combined and balanced, not only put together.
- Two cross-references (links between entries) were wrong: *get* as the opposite of *lose*, and *transport* as a synonym of *move*. Both were removed from both entries, so that the next lint pass does not re-create them.
- A note in *move* said *moved me to cry* "is not used". It is only rare, so the note now names the usual phrases instead.

Most rejections were points the house rules already settle. Examples are the American transcriptions without the length mark, and the quoted headword at the start of adaptation notes (notes for translators). I also kept *affect* as a synonym of *move* in its feelings sense (*deeply affected by her speech*), and *She moved to another department* as an example of moving to work in a different place.

The tools added two back-links (a link the tools add so that both entries point to each other). Both were in the right place: *look* now appears under *examine* and *consider*.
