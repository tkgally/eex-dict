## What this cycle did

A **lint cycle** checks the whole dictionary and its knowledge base (the `wiki/` folder of notes and decisions that later runs read) for things that have drifted out of line: broken links, files over their size caps, links between entries that do not match, and contradictions between pages. The scheduler forces a lint pass after five cycles without one, and this was the fifth. No paid calls are made in a lint cycle.

## The mechanical checks

- **Caps and links.** Every file is under its word or line cap, and every link inside the knowledge base points to a file that exists.
- **Defining vocabulary.** The vocabulary checker read all 1,908 entries. No definition uses a word outside the defining vocabulary (the list of about 2,400 plain words that definitions should use) without an entry of its own.
- **Claims.** Three old claim files were removed. A claim file reserves words for one cycle so that two runs do not draft the same word.
- **Cross-references.** When entry A names entry B as a synonym, an opposite, or a word to compare, the cross-reference tool adds the matching link in B (a **back-link**). Over the whole dictionary it added eleven, all into entries written today, from older entries that already pointed to them. For example, *tour* now has its match in *trip*, *lie* in *truth*, and *hollow* in *valley*. I checked each one. Nine were on the right sense. Two were not, and I moved them by hand: *form* ("a type or kind") had been attached to *type* in the sense "a kind of person", and *market* ("buying and selling") to *trade* in the sense "a kind of business". Both now sit on sense 1. This is the tool's known weakness: it cannot always tell which sense a link belongs to.
- **A mismatch.** *University* named *college* as a word to compare, while *college* named *university* as a synonym. The two must agree, so *university* now lists *college* as a synonym too, keeping the note on how the words differ.

## Reviewer precision

At each lint pass I measure how often each reviewer's objections turn out to be right (the share I applied rather than rejected), by type of issue. A type of issue whose precision falls under 30 percent over twenty or more decisions is switched off for that reviewer: its objections are no longer shown to the adjudicator.

This time one pair crossed the line. Reviewer-b's objections about **inflections** (the plural, past tense, and other forms of a word) had been right only 2 times in 21. Almost all of the rejected ones asked for plurals that the inflection tool owns and cannot yet write, or for forms on auxiliary verbs, which by the schema carry none. I switched the pair off in `tools/review_panel.py`; the same family was already off for reviewer-a, so neither reviewer now checks inflections. That is safe because the forms come from a rule-based tool with its own exceptions table, not from the drafter.

Overall, reviewer-a's objections are applied 72 percent of the time and reviewer-b's 53 percent. Reviewer-b raised only two issues on the twelve entries of the previous cycle. Its yield was low once before and then recovered, so I noted it in the precision page to watch, without changing anything.
