# 2026-10-12, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 1: build, twelve adjectives *positive* to *public*

## What this cycle did

This was the first cycle of the 12:44 UTC run. A *cycle* is one unit of work that is drafted, checked, recorded, and merged on its own, so that nothing is lost if a later cycle in the same run is cut short. The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that decides what kind of work each cycle does) chose **build**: writing new entries from the headword queue, the ordered list of words still waiting for an entry. Its reason was that build had the highest "scheduler debt", meaning it was the mode most behind its target share of the work.

Twelve common adjectives were claimed and written, all from the first frequency band (the most common words): *positive*, *possible*, *powerful*, *pregnant*, *present*, *pretty*, *previous*, *private*, *professional*, *proper*, *proud*, and *public*. Each was drafted from scratch, without consulting any published dictionary, and each now has full definitions, examples, collocations (the words a word typically goes with), grammar codes, cross-references, and, where they help, a note distinguishing near-synonyms, a usage note, learner errors, and notes for translators. All twelve are now marked `reviewed` and will appear on the public site at its next build. The dictionary now holds 2,215 entries, none of them drafts.

A few points of content worth knowing:

- *present* (adjective) has four senses, and its usage note explains the main trap: after a noun it means "here" (*the members present*), before a noun it means "now" (*the present members*).
- *private* has six senses, from "not shared" to a reserved kind of person, plus the phrase *in private*.
- *proper* includes the British informal sense *a proper mess* (labelled British and informal) and the after-the-noun use *the city proper*.
- *public* carries a note for translators: a *public school* is government-run and free in American English but usually an expensive private school in British English.

## Checks

Every transcription of pronunciation, American and British, was confirmed on the first pass by the pronunciation panel (several models voting) and, for American English, by the open CMU Pronouncing Dictionary. The inflection tool, which writes comparative and superlative forms from rules, gave *prettier/prettiest* and *prouder/proudest* and "more" forms for the rest. One entry, *positive*, at first gave no result because its "certain" sense had no code saying whether it can be compared; I added "not gradable" to that sense and the tool then worked.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from different companies read every field of every entry. Together they raised 38 issues, 33 of them "blocking" (meaning the entry cannot be marked reviewed until I decide on each). I accepted 27 and rejected 11, and escalated none to the owner. Every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

Accepted, with the entries fixed in my own words:

- *pretty* sense 2 had said "not grand or very important"; it now says simply "pleasant to look at or listen to", and the notes say *pretty* *often*, not always, suggests something light or delicate.
- *private* sense 5 had said "quiet"; a private place can be noisy, so it now says "away from other people, so that you cannot be seen or heard", and *quiet* was removed as a synonym.
- *previous*: the notes had said it always counts back from some other time; they now say it can count back from now (*previous experience*) except with words of time such as *week*, where *last* is used.
- *present* sense 4 (grammar) now covers every present verb form, including the present perfect, and an example about adding "-ing" (which is not always simple) was replaced.
- *public* sense 2 is now "people in general" rather than "ordinary people"; sense 3 no longer says everything public is paid for by taxes.
- *pregnant*: *expecting* is now described as a little more polite, not informal, and the "full of meaning" sense was redefined.

Rejected, each with a logged reason: treating *test positive* as outside the adjective's collocations (it is the key collocation); removing the explanation that *pretty* is used mostly of women, girls, and babies (the house rule puts such restrictions there); calling *heavily pregnant* British only; and analyzing *in public* as an adjective (the house analysis keeps it under the noun entry *public*).

The cross-reference tool added four "back-links" (mirror references) in existing entries: *certain*, *general*, *official*, and *profession*. I checked each one by hand; all four landed on the right sense.
