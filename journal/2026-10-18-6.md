## What the entries contain

The small cardinal numbers have one sense (*the number 6, one more than five*), four examples, typical phrases (*six o'clock*, *the six of us*), a usage note on using the number alone for a time or an age, and a pronunciation note for learners. Several carry an idiom: *on all fours*, *at sixes and sevens* (labeled British and informal), *dressed to the nines*, and *ten to one* (meaning "very likely"). The learner errors are ones learners really make: *fourty* for *forty*, *fiveteen* for *fifteen*, *the nineth* for *the ninth*, *I have ten years* for *I'm ten years old*.

*Hundred*, *thousand*, and *million* each have a second sense for the plural used for a large number that is not exact (*hundreds of people*, *thousands of times*, *millions of people*), and learner errors such as *two hundreds people* and *a million of people*. *Million* also has the idiom *one in a million*. *Thousand* has a culture note for translators: English writes *10,000* with a comma and *2.5* with a period, and many countries do the opposite.

*Second* has four senses: order (*the second floor*), dates (*the second of August*), next after the best (*second prize*, *the second largest city*), and another one (*a second chance*, *a second language*), plus the idiom *second to none*. *Third* has three: order, place in a competition, and dates. Both point the fraction and time uses to their own noun entries.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewers raised 26 issues (22 marked blocking). I applied 14 and rejected 12. No question needed to go to the owner.

Applied, the main points:
- I had written that *hundred*, *thousand*, and *million* are "used after **a**, **one**, or another number, never alone". The reviewer was right that *several hundred*, *a few thousand*, and *the hundred people selected* are normal. The explanations and the translator notes now say "after **a**, **the**, a number, or a word such as **several** or **a few**".
- The note on *second* called the time unit (*wait a second*) "a different word". It has the same origin, so it is now "a noun with its own entry", and the translator note says the two senses "are translated with different words in many languages".
- *Dressed to the nines* no longer says "most expensive clothes", only "your best, most formal clothes".
- British English "always" puts **and** in *a hundred and twenty*; now "usually". The comma rule for large numbers is now "usually".

Rejected, the main points:
- reviewer-b said the plural *millions* is a noun and belongs in a separate entry. The three large numbers all keep the plural as a second sense, which is how learners meet them; splitting only *million* would make the three entries disagree.
- reviewer-a said the plurals can be exact (*a population of millions*). That is still an inexact number, so the rule stands.
- Dates (*the second of August*) and the cardinal–ordinal family link (*second* to *two*) follow the pattern already accepted for *first*.
- *At sixes and sevens* keeps its British label: our region labels mean "mainly used there".
