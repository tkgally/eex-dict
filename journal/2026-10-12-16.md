# 2026-10-12, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 4: build, twelve adjectives *pure* to *regular*

## What this cycle did

This was the fourth and last cycle of the 12:44 UTC run. A run is capped at four cycles, because entries written late in long runs used to come out thinner. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job from how long each kind of work has waited) chose **build**: new entries from the headword queue, the ordered list of words still waiting for an entry.

Twelve band-1 adjectives were drafted from scratch and taken through the full pipeline. Band 1 holds the most common words. The twelve are *pure*, *purple*, *quick*, *quiet*, *rare*, *raw*, *ready*, *real*, *reasonable*, *recent*, *red*, and *regular*. All twelve are now `reviewed`, and the dictionary holds 2,227 entries, none of them drafts. As in the first cycle, I wrote each entry at full depth: every sense with its own examples, collocations, and grammar codes, plus notes comparing near-synonyms (*quick*/*fast*/*rapid*; *real*/*genuine*/*true*/*actual*), learner errors, and notes for translators where something could be a trap. Examples of those traps: in some languages a word like *real* also means "royal", and English *regular* does not mean "so-so".

Before review, the vocabulary check found four words in definitions that are outside the defining vocabulary (the roughly 2,400 plain words definitions must be written in): *fancy*, *fuss*, *grapes*, *sore*. I rewrote those four definitions first.

## Pronunciation

The pronunciation panel (three models voting, plus the open CMU Pronouncing Dictionary for American English) verified every transcription except one. American *real* was written as one syllable (`ril`), which is what the CMU dictionary gives. Three of the four votes preferred two syllables. The project's rule is to settle such a split by the CMU vote, so I kept `ril` and the entry carries the `pronunciation-disputed` flag. Earlier entries such as *detail* were handled the same way. The pronunciation note already tells learners that some speakers say *REE-ul*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 54 issues, 50 of them "blocking" (each must be decided before an entry can be marked reviewed). I applied 40 and rejected 14; nothing needed escalating to the owner. *quick* and *recent* came back clean.

Applied, with the fixes written in my own words:

- *quiet* sense 4 had put *a quiet word* (private) and *a quiet wedding* (small) under one definition. It is now two senses: "kept private, so that other people do not hear, know, or notice" and "with only a few people, and without making it a big event".
- *real*: sense 2 is now "being what it seems to be, not a copy or something made up", which covers both *real leather* and *your real name*. *false* was dropped as its antonym. The economics sense now says prices can change in either direction. *realize* was removed from the word family. The usage note now calls *really* the standard choice rather than calling *real good* wrong.
- *purple*: the writing sense (*purple prose*) is now "trying too hard to sound beautiful, with too many unusual words". The face sense now includes difficulty breathing.
- *pure*: the "total" sense (*pure luck*), the moral sense (now not labelled literary), and the sound-or-color subsense were all redefined. A claim that the "total" sense is never compared was removed.
- *rare* steak is now "red or pink" inside. The note comparing *rare* and *scarce* now covers events as well as things.
- *raw*: reviewer-b pointed out that *rawer* and *rawest* are normal, so the codes that blocked them were removed and the inflection tool now gives those forms.
- *regular*: the claim that British English does not say *a regular guy* was removed. The "size" subsense (*a regular coffee*) now means the usual or standard size rather than the middle one.
- Smaller fixes: *red* eyes are no longer said to be painful, *red* wine and *in the red* were redefined, and *ready* and *reasonable* were made more precise.

Rejected, each with a logged reason:

- reviewer-b asked for *purpler* and *readier*. The forms with *more* are more common, so the house codes stay.
- reviewer-b wanted *in the red* moved to a noun entry. The house rule keeps an idiom under its keyword entry whatever its part of speech, and there is no noun entry for *red*.
- Removing the explanation that *red* (of hair) is used of hair and fur. The house rule puts that kind of restriction in the explanation.
- reviewer-a said *ready money* contradicts the note that senses 1 to 4 come after a verb. *ready money* belongs to sense 5, which is the one used before a noun.
- *frequent* as a synonym of *regular* in *a regular customer*: *a frequent customer* is common.
