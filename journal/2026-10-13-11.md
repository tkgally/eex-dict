# 2026-10-13, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 3: twelve adjectives, *used* to *wet*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries written from the queue of words the dictionary still needs. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose it because building was furthest behind its share of the work. The queue continues through the most common adjectives (band 1, the words every learner meets first), and this cycle took the next twelve: *used*, *useful*, *useless*, *usual*, *violent*, *visible*, *warm*, *weak*, *wealthy*, *weekly*, *well*, and *wet*.

Some points worth knowing:

- *used* has two senses: *not new* (*a used car*) and *used to* (*I'm used to getting up early*). The entry warns against confusing *be used to doing* with *used to do*, which has its own entry.
- *weak* has eight senses, mirroring the senses of *strong*, from the body to tea and coffee, arguments, and the economy.
- *well* as an adjective means *healthy* (*I'm not feeling well*). Its comparative is *better*, taken from the project's table of irregular forms.
- Before drafting, I listed every existing entry that already pointed at one of the twelve words, and put the return links on the right senses myself. For example, *cold* sense 4 (not friendly) lists *warm* as its opposite, so *warm* sense 3 (friendly) lists *cold*.

## Pronunciation and word forms

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation check, which compares several models with an open pronouncing dictionary. The comparative forms (*warmer*, *better*, and so on) came from the project's rules.

One thing did not fit: *used* in *used to* is said with an *s* sound (*YOOST*), not a *z* sound. The style guide says a sense with its own sound gets its own transcription, but the checking tool only checks the main transcription, so an unchecked one would block the entry. I put the sound in the plain-English pronunciation note instead (*YOOST tuh*).

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from other companies read every field of every entry. They raised 24 issues, 17 of them marked blocking (meaning the entry must not be published until each is settled). I accepted 16 and rejected 8. None needed the owner. One reviewer's reply on *weak* was cut off, so I ran it again; the second reply was complete.

Accepted, among others:

- *used* sense 1 said *so not new or not clean*. A used car need not be dirty; it now says *already used, or owned by someone else before, so not new*.
- *violent* sense 3 (feelings) said *especially anger, that are hard to control*, which did not fit *a violent dislike*. It now reads *having or showing extremely strong feelings, such as anger or hate*. *A violent argument* was moved out, because it usually means people were hitting each other.
- *weak*: the character sense now covers *a weak moment* (*not having the strength of mind to make firm decisions or to say no to people*); the taste sense now fits weak coffee; the money sense no longer says *losing value*.
- Four near-synonym notes were corrected: *helpful* is used for advice as well as people; *hot* can be pleasant (*a hot shower*); *wealthy* does not suggest old family money; *moist* is not always pleasant.
- One reviewer said *the usual* (*I'll have the usual*) is missing. It is a noun use, so I queued *usual* (noun) and pointed to it in the usage note.

Rejected: four objections to the code *comparative with more*, which is how the project marks adjectives that use *more*; a claim that *thin* is not a synonym of *weak* for excuses (our own *thin* entry has exactly that sense); a claim that *close to guessing the right answer* cannot define *warm* in a guessing game (*You're getting close to guessing the right answer* works); an objection to the *informal* label on *make yourself useful*; and a note that the empty verb-pattern field on *used to* contradicts the usage note (that field is for verbs only).
