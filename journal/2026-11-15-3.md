Twelve new nouns are now published as `reviewed`: *quiet*, *quiz*, *quote*, *rainbow*, *rat*, *reaction*, *real estate*, *reality*, *reception*, *recognition*, *recommendation*, and *rectangle*. All are in band 2, the second of the dictionary's five frequency bands.

Before drafting, I ran the safety checks. No pull request from an earlier run was left open. Some details worth knowing:

- *reaction* has five senses: a response (*my first reaction was to laugh*), an effect on the body (*an allergic reaction*), quick reactions when driving or playing, the chemistry sense, and a turn against something (*a reaction against modern design*). Its one learner error is *a reaction on the news*, which should be *to the news*.
- *reception* has five senses: the desk in a hotel or office, a formal party (*a wedding reception*), the way people greet something new (*a warm reception*), phone or radio signal quality, and, added after review, the British first year of school (*she is in reception*).
- *real estate* is labeled American. Its note says that *real* here does not mean "true", a trap for learners who know the adjective.
- *recognition* covers knowing someone again (including *face recognition* by a phone), accepting that something is true or legal, and public praise. It has two phrases: *in recognition of something* and *beyond recognition*.

**Pronunciation.** Each entry has an American and a British transcription. A panel of three other models votes on each, and for American English a public pronouncing dictionary votes too. Twenty-three of the twenty-four were confirmed. The American form of *real estate* was disputed: the panel prefers *real* in two parts (*REE-ul*), while I wrote one part, as the existing *real* entry does and as the pronouncing dictionary has it. I kept the one-part form, added a note naming the two-part form, flagged the entry, and left a line for the curator (the owner's list of questions to settle in person).

**What the reviewers found.** Two reviewer models read every field. They raised 61 issues, 7 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before the entry can be published). All seven were right and were fixed:

- The *quiet* note said *a quiet* is wrong, but the entry itself used *a sudden quiet*. The note now says *a* is used only with an adjective before it.
- The *rainbow* culture note named English and used an abbreviation. It now says only that the number of colors people name differs between cultures, and spells out *gay, lesbian, bisexual, and transgender*.
- A *reaction* example stated a general science fact (*heat speeds up the reaction*), which house rules forbid; it now describes one occasion.
- *receive a reception* was listed as a collocation (a typical word combination); it now reads *get a warm reception*.
- The phrase *beyond recognition* was defined with *recognized*, the headword's own verb, which is circular. It now says *so much that people can no longer tell who or what someone or something is*.

Of the 54 minor issues, 50 were applied and 4 rejected. Both reviewers wanted *chain reaction* added to *reaction*. I rejected it because the house rule files a phrase under its first noun, which is *chain*; it is noted in the queue for *chain*. I also kept *quiet*'s second sense (a calm time, as in *peace and quiet*) and its uncountable code, with reasons logged. Applied catches include fun online quizzes for *quiz* and titles for the punctuation sense of *quote*.

**Links.** Eleven entries that the new words point to (for example *silence*, *property*, *praise*, *credit*) had no single matching sense for the return link, so I placed those back-links by hand.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector chose **review**: a second reading of entries that are already published. No entries were waiting as drafts, so the cycle continued the **coverage pass**. This is a reading of the most important band-1 words (band 1 is the most frequent of the five bands), most senses first. It asks whether each entry is missing a common meaning or idiom, and whether its examples fit. This cycle read four words with four senses each: *always*, *answer*, *apply*, and *arm*. The pass has now read 345 entries.

**What I changed before the reviewers saw the entries.**

- *answer* gained a fifth sense, a local version of something better known (*this café is our town's answer to a fancy restaurant*). It lost a usage note and a grammar note that only repeated its learner error (*an answer of this question* should be *to*). A repeated *in answer to* was cut from sense 1; it stays as a phrase.
- *apply* gained *apply online* and *apply the brakes* as collocations (typical word combinations).
- *arm* gained three idioms: *the long arm of the law*, *within arm's reach*, and *give your right arm for something*.
- *always* was read and left unchanged at this stage.

**What the reviewers found.** Two reviewer models read every field. They raised 38 issues, 6 of them blocking (issues that must be settled before publication). All six were right:

- Both reviewers said *ever* is not a synonym of *always*. Today *ever* means "at any time", mostly in questions and negatives. The link was removed, together with its mirror link in the *ever* entry, as the house rule requires.
- *answer*'s test sense was defined as "the correct result", but the entry also lists *the wrong answer*. The definition now covers any answer written for a test question.
- *apply*'s second sense was defined as "be about" a person or situation. A rule that applies to everyone affects everyone; it is not "about" them. It now reads *have an effect on, or be right for*. My new example *Click here to apply the changes* (software) did not fit the method sense, so it was removed.
- *arm*'s chair sense used the word *arm* in its own definition. It now says *the part at each side of a chair or a sofa that you can rest your elbows on*.

Of the 32 minor issues, 27 were applied and 5 rejected. Applied: *apply*'s method sense (*apply what you learned*) is more common than the surface sense (*apply the cream*), so it now comes first. *arm*'s machine part and arm of the sea are now separate senses. *answer* gained the idiom *not take no for an answer*. Several core ideas (the one-sentence summary that opens an entry) were rewritten to cover every sense. Rejected, with reasons logged: a sleeve sense for *arm* (*sleeve* is the usual word); a claim that *apply the brakes* does not fit the pressing sense (the definition covers force); and two requests to merge senses whose collocations and translations differ.

**Spend.** US$0.19 for this cycle; US$2.47 of the US$15 daily budget so far today.

**What did not work.** One new idiom definition (*arm in arm*) used *hooked*, a word outside the defining vocabulary (the plain words definitions must use). It was reworded.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *arrive*, *attention*, *bank*, and *bath*. A lint pass (the periodic whole-dictionary check) is now due.

## Cycle 3: lint

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector forced a **lint** pass, because ten cycles had run since the last one. A lint pass is the periodic whole-dictionary check. It runs the mechanical checks over every entry and then reads the project's own records for drift. This was the hundred-and-thirteenth pass.

**Mechanical checks.** The word caps on the project's control files and all internal links were clean. The cross-reference tool keeps links symmetric: if *rainbow* lists *rain* as a related word, *rain* lists *rainbow* back. It added such mirror links in seven entries, for example *puppy* now compares *dog*, and *rainbow* now names *rain* in its word family. In two cases the tool could not tell which sense of the target should carry the return link, so I placed them by hand: *puzzle*, sense 2 (something hard to understand), now compares *problem*, and *purchase*, sense 1 (buying), now has *sale* as its opposite. Six used-up claim files (records of which run is writing which word) were removed.

**Band vote.** Forty-five words waiting in the queue had no frequency band yet. A panel of three small models voted on them, at a cost of under one cent. Seven words had votes more than one band apart: *heap* (verb), *preferably*, *preferential*, *pup*, *queue* (verb), *quiz* (verb), and *shove* (noun). I settled each at the middle vote, which in every case was also the most reasonable reading. All forty-five words are now ready to be built in their turn.

**Reports.** Three standing reports came out much as they did yesterday. There are 1,034 pairs where one entry links to another but the target entry may lack the matching sense, up from 1,023 as the dictionary grows. 315 definitions use their own headword, nearly all of them harmlessly (*a path where people walk* inside *walk*); the review cycles judge each one as they reach it. The same six "promises" remain: notes that point to an entry that does not exist yet, such as *storey* and *used to*.

**Reviewer precision.** Precision is the share of a reviewer's issues that I apply rather than reject. It stands at 0.85 for reviewer A and 0.83 for reviewer B. Under the house rule, a type of issue is switched off for a reviewer when its precision falls below 30 percent over twenty or more decisions. The types below that line are already switched off, so there were no new switch-offs. Reviewer B's spelling-and-format checks remain on by the owner's ruling, at 0.38.

**Records.** The wiki index matches the pages, the curator file has no duplicate lines, the open-questions page has nothing stale, and the log has no gaps.

**Spend.** Under one cent for this cycle; US$2.48 of the US$15 daily budget so far today.
