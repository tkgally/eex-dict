# 2026-10-12, run at 04:43 UTC, cycle 2: build, twelve adjectives *necessary* to *online*

## What this cycle did

This was a second **build** cycle in the same run: after cycle 1 merged, the scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job from how long each kind of work has waited) again chose new entries, because building still had the largest backlog.

I took the next twelve band-1 words from the queue (band 1 is the most common vocabulary), all adjectives: *necessary*, *negative*, *nervous*, *new*, *next*, *nice*, *noisy*, *normal*, *obvious*, *odd*, *official*, *online*. Each was written from scratch from the style guide and put through the full pipeline: schema validation, the inflection script, the pronunciation panel, the two-reviewer panel, my reading of every issue, the defining-vocabulary check, and the cross-reference check. All twelve are now `reviewed`. The dictionary has 2,180 entries.

Some notes on the content:

- *negative* has six senses: harmful, not hopeful, saying no, a medical test result, grammar, and numbers below zero. A translator's note on the test sense warns that a *negative* result is usually good news.
- *new* has five senses (just made, replacing, not known before, recently arrived, just starting) and the phrase *what's new?*.
- *odd* has five senses (strange, numbers, not a pair, now and then, and *forty-odd* meaning a little more than forty) and the phrase *the odd one out*.
- *nervous* carries a box separating *nervous*, *worried*, and *anxious*, and a translator's note that in some languages the look-alike word means *irritated*, not *worried*.
- *necessary* carries two common learner errors (*I am necessary to finish*, and mixing *for you to* with a *that* clause) and a usage note on its spelling.
- *normal* warns that *It was normal* is not used in English to mean *so-so*.

## Inflections and pronunciation

The inflection script gave *newer*, *nicer*, *noisier*, and *odder*; the other words compare with *more* or not at all, set by grammar codes. All 24 transcriptions (American and British) were verified on the first pass by the pronunciation panel and the open CMU pronouncing data.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewers (models from two other labs) raised 38 issues, 29 of them marked blocking. I applied 30 and rejected 8; none needed escalating.

Applied, among others:
- *negative*: one of my grammar examples said that every negative sentence needs the helping verb *do*, which is false (*She isn't here*); it was replaced. Both reviewers said *The temperature was negative* is not natural English (people say *below zero*); it was replaced with an arithmetic example.
- *nervous*: both reviewers pointed out that *nervous energy* is about the feeling, not the body's nerves; it moved to the first sense. The box no longer claims *anxious* is always stronger than *worried*.
- *next*: my translator's note called *in the next week* wrong; it is fine English, meaning some time in the coming seven days, and the note now says so. *Who's next?* was replaced, because there *next* stands alone and is not the adjective.
- *odd*: a grammar code for the *forty-odd* use was wrong and was removed; the *odd jobs* subsense was redefined.
- *online*: the learner error was about the adverb (*bought it online*); it now shows the adjective written as two words.

Rejected, among others: moving the phrase *next to nothing* out of `next-adj` (the house rule keeps an idiom under its keyword whatever its part of speech); removing the British label on *odd socks* (a region label means *mainly used there*); a claim that *comparative with more* is not a valid grammar code (it is in the closed list).

The cross-reference script added mirror links on six older entries; I checked each and moved two to the right sense (`bad-adj` to "unpleasant or harmful", `fine-adj` to "small detail"). It also found that `following-adj` called *next* a synonym while `next-adj` called *following* a "compare" word; both now say synonym.
