# 2026-10-16, cycle 3 of the 12:44 UTC run: twelve adverbs, *nowhere* to *over*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries drafted from the queue (the project's list of words still to write), in order of how basic they are. Twelve adverbs were drafted, all from band 1, the most common words: *nowhere, obviously, off, often, okay, on, once, only, otherwise, out, outside,* and *over*. Each went through the full pipeline: the format check, the automatic forms (none, since these adverbs have no *-er* or *-est*), the pronunciation check, both outside reviewers (the review panel), my decision on every issue they raised, and the final checks. All twelve are now published as `reviewed`.

Four of these, *off, on, out,* and *over*, are the particles of hundreds of phrasal verbs, and they were the hardest. Each entry covers only the adverb meanings a learner can see in many verbs (*over* meaning *falling*: *fall over, knock over*; meaning *across*: *come over*), and points to the preposition entry for uses before a noun (*over the bridge*), to the adjective for uses after *be* (*The game is over*), and to separate phrasal-verb entries for combinations whose meaning cannot be guessed (*get over, take over*).

## Pronunciation

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel. For *outside*, the open pronouncing dictionary (CMU) put the main stress on the first part, while three of the four panel models put it on the second; the entry keeps the second, which is the usual stress when the word is said alone. *Often* records the common form without a **t** sound and the form with a **t** as a variant.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 76 issues; reviewer-b raised 4. I applied 60 and rejected 20. No question needed the owner. reviewer-b's first answer on *only* came back empty (a broken reply); I ran that reviewer again on that one entry, and its second answer was complete with no issues.

Applied, among others:

- ***once***: the first definition said *one time, and no more*, but the examples included *once a week*. It now says *a single time, or one time in each day, week, or other period*.
- ***often***: a learner error (*I go often to the park*) is awkward but not wrong. It is now *I eat often rice*, which is a real and common error.
- ***out***: the meaning *not at home* no longer says *often to do something enjoyable*, and *find out* and *be out* were taken out of a collocation list where they did not fit.
- ***over***: several definitions were tightened (*from standing to lying* did not fit a lamp; *completely covered* did not fit *paint over*), and an adaptation note that said *most languages* now says *many*, as the style guide requires.
- ***on***: the opening summary no longer says *contact with a surface*, which belongs to the preposition.
- ***off***, ***otherwise***, ***outside***: reviewer-b caught three places where an entry described a use belonging to another part of speech (the preposition in *fall off of a bike*, an adjective use of *otherwise*, and the stress of *outside* before a noun). All three were removed.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- reviewer-a objected that pairs such as *fall over* and *turn over* are phrasal verbs and should not be listed as collocations. The dictionary's other adverb entries (*down, away, back*) list such pairs this way, so the lists stay.
- *Be on* meaning *working* and *be over* meaning *finished* stay adjective uses, as the preposition entries already say.
- *Start over* (again) and *It's cold out* keep their *American* labels: a region label means *mainly used there*.
- *I'm off to the beach* keeps *off* meaning *starting a trip*.

## Links between entries
