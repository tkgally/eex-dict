## Cycle 1: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's kind of work, `tools/next_mode.py`) chose *review*: there were no unfinished drafts, and reviewing had the largest backlog. The work was the next step of the *coverage pass*, which you asked for in your rulings of 2026-10-24 (ruling B1). That pass rereads the most important everyday words, those in frequency band 1, and checks that each entry covers every common present-day use, function use, and idiom of the word. This cycle took the next four words in its order, each with four senses: *in* (adverb), *island* (noun), *issue* (noun), and *join* (verb). Pre-flight found no open pull requests left over from earlier runs, no new entry reports from readers, and nothing in the inbox. The four leftover branches from earlier runs had already been merged, and each is already on the curator list to be deleted.

**What changed in the entries.** I read each entry myself before sending it to the reviewers:

- *in* (adverb) gains a subsense for the tide (*The tide is in*), a new informal sense, *taking part* (*If you're going to the beach, I'm in*), and the phrase *in and out* (*in and out of the hospital all year*). The use meaning "fashionable" (*Bright colors are in this year*) is an adjective, so I did not put it in the adverb entry. The entry now points to an adjective entry for *in*, which I added to the queue of words to write.
- *island* was mostly complete. It loses two repeats: a usage-note sentence that said the same thing as its learner error ("on an island", not "in"), and a translator note that said the same thing as the pronunciation note (the silent *s*). It gains *an island nation* and *island life*.
- *issue* gains a subsense for *the issue* meaning the main point (*Who broke it is not the issue*), a subsense for a set of coins or shares given out at one time, and the idiom *cloud the issue* (also *confuse the issue*). The definition of sense 4 had begun with *the act of*, which the style guide does not allow, so I rewrote it.
- *join* gains a subsense for taking part in an activity: *join the search*, *join a protest*, *join the meeting from your phone*, and the formal *Please join me in thanking ...*. Its usage note and a translator note both repeated the learner errors, so I removed them and kept one copy of each fact.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Two reviewer models read every field of each entry. They raised 31 issues, none of them blocking. I applied 17 and rejected 14, and each decision is logged with a reason. The applied fixes included:

- a broader core idea for *in* and for *join* (the one-sentence summary of what an entry's senses share);
- a clearer definition for the newspaper sense of *issue*;
- an informal label and a "usually plural" grammar code on *trust issues*, in the proper fields instead of in prose;
- a new example for *an island of calm*, because the old one described an object, not a place;
- a looser definition for the "connect" sense of *join*, so that *a bridge joins the island to the coast* fits it.

The rejections were mostly the house rule on where idioms go: an idiom is filed under its first noun, or else its first verb. So *join forces* stays under *force*, where it already is, and the reviewer's suggestions *be in for something* (*you're in for a surprise*) and *be in on something* belong under *be*. *Be* has neither idiom yet, so I added both to the to-do list for its next review in `NEXT.md`. I also rejected a request to mark a word by hand in an example (the website does that itself) and two requests to remove synonyms that the linked entries list in return.

One fix came from the vocabulary check. The definitions must use the defining vocabulary, or words that already have entries. *Stamps* has no entry, so it came out of two *issue* definitions. It stays in an example sentence, where that rule does not apply.

## Cycle 2: lint

**What the cycle did.** The selector forced a *lint* pass because ten cycles had run since the last one. A lint pass is a periodic check of the whole dictionary and its knowledge base for things that drift out of line. It spends almost nothing: it runs checking tools and makes small fixes by hand.

**Mechanical checks.** The word caps on the project's own files and the internal links in the wiki were all within bounds. The cross-reference tool keeps synonym, antonym, and "compare" links two-way: if *trend* lists *tendency* as a synonym, *tendency* should list *trend* back. It added four missing return links on its own (for example, *tourist* now names *local* as its opposite). In seven more cases it could not tell which sense of the target word should carry the return link, so I placed them by hand. *Vacation* now names *break* as a synonym, *wardrobe* compares with *cupboard*, and *trash* compares with *litter*; the others are *understanding*/*insight*, *version*/*model*, *workshop*/*shop*, and *trend*/*tendency*. In every case the right sense was the first one. Eleven entries changed in all, only in their cross-references. Six used-up claim files were removed. A claim file records which words a cycle has taken, so that two runs do not write the same word.

**Band vote.** Words that tools add to the queue wait without a frequency band until three word-list models vote on one. The bands run from 1, the most common, to 5. Forty-eight such words were voted on, at a cost of under one cent. The models disagreed by more than one band on two of them, and I settled both at band 3: *in* as an adjective meaning "fashionable" (queued in cycle 1) and *vacation* as a verb. All 48 are now ready to be built.

**Reports.** These are lists for later review cycles, not fixes. They are about the same size as at the last lint:

- 1,071 cases where a word names another as a synonym or antonym but no sense of the other word matches;
- 321 definitions that use their own headword, most of them harmless (*a wing of a chicken* inside *wing*);
- the same seven usage notes that point to a separate entry that does not exist yet. These are all words with two unrelated meanings, such as *corn* on the foot and *story* meaning a floor of a building, and they are already on `NEXT.md`'s list to write.

**Reviewer accuracy.** For each reviewer, I measure how often its issues are accepted, overall and by kind of issue. Under the standing rule, any kind accepted less than 30 percent of the time over twenty or more decisions is switched off for that reviewer. Reviewer A's issues are accepted 85 percent of the time and reviewer B's 84 percent, with no new kind under the line. Reviewer B's formatting issues are accepted only 41 percent of the time, but they stay on by your earlier ruling.

**Knowledge-base checks.** Every wiki page is listed in the index. The curator file has no duplicate lines. There are no open questions for you, and the log has no gaps: no runs took place on 24 and 25 November.

## Cycle 3: originality

**What the cycle did.** Every twentieth run includes an *originality check*. Its job is to make sure our definitions are our own wording, not copied from a published dictionary. This run's number came up, so the selector forced the check. The tool picked ten written fields at random from the 4,741 entries:

- definitions of *attach*, *this* (as in *this book*), *confident* ("believing strongly that something is true"), the wood of the *ash* tree, and *process* in its computer sense;
- the definitions of four idioms: *in season*, *coin it in*, *on thin ice*, and *the big picture*;
- one usage note, on *stand* versus *stand up*.

**How it was checked.** For each field I ran a web search for the exact wording in quotation marks and looked only at whether a published dictionary or word list used the same words. Nothing was copied from the results; only the verdict was kept. None of the ten searches found a dictionary with matching wording. The closest was a city government question-and-answer page whose textbook-style wording for computer processing ("a series of operations on the information") is near ours for *process*. It is not a dictionary, and the phrase is standard technical language.

I also asked one reviewer model, reviewer B, the standard question: does each definition read as copied, or as original? It may answer "copied" only if it can quote the published wording. It called nine of the ten original. It answered "cannot tell" for *attach* ("fix or join one thing to another") because the definition is so short and plain that many dictionaries would say something similar.

**Verdicts.** Six fields are *original*. Four are *generic overlap*, the label for a short, plain definition that any dictionary would write in much the same way, which the style guide says is not copying: *attach*, *confident*, *process*, and *the big picture* ("the general situation, not the small details"). None needed rewriting. The completed checklist is in `reviews/originality/2026-11-27.md`.

**Spend.** Less than half a cent, for the one question to reviewer B. The day's total is US$0.22.
