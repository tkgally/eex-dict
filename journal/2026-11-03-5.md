# Journal, 2026-11-03 (run 20261103T184509Z-q07nf2)

## Cycle 1: lint

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose a lint pass because ten runs had passed since the last one. A lint pass is the dictionary's housekeeping: it checks that every rule the tools can test still holds, and then reads the knowledge base (the `wiki/` pages that hold the project's rules and notes) for contradictions and gaps. This was the hundredth such pass. No entries were drafted; it was almost entirely unpaid work.

**Mechanical checks.** The word caps on the project's own files, the links between wiki pages, the entry validator, the unit tests, and the vocabulary and cross-reference gates all passed. The wiki stands at 29,959 of its 30,000-word cap, so every addition needs a matching trim; this pass trimmed one note to make room for its new finding.

**Cross-references.** When entry A names entry B (as a synonym, a word to compare, or a family member), B should usually name A back; these are "mirror" links, and a script adds them. It added four this time, in *backpack*, *barrier*, *beef*, and *belongings*, all entries built earlier today. Seven links could not be placed by the script because it could not tell which sense of the target they belonged to. Two were new, and I placed them by hand: *nerve* (the worry sense, as in *a case of nerves*) is now a synonym on the first sense of *anxiety*, and *way* (as in *in some ways*) is now a synonym on the first sense of *aspect*. The other five are the long-standing cases that name no real sense, waiting for the cross-reference audit the owner asked for on 2026-10-24.

**Band vote.** Every word waiting in the queue (the list of headwords still to be written) carries a band from 1 (most important for learners) to 5. Words that tools add to the queue arrive "unbanded" until three word-list models vote. Twenty-six such rows were voted on for half a US cent. One vote spread more than one band: *real* as an adverb (*real good*), voted 1, 2, and 3; I settled it at 2, since it is very common in informal American speech. Reading the newly banded rows found two faults: *sunny* had been queued as a noun (a wrong part-of-speech guess from the tool that scans definitions; *sunny* the adjective already has a row), so that row was marked a duplicate; and *award-winning* had lost its hyphen, which I restored by hand.

**Promised entries.** A report lists usage notes that promise "its own entry" for a word that has neither an entry nor a queue row. Seven were found. *Left-wing* and *right-wing* (promised by *wing*) were added to the queue and voted into band 3. Three are unrelated words spelled the same as an existing entry (homographs), which the queue cannot hold: *corn* (the painful spot on the foot), *story* (a floor of a building), and *file* (to smooth with a tool). They are listed in `NEXT.md` for a later build to claim by hand. The other two are false alarms: the promised phrasal verbs exist under slightly different names.

**Reviewer precision.** Each issue a reviewer raises is applied or rejected, and the share applied is its precision. A kind of issue under 30 percent over twenty or more decisions is switched off for that reviewer. No new switch-off was due. Reviewer A (Claude) stands at 0.83 overall; reviewer B (Gemini) at 0.81, with its pronunciation flags at 0.38 the closest live family to the line.

**What did not work.** Running the band vote a second time in the same session (for the two *-wing* rows) crashed, because the tool reused its cached replies from the first vote, which it files by batch number rather than by content. Moving the cache aside fixed it. This is written up in the band-vote note for a later fix, together with two smaller faults seen again: the vote tool stamps every settled row with the date 2026-10-24, and the queueing tools still sometimes drop hyphens.

**Reports for later reviews.** 328 definitions use their own headword (most are harmless, such as *a university as an organization*); 930 cross-reference pairs may point at the wrong sense. Both lists feed later review cycles.

**Spend.** US$0.007 this cycle; US$3.12 of the day's US$15 so far.

**What is next.** The selector will choose again; the queue in `NEXT.md` is unchanged at the top: the cross-reference audit, band-2 nouns from the build queue, and the coverage reading of six-sense entries.

**For the owner.** Nothing new. The open items are unchanged: names used as family names after a title, continent names in definitions, *the former* and *the following*, and the pronunciation disputes in `reviews/needs_curator.txt`.

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose a build: new entries from the queue, at most twelve per cycle. Two verbs came first because this morning's band vote had just placed them high: *approach* (band 1) and *assign* (band 2). The other ten are band-2 nouns, next in the queue's alphabetical order: *bond*, *bonus*, *booking*, *bookshop*, *bookstore*, *boost*, *bother*, *boundary*, *boxing*, and *bra*. Each was written from scratch to the style guide, then sent through the full pipeline: the validator, the inflection generator, the pronunciation check, both reviewers, my adjudication, the vocabulary lint, and the cross-reference tool.

**Some content choices.** *Approach* has four senses under a core idea (come nearer; deal with a problem; contact someone to ask or offer; come close to an amount), with "come nearer in time" as a subsense, and a learner error for *approached to the house*. *Bond* covers the feeling between people, the money paper, the glue join, and the American court sense (money paid so that an accused person can stay out of prison). *Booking* is labeled British for its reservation sense, with *reservation* as the synonym, and it also has the performer, sport, and American police senses. *Bookshop* and *bookstore* share a definition and differ only in their region labels, British and American. *Bother* has three senses: the effort something takes (*it's no bother*), a person or thing that is a nuisance, and the British informal sense of fighting (*a bit of bother*). *Boundary* has a pronunciation note saying it is often said in two syllables.

**Pronunciation.** All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each entry) were verified by the pronunciation panel, and the American ones also agreed with the open pronouncing dictionary the checker uses.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 24 issues. I applied 22 and rejected 2. Three issues were blocking, and I applied all three. In *assign*, the fourth sense was defined as "decide that something has a particular value, cause, or importance", but its examples were about giving a score or a letter. It now reads "give something a particular value, number, or name, or say who or what is responsible for something", and a weak example (*the tests assign a score*) became *the judges assign each dancer a score*. In *bond*, reviewer A pointed out that the very common chemistry use was missing, so a subsense now covers it. A *booking* example had a tense error (*is getting more bookings since*), now *has been getting*. Among the minor fixes, both reviewers called *bond*'s note on how its senses translate generic, so it was removed. The two rejections were both from reviewer B. One said *usually passive* is a grammar code, but the project lists it as a verb pattern. The other said an example fitted another sense, but that sense had already been redefined to cover it.

**Vocabulary and cross-references.** The vocabulary lint found two definition words that are outside the defining vocabulary and have no entry yet: *referee* (in *booking*) and *breasts* (in *bra*). Both were reworded. The cross-reference tool could not decide which sense of six existing entries should point back to the new ones, so I placed those links by hand: *tie* and *link* now point to *bond*, *trouble* and *limit* to *bother* and *boundary*, and *tackle* to *approach*. *Effort* was dropped as a synonym of *bother*, because no sense of *effort* matches it. New queue rows include *allocate*, *bail*, *bond* (verb), *boost* (verb), *booster*, and *bothersome*. They wait unbanded until the next band vote.

## Cycle 3: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose a review cycle. No entries were waiting as drafts and no reader had sent an entry report, so the cycle continued the coverage pass the owner asked for on 2026-10-24. That pass re-reads the most important (band-1) entries, those with the most senses first, and asks whether anything a learner needs is missing. The next four entries in that order were *note* (noun), *lose*, *live*, and *lead* (verbs), each with six senses. With these, 177 entries have now had this reading.

**What I added.** *Note* gained a subsense for a small taste or smell in food or drink (*this coffee has notes of chocolate*). *Lose* gained a subsense for causing someone to lose something (*that mistake lost us the game*) and two new senses: a clock or watch that runs slow (*my watch loses five minutes a day*), and getting away from someone who is following you (*we lost them in the crowd*). *Live* gained the sense of having something as the most important thing in your life (*she lives for her children*), plus collocations such as *live in fear*. *Lead* gained a subsense for influencing someone, especially toward bad behavior (*he's easily led*), and the phrase *lead by example*. Its senses were also reordered so that the common senses come before the sense about roads and paths: causing something (*stress can lead to illness*) and living a kind of life (*lead a quiet life*).

**Cleaning the notes.** The house rule since 2026-10-24 is that each fact lives in one field. All four entries broke it. In *lose*, the usage note repeated three other fields, so it was removed. One fact had been stated four times: English uses *miss*, not *lose*, for a bus or a chance. It now lives only in a learner error. In *lead*, the usage note repeated the past form *led* and the *lead to* + *-ing* rule, both already in learner errors, so it was removed too. In *note*, the warning that *note* does not mean a school grade sat in both a learner error and a false-friend note; it now stays only in the false-friend note.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 51 issues. I applied 43 and rejected 8. All five blocking issues were applied. Reviewer A objected to an example I had just added, *I made a mental note*, because a mental note is not written down, and *note*'s sense 2 is defined as words you write. It was removed. Reviewer B caught a real contradiction in *lose*. One sense listed *lose a chance*, while a learner error told readers to say *miss a chance*. That sense is now about time only (*there's no time to lose*). Other applied fixes: three examples that stated checkable facts about the world were replaced (*fish can't live out of water*, *some birds live on insects*, *the company leads the world in solar panels*); *half-time* became the American *halftime*; and a learner error built on a regularized form (*leaded*) was replaced with a real spelling mistake (*lead* written for *led*).

The rejections, with reasons. *Lose track of* and *lose touch with* are idioms that belong under their nouns (*track*, *touch*) by the keyword rule. *Live through* and *live on* (*his music lives on*) are phrasal verbs, which get their own entries, so I added both to the queue. Reviewer B three times asked for bold marks on *lived*, *living*, and *led* in examples, but the site marks every listed inflection itself. One grammar objection was wrong: an intransitive use needs no pattern.
