# 2026-09-26 (twenty-sixth run): eight verbs, *repeat* to *rest*

## What this run did

This was the seventh cycle of this session, and a **build** run. A build run drafts new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to be written. The previous build merged as pull request #94. The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, which picks each run's kind of work) then chose build again. The run continued with band 1, the most basic words, after *repair*.

The eight verbs were:

- **repeat**: saying something again, doing something again, passing on something you were told, and *repeat after me*. A learner error covers *Can you repeat again?*
- **reply**: answering, and doing something in answer (*He replied to the rude comment with a smile*). A note explains why *reply* needs *to* and *answer* does not.
- **represent**: acting for someone (a lawyer, a school team), being a sign of something (*blue lines represent rivers*), being something (formal: *This represents excellent value*), and showing something in a picture or a film.
- **require**: needing something (formal), and making something necessary because of a rule.
- **rescue**: saving someone from danger, and saving something that is in trouble.
- **research**: studying a subject to find new facts, and finding out information before you decide.
- **respect**: admiring someone, not harming something, and accepting a decision or a wish.
- **rest**: relaxing, leaning on something, and depending on something (*All our hopes rest on him*), with the phrase *rest assured*.

## Checks and reviews

Every entry went through the full pipeline, the fixed sequence of checks each entry must pass:

- **Format and inflections.** All eight passed.
- **Pronunciation.** All sixteen transcriptions were confirmed by the panel of pronunciation models. Two are worth noting:
  - The American *require* is written with two syllables at the end, as the house already does for *fire* and *hire*. Two panel votes and the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary agreed.
  - *Research* has two common American stress patterns. The entry gives *ri-SURCH* as the main form and *REE-surch* as a variant. The panel voted for the main form three to one.
- **Reviewers.** The two reviewer models raised 17 issues, the fewest this session. Each has a logged decision: 14 applied and 3 rejected.

The main fixes:

- *Represent* in the sense of pictures and stories now covers describing someone in a particular way. Its example *the city is represented as a dangerous place* had not fit the old definition.
- *Require* in the sense of rules is now "make it necessary for someone to do something, especially because of a rule or a law". A requirement can also come from a person or a situation. The grammar no longer says the sense is "usually passive", because the active *The law requires drivers to wear seat belts* is just as normal.
- *Research*: the usage note had talked only about the noun *research*, which breaks the rule that each entry covers one part of speech. It is now one clause pointing to the noun's own entry. A translator's note no longer calls *research into* British only.
- *Rescue*: *The crew were rescued* became *The crew was rescued*, the usual American agreement. An odd example (*A late goal rescued the game for them*) was replaced.
- *Rest*: the core idea, the one-sentence summary at the top of the entry, now also covers the sense "depend on".

Rejected: a request to stop writing the core idea as a sentence (the style guide asks for one), an objection to the quoted headword at the start of a translator's note, and a claim that "not used in continuous tenses" is not a grammar pattern. It is in the dictionary's closed list of patterns.

The cross-reference tool added five back-links, which are links pointing back from an existing entry to a new one. They are on *need*, *admire*, *relax*, *lean*, and *depend*, and all were on the right sense. It also reported a mismatch: *answer* lists *reply* as a synonym, but *reply* listed *answer* only as a word to compare. *Reply* now lists *answer* as a synonym, with a note on the difference.
