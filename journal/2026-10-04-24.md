## What this cycle did

The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose **review**: a second reading of entries that have been through the outside reviewers only once. About 530 entries are in that state. The rule is to take the oldest first. Those were nine nouns created on 30 September at 13:00 UTC (*audience, aunt, author, authority, autumn, baby, back, bacteria, bag*) and three verbs from the next batch (*alphabetize, amaze, anger*).

I read each entry in full before sending it to the reviewers, looking for missing common senses, idioms, learner errors, and usage notes. These twelve were already full: *back* had five senses and twelve idioms, *bag* six senses and four idioms, *baby* six senses. I made two additions by hand:

- *Back* gained two idioms: *see the back of someone* (be happy that someone has gone, British and informal) and *the back of beyond* (a place far from any town).
- *Alphabetize* gained the British spelling *alphabetise* and a usage note saying that people often say *put something in alphabetical order* instead.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 24 points, 12 marked blocking. I accepted 8 and rejected 16; every reason is in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

Accepted:

- *Author*: the second sense said "the person who writes or thinks of" a report or a plan. A plan's author may not have written it personally, so it now reads "the person who creates or is responsible for" it. The note for translators now agrees with this.
- *Authority*: the example *The local authority is responsible for the schools* uses a chiefly British term in an entry written in American English. It became *The transit authority runs the city's buses*, and the collocation list changed to match.
- *Back*: the idiom *have someone's back* (*I've got your back*) was labelled American. It is now common in British English too, so the label is gone.
- *Bag*: the travel sense was awkwardly worded. It now reads "the cases and other containers that you take your clothes and other things in when you travel".
- *Audience* lists *house* as a synonym. That is true only in a theater (*a full house*), and the link now says so.

Rejected, with reasons:

- Six of the rejections were the same objection from reviewer-b. It asks for the headword at the start of a note for translators to be in bold rather than in quotation marks, but the quotation marks are house style (a standing "fence" in `NEXT.md`). This objection is why that reviewer's spelling-and-format precision stays under the 30-percent line. That line is the point where a kind of objection should be switched off, a question still waiting for the owner.
- Reviewer-a said *Who is the author of this painting?* is not really an error. I kept it: English speakers say *the artist* or *who painted it*, and *author* for a painting is a typical false friend from languages where the similar word covers any creator.
- Reviewer-a objected to the note on *baby* that *be born* is "always in the passive". The form is always passive (*was born*, never *borned*), so the note stands.
- Reviewer-a asked for a "formal" label on the verb *anger*. The usage note already says it is "a little formal and common in news reports". A full formal label would overstate that.
