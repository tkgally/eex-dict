## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 46 issues, 38 of them "blocking" (an entry cannot be marked as reviewed until each one has a decision). I applied 34 and rejected 12. None needed to go to the owner.

Half of the issues were about *sharp*, and reviewer-a was right about most of them. I reorganized the entry:

- Sense 2 had joined two ideas, a sudden large change (*a sharp rise in prices*) and a sudden change of direction (*a sharp bend*). The bend is now its own subsense.
- Sense 5 (a clear picture) had been defined with the word *clear* and also covered *a sharp difference*. It now reads "having edges and small details that are easy to see", and *a sharp contrast* and *a sharp difference* have their own subsense ("very easy to notice").
- Sense 6 now says "strong, angry, or unkind", so it covers *sharp criticism*.
- The music sense had mixed two uses with different grammar: the name of a note (*F sharp*) and singing or playing a little too high (*The violin sounds a little sharp*). They are now senses 8 and 9, and *flat* is listed as the opposite of sense 9 only.
- An example about *a sharp businessman*, which can suggest dishonesty, was replaced.

Other changes I applied:

- *sensible*: the label "approving" was on the whole entry, but *sensible shoes* is neutral. It now applies to sense 1 only.
- *separate*: the adverbs *apart* and *together* were removed as synonym and opposite. Both reviewers flagged them.
- *serious*: the informal sense (*serious money*) now means "large in amount or degree", not "very good".
- *short*: *small* and *rude* were removed as synonyms, *short and sweet* was redefined as "brief and pleasant, and not longer than it needs to be", and a doubtful learner error was removed.
- *sick*: two mainly British collocations were removed, and the note comparing *sick* and *ill* now says that **ill** is also used before a noun (*seriously ill patients*).

Rejected:

- reviewer-b again said that *comparative with more* is not a valid grammar code. It is in the closed list.
- reviewer-b said that *secret* in *keep this secret* is a noun. It describes *this*, so it is an adjective.
- *a secure password* is a common collocation.
- *just shy of* (a little less than) stays labelled American, because a region label here means "mainly used there".
- Short pointers to the suffix *-shy* (*camera-shy*) and to *the sick* (sick people as a group) stay in the usage notes.

The tools added back-links (the mirror cross-references that keep links two-way) in eight entries. Three were attached to the wrong sense, and I moved them by hand: *secret* on *open* (now under "for anyone"), *sharp* on *quick* (now under "clever"), and *sick* on *cool* (now under "good, fashionable").
