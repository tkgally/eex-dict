## Cycle 1: review

**Why a review.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's job by weighing how long each kind of work has waited) chose a review. There were no draft entries and no open reader reports ("Entry report" issues on GitHub), and no stranded pull requests or leftover branches from earlier runs. So the work was the next step of the coverage pass the owner asked for in October (ruling B1): reading the most important (band 1) entries again to add whatever a complete learner's dictionary would have but ours lacks. The pass is working through entries with six senses. I took four: *sure* (adjective), *support* (noun), *subject* (noun), and *study* (noun). That brings the pass to 155 entries, with 52 six-sense content-word entries still to read.

**What I added.**

- **support**: a subsense for a person who helps you through a hard time (*My sister was a real support to me*), and a new British sense for the singer or band that plays before the main act at a concert, pointing to the American word *opening act*.
- **subject**: a subsense for the subject line of an email, which learners meet every day and the entry did not have.
- **study**: a subsense for careful study of a plan or a map before deciding (*The plan needs more study*), and the American informal phrase *a quick study* (a person who learns new things fast).
- **sure**: the "reliable" sense now covers things that can be trusted to work (*a sure bet*), not only signs and ways.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (one Claude model, one Google model, each reading every field) raised 44 issues, 5 of them blocking (must be fixed or answered before the entry counts as reviewed). I applied all 5:

- *sure*'s comparison box said that only **sure** is used in *make sure* and *be sure to*. Both reviewers caught that this is false: *make certain* is normal English. It now says the *sure* forms are far more common.
- *sure*'s second sense said "the speaker, not the subject, is the person without doubt", which is true of *sure to win* but wrong for *sure of a seat*, where the subject is the one who will get the seat. The sentence is gone; the speaker/subject contrast stays only in the translators' note, where it is stated correctly.
- *study*'s subject sense (*media studies*) was defined with the verb *study* itself, a circular definition. Both reviewers flagged it; it now says "taught at a college or university".

Of the minor issues I applied 35 more. The main ones: weak synonyms removed (*clear* and *satisfied* for *sure*, *favor* for *support*, *field* for a school subject), each with its mirror link in the other entry, and *approval* and *backing* given to *support* instead; I placed the *approval* back-link by hand because the tool could not pick the sense. The grammar sense of *subject* now says it is a word or phrase in a sentence, not the person or thing itself. An example in *sure* that stated a medical fact (*there's no sure cure for a cold*) was replaced, as the house rules forbid checkable facts in examples. A *support* example that really showed the compound *support act* was replaced. Three generic translators' notes and one doubled pronunciation fact were removed under the notes rules, and British *holidays* in a *subject* example became *vacations*.

I rejected 4: two requests to remove the explanation on *studies* in subject names (a restriction of use is exactly what the explanation field is for), a request to put the research sense of *study* before the student sense (keeping the learning senses together reads better), and a request to add the idiom *to be sure* to *sure*. Under the house keyword rule an idiom with no noun goes under its first verb, so *to be sure* belongs in the entry for *be*; I added it to the list of idioms waiting for that entry's next review.

## Cycle 2: build

**Why a build.** After the first cycle merged, the selector chose a build: new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to write). The queue is on band-2 verbs (the second most important band) in alphabetical order. I took six, under the cap of twelve the selector allowed, so each could be full: *waste*, *welcome*, *wind*, *withdraw*, *witness*, and *wreck*.

**What I built.**

- **waste**: four senses: using something badly (*waste time looking for my keys*), not using a chance or a talent, *be wasted on someone* (*Good coffee is wasted on me*), and the American slang sense *kill*. It also has the saying *waste not, want not*, and a learner error: *waste time doing*, not *waste time to do*.
- **welcome**: greeting a guest, being pleased about a decision or a chance, and a place or group being happy to let people in (*The hotel welcomes dogs*), with a learner error (*welcomed us*, not *welcomed to us*).
- **wind**: the verb that rhymes with *find*, with the past form *wound*: a road or river with many bends, wrapping string or a bandage, winding a clock, and turning a handle. Its pronunciation note warns that the noun *wind* (moving air) sounds different. The translators' note warns that the past *wound* sounds unlike *wound* meaning an injury.
- **withdraw**: taking money out of a bank, taking back an offer or a product, soldiers moving back, stopping taking part in a race or a course, pulling away from people, and moving a hand or an object back, with a formal subsense for leaving a room (*the guests withdrew to the garden*).
- **witness**: seeing a crime or an accident (with a subsense for living through a big change), a time or a place as the subject (*the last ten years have witnessed great changes*), and witnessing a signature.
- **wreck**: damaging something beyond repair, with a subsense for ships, and spoiling a plan, a chance, or a marriage.

All twelve transcriptions (American and British) agreed with the pronunciation panel and the open pronouncing data. All inflections came from the rules or the exceptions table (*wound*, *withdrew*, *withdrawn*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 64 issues, 8 of them blocking, and I applied all 8. Most of them came from one mistake of mine. Where a sense had a single passive example, I had listed *usually passive* as a pattern for the whole sense (*waste a chance*, *wind a clock*, *withdraw troops*). Those verbs are mostly active, so the label was wrong. The passive examples now just carry the note "passive". *Wind your way* was coded intransitive even though *your way* is its object, and the *be wasted on* sense now has an active definition and an active example to match.

Of the rest, I applied 45. These included clearer definitions (a misplaced *such as a car window*, and *many times* in the wrapping sense when the example wound a rope twice), a new subsense that reviewer B found missing (*withdraw* meaning to leave a room), British *tap*, *wool*, and *wind the window down* replaced, three notes that repeated other fields removed, and *smash* dropped as a synonym of *wreck*.

I rejected 11. Five asked me to hand-mark *wound* in examples, but the site already marks every form listed in the inflections table. Three asked for idioms that the keyword rule puts elsewhere: *waste your breath* is already under *breath*, and *waste no time* belongs under *time*, where I listed it for that entry's next review. Two repeated reviewer B's claim that *usually passive* is not a pattern (it is in the closed list). One asked for a *nautical* label, which the closed set of subject labels does not have.

I also placed seven cross-links by hand where the tool could not pick the sense in the other entry (*reject*, *wrap*, *advance*, *quit*, *observe*, *destroy*, *spoil*). *Greet* and *welcome* are now marked "compare" on both sides instead of being called synonyms on one side only.

## Cycle 3: closure

**Why closure.** The selector chose closure: building entries for words the dictionary already uses but has no entry for. This cycle they came from word families (a word listed in another entry's family box, such as *banker* in *bank*). The "closure" source proper, words used inside our definitions, had no rows left in bands 1 and 2. So the tool moved on to the family source, as the build order says. I took five nouns in queue order: *ban*, *bang*, *bank holiday*, *banker*, and *banking*.

**What I built.**

- **ban**: an official rule against something (*a ban on fireworks*, *lift the ban*), and an order that keeps a person out of a sport or a place (*a three-game ban*, *a lifetime ban from the club*). It has a learner error: a ban is **on** something, not **for** it.
- **bang**: a sudden loud noise, a hard hit (*a nasty bang on the head*), and the American *bangs*, hair cut straight across above the eyes, with the British word *fringe* given as its synonym. It has two phrases: *with a bang* (in an exciting way) and the American informal *more bang for your buck*.
- **bank holiday**: the British public holiday. A note for translators says it is a day off for almost everyone, not only for bank staff, and gives the usual American words.
- **banker**: a person with an important job in a bank, and the player who looks after the money in some games.
- **banking**: the business of banks (*a career in banking*), and using a bank's services (*I do all my banking on my phone*).

All ten transcriptions agreed with the pronunciation panel and the open data, and all plurals came from the rules (*banking* has none).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 11 issues, none blocking, and I applied all 11. The most useful: my definition of a ban from a sport said "for a period of time", but my own example was *a lifetime ban from the club*, which has no end and is about a place, not a sport. The definition now covers both. The pronunciation of *bank holiday* now marks a secondary stress on *bank*, matching how the dictionary writes other two-word nouns such as *ice cream*; the checker verified the new form again. Three collocation lists were tidied (*a rich banker* is a free combination, not a fixed pairing), and the American equivalent of a bank holiday was corrected to *holiday* or *federal holiday*. One of my own definitions used *forehead*, a word outside the defining vocabulary; I reworded it before the check gate.

Reviewer B raised nothing on this batch. That is not a fault: its reply was complete. It has happened before on short, simple entries (`wiki/notes/reviewer-b-low-yield.md`).
