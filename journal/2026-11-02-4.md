## Cycle 1: review

**Why a review.** The selector (the tool that picks each cycle's job by weighing how long each kind of work has waited) chose a review. There were no draft entries and no open reader reports ("Entry report" issues on GitHub), and no stranded pull requests or leftover branches from earlier runs. So the work was the next step of the coverage pass the owner asked for in October (ruling B1): reading the most important (band 1) entries again to add whatever a complete learner's dictionary would have but ours lacks. The pass is working through entries with six senses. I took four: *sure* (adjective), *support* (noun), *subject* (noun), and *study* (noun). That brings the pass to 155 entries, with 52 six-sense content-word entries still to read.

**What I added.**

- **support**: a subsense for a person who helps you through a hard time (*My sister was a real support to me*), and a new British sense for the singer or band that plays before the main act at a concert, pointing to the American word *opening act*.
- **subject**: a subsense for the subject line of an email, which learners meet every day and the entry did not have.
- **study**: a subsense for careful study of a plan or a map before deciding (*The plan needs more study*), and the American informal phrase *a quick study* (a person who learns new things fast).
- **sure**: the "reliable" sense now covers things that can be trusted to work (*a sure bet*), not only signs and ways.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (one Claude model, one Google model, each reading every field) raised 44 issues, 5 of them blocking (must be fixed or answered before the entry counts as reviewed). I applied all 5:

- *sure*'s comparison box said that only **sure** is used in *make sure* and *be sure to*. Both reviewers caught that this is false: *make certain* is normal English. It now says the *sure* forms are far more common.
- *sure*'s second sense said "the speaker, not the subject, is the person without doubt", which is true of *sure to win* but wrong for *sure of a seat*, where the subject is the one who will get the seat. The sentence is gone; the speaker/subject contrast stays only in the translators' note, where it is stated correctly.
- *study*'s subject sense (*media studies*) was defined with the verb *study* itself, a circular definition. Both reviewers flagged it; it now says "taught at a college or university".

Of the minor issues I applied 35 more. The main ones: weak synonyms removed (*clear* and *satisfied* for *sure*, *favor* for *support*, *field* for a school subject), each with its mirror link in the other entry, and *approval* and *backing* given to *support* instead; I placed the *approval* back-link by hand because the tool could not pick the sense. The grammar sense of *subject* now says it is a word or phrase in a sentence, not the person or thing itself. An example in *sure* that stated a medical fact (*there's no sure cure for a cold*) was replaced, as the house rules forbid checkable facts in examples. A *support* example that really showed the compound *support act* was replaced. Three generic translators' notes and one doubled pronunciation fact were removed under the notes rules, and British *holidays* in a *subject* example became *vacations*.

I rejected 4: two requests to remove the explanation on *studies* in subject names (a restriction of use is exactly what the explanation field is for), a request to put the research sense of *study* before the student sense (keeping the learning senses together reads better), and a request to add the idiom *to be sure* to *sure*. Under the house keyword rule an idiom with no noun goes under its first verb, so *to be sure* belongs in the entry for *be*; I added it to the list of idioms waiting for that entry's next review.
