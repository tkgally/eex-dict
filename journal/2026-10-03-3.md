# 2026-10-03 (00:43 UTC run, cycle 3): twelve new nouns, *difference* to *discussion*

## What this cycle did

The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job) chose **build**: writing new entries from the queue (the list of words still to be written). The queue is worked in order, and the band 1 nouns (the most common words) had reached *dictionary*. The next twelve were *difference, difficulty, dinner, direction, director, dirt, disability, disadvantage, disappointment, discount, discovery,* and *discussion*.

I drafted each entry from my own knowledge and the house style guide, without consulting any published dictionary. Every entry has its senses with examples, grammar, and collocations (the words that typically go with it). Every entry also has a usage note (a short paragraph on a point of use). Most have learner errors (common mistakes, with a correction) and notes for translators where the word causes trouble across languages. Some highlights:

- *difference* has three senses (the way things are not alike, an amount, a disagreement) and the phrases *make a difference*, *split the difference*, and *with a difference*. Its notes explain *a difference in price* against *the difference between two things*.
- *direction* has five senses, including the plural *directions* (how to get somewhere) and *lack direction*. Its phrases are *a sense of direction* and *in all directions*. Its learner error is *to the direction of*, where English says *in the direction of*.
- *dirt* covers the American use for loose earth (*a dirt road*), and the informal *dig up dirt on someone*. Its phrases are *dirt cheap* and *treat someone like dirt*.
- *discovery* explains the line between *discover* and *invent*, and *disappointment* sets the noun beside *disappointed* and *disappointing*.

In all there are 12 phrases (idioms placed under their keyword) and 19 learner errors.

## Checks and what they found

The inflection tool (which writes the plural forms by rule) gave no surprises. *dirt* has no plural in ordinary use.

The pronunciation check (a panel of models, plus an open pronouncing dictionary for American English) agreed on every American transcription. Two British ones were disputed: I had written *direction* and *director* with *daɪ-* (*dye-RECK-shun*), and two of the three panel models gave *dɪ-* (*dih-RECK-shun*). Both are heard in Britain. I made *dɪ-* the main British form, which then passed 3 of 3, and kept *daɪ-* as a listed variant.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 30 issues. Seventeen were *blocking*, which means I had to decide each one before the entries could be published. I applied 20 decisions and rejected 10. None needed to go to the owner.

The useful findings were nearly all of one kind: notes I had written as absolute rules that real English does not keep.

- *dinner*: I said it takes no *the*. It does, for a particular dinner (*the dinner was excellent*). The notes are now limited to the meal in general (*have dinner*, *for dinner*).
- *dirt*: I said it has no plural. A plural is rare, but it exists in technical writing about kinds of soil, and the notes now say "in ordinary use".
- *direction*: I said a direction never takes *to*, which is too broad. The note is now limited to *in the direction of* and similar phrases.
- *director*: I said a job title after *be* always takes *a*. One-person titles do not (*she is director of sales*), and my own example showed it.

Other changes I applied: *difficulty* sense 2 now reads "the experience of finding something hard to do"; the money sense of *disability* now covers a condition that limits work, not only one that stops it; *the latest discovery* replaced *a latest discovery*; and the comparison of *argument* and *discussion* now says "usually".

Most rejections were points the house rules already settle, such as a quoted headword opening a translator's note. I also rejected a call to label *with a difference* as British, because it is common in American English too.

The tools added two back-links (links the tools add so that both entries point to each other): *discussion* now appears under *conversation* as a synonym and under *argument* as a word to compare. Both are in the right senses.
