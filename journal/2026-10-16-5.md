# 2026-10-16, cycle 1 of the 12:44 UTC run: second review of *niece* to *officer*, and five more entries

## What this cycle did

This was a **review** cycle: a second reading of entries that had been through the two outside reviewers (the review panel) only once. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose review because it was the kind of work most overdue, and it picks the oldest such entries first. This block was twenty entries drafted on 2026-10-06: fifteen nouns in alphabetical order (*niece, night, noise, noon, nose, note, novel, number, nurse, nut, object, ocean, offer, office, officer*) and five entries drafted in the same hour from other parts of the alphabet: the verbs *bug, bus, butter, capitalize*, and the noun *history*.

Each entry went to both reviewers again. I read every issue they raised and decided whether to change the entry (logged as *applied*) or leave it (logged as *rejected*, with a reason). I also read each entry myself for anything missing.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 30 issues; reviewer-b raised one. Five entries drew no issues at all (*night, noon, ocean, office, butter*). I applied 16 and rejected 15 (reviewer-b's one issue included). No question needed the owner.

Applied, among others:

- ***nut***, the metal sense: the entry called a bolt *a thin metal bar*. The definition now says the nut turns onto *the end of a metal pin*, and the explanation describes the bolt properly: a pin with a flat top at one end and a raised line winding around the other, which fits the inside of the nut. (A first rewrite used the words *screw* and *bolt* in the definition itself; the vocabulary check rejected them, because neither has an entry yet, so they moved to the explanation.)
- ***object***, the grammar sense: an object can be a clause as well as a noun, a pronoun, or a phrase. Added.
- ***novel***: its learner error said *a novel about the history of science* was a mistake, but a novel can be about anything. The example is now *a novel about how to grow vegetables*, which is clearly a how-to book. The usage note now says *a nonfiction book* instead of calling one book *a nonfiction*.
- ***nurse***, the old sense *a woman who looks after children in a home*: its two collocations (*a children's nurse*, *a live-in nurse*) now mostly mean medical nurses, so they were removed.
- ***history***, the idiom *be history*: the definition joined two meanings with a semicolon and named a subject inside it. It is now one phrase (*be finished, gone, or no longer important*), and the use about a person (*one more mistake and you're history*) is in an explanation, as the style guide asks.
- ***capitalize***: an explanation repeated what the grammar pattern and collocations already show (*capitalize on something*). Removed.
- Small collocation fixes on *noise*, *offer*, and *officer* (*a chief executive officer* is a job title, not this sense), and the *music* domain label came off *number* in the sense *a song or act in a show*, since it covers dances too.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- *Follow your nose* keeps both meanings, *go straight ahead* and *do what you feel is right*. The first is a real, common use, especially when giving directions.
- *On the nose* (exactly) keeps its *American* label. In this dictionary a region label means *mainly used there*.
- The childcare sense of *nurse* keeps its *old-fashioned* label; the usual word today is *nanny*, which the entry gives as a synonym.
- *I received a job proposal* stays as a learner error on *offer*: when a company offers you a job, the phrase is *job offer*.
- *Tell me the history of your trip* stays as a learner error on *history*: for what happened on a trip, a learner needs *story*.
- The note on *bus* (*bused* usual in American English, *bussed* more common in British) stays; the reviewer's own reason contradicted itself.
- reviewer-b's one issue, on *bug*, said in its own reason that the definition was fine.

## My own additions

- The idiom *keep your nose to the grindstone* (keep working hard) added to *nose*.
- A translator's note on *niece*: English uses one word for the daughter of a brother or a sister, on either side of the family; many languages use different words.
- Plain pronunciation notes added to fifteen entries that had none, for example *noise* (rhymes with *boys*), *nose* (sounds like *knows*), *novel* (*NOV-ul*), *object* (*OB-jekt*), and *butter* (in American English the *tt* often sounds like a quick *d*).
