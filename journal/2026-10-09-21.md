## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The panel raised 23 issues, 16 of them marked blocking. All came from reviewer-a; reviewer-b answered every field of every entry and found nothing to object to. I agreed with 11 and fixed the entry myself, and rejected 12 with a logged reason. None needed the owner.

Fixes I agreed with:

- *end*: the example *There's no end to his questions* was under the sense "the point where something stops", but it really means "an unlimited amount", like the phrase *no end of*. It was replaced.
- *enemy*: the note said *the enemy* takes a singular or plural verb without saying where. It now says American English uses a singular verb and British English either; the explanation and the translator's note were brought into line.
- *energy*: the note claimed the plural *energies* is used only for a person's effort; scientists also use it, so the note now says "mainly". A learner-error note implied that only machines *use* energy; it now explains the difference without that restriction.
- *engine*: the translator's note now says English usually calls an electric machine a *motor* and a fuel-burning one an *engine*, matching the definition.
- *entrance*: the sense "coming in" was defined as *the moment* someone comes in; it is now *a time when* someone comes in, often in a way people notice.
- *error* and *bug*: the two were listed as synonyms. A bug is the fault in a program; an error is the problem it causes or the message about it. They are now "compare" links with a note, in both entries.
- *equipment*: the "compare" link to *machine* now carries a note saying how the two differ.

The main rejections: reviewer-a said *emergency* should be countable only, but *in case of emergency* is an uncountable use. It said *employee* and *employer* do not "differ only in their endings", but they do. It called *effort* a poor synonym of *energy*, though the entry's own example (*puts a lot of energy into her work*) shows them interchangeable. It wanted the *engine* definition widened to electric machines, which English normally calls motors. It objected that *material* is not a synonym of *equipment*, but the link matches the *material* sense "the things you need for an activity", which points back to *equipment*.

After the panel, the vocabulary checker flagged *occasion* in my new *entrance* definition (a word outside the plain defining vocabulary with no entry yet), so it became *a time when*.
