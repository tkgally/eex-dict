## What the entries contain

Each entry has the senses a learner needs, with idioms, word combinations, a learner error, and notes for translators. Some highlights:

- *shadow*: four senses (the dark shape, a dark area, dark skin under the eyes, a sad influence) and three idioms, including *a shadow of your former self*. A note sets out the *shade*/*shadow* choice (you sit in the shade, not the shadow, on a hot day).
- *shame*: the feeling, the loss of respect, and *a shame* meaning *a pity*, with a note that *It's a shame* is polite and carries no guilt.
- *shape*: five senses, including *in good shape* for condition, and idioms such as *take shape* and the informal American *bent out of shape*.
- *sheep*, *sheet*, *ship*, *shirt*: each has a pronunciation note for translators, because a short or long vowel turns one word into another (*sheep*/*ship*), or into a vulgar word.
- *shoe*: *in someone's shoes*, *fill someone's shoes*, and the American *the shoe is on the other foot* (British *the boot*).

Before the panel, the vocabulary check found four definition words outside the defining vocabulary (the 2,394 plain words definitions must use): *spoils*, *embarrassed*, *triangle*, and *bet*. I reworded those definitions.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Reviewer-b returned a complete reading of every field and found nothing. Reviewer-a raised 16 issues. I agreed with 13 and rewrote the fields in my own words:

- Four idiom definitions were too narrow or slightly off: *put someone to shame* also compares things; *lose your shirt* is about risking money, not a bad business plan in particular; *give someone the shirt off your back* means being very generous; *abandon ship* also covers a ship in great danger, not only one sinking.
- *shell* as a weapon is the object that is fired, not a case.
- The *sheet* sense for rain (*coming down in sheets*) is now *rain falling together*, not rain that "moves as one".
- The *ship*/*boat* note said *boat* is used for ships of any size; it now says *boat* is used for some ships, mostly ones that carry passengers.
- The *shirt* note said a T-shirt has no collar or buttons; it now says *usually*.
- My *shoe* learner error (*Wait, I'm wearing my shoes!*) could be correct English in another situation; I replaced it with one that is clearly wrong (*Every morning I wear my shoes and leave the house*).

I rejected three related flags claiming that *have a shock* is not common. It is common in both American and British English (*she had a terrible shock*).
