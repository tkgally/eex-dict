## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: new entries from the queue, the waiting list of words. I wrote twelve entries, and all twelve passed the full checking pipeline and are now `reviewed`. They will appear on the site at the next build.

Two of the twelve were words that the baton file `NEXT.md` asked to be claimed by hand. The first was *hood* in its slang sense, an American word for the part of a city where someone lives or grew up (*back in the hood*). It has no connection with the *hood* of a coat, so it is a separate homograph entry, `hood-n-2`. A homograph is a word with the same spelling but an unrelated meaning. The second was the adjective *grown-up*. The queue listed it as *grown up* with no hyphen, so I wrote it with the hyphen that it takes before a noun. The two-word spelling, common after a verb (*the children are grown up now*), is recorded as a variant.

The other ten came from the queue in its usual order: *hurricane*, *ice skating*, *identification*, *idiom*, *idiot*, *imagination*, *immigrant*, *immigration*, *implication*, and *impression*. Among the details worth noting:
- *Idiom* has a note for translators. In some languages a word that looks like *idiom* means a whole language.
- *Immigrant* has a short box that explains the difference between *immigrant*, *emigrant*, and *migrant*. It also has a usage note saying that many people find *illegal immigrant* offensive.
- *Impression* has the idiom *be under the impression that* and a learner error: *make an impression to someone* instead of *on someone*.

**Pronunciation.** Every transcription is checked by a panel of models and, for American English, against an open pronouncing dictionary that is consulted at run time and never stored. Eleven entries passed on the first try. *Grown-up* did not. I had put the main stress on *up*, but no panel model and not the open dictionary agreed with that. The word does take that stress after a verb (*she's all grown-UP*), but before a noun, which is its most common use, the stress moves to the start (*a GROWN-up son*). I rewrote the transcription with the main stress on *grown*, and the panel then agreed, 3 votes of 4. The note for translators explains the shift.

**What the reviewers found.** Every entry goes to two automated reviewers from different companies. Between them they raised 43 issues. Seven were blocking, meaning serious enough to stop an entry from being published until they are settled. Six of the twelve entries had no issues, or just one.
- *Grown-up*: one example (*my little brother is so grown-up*) belonged to the "behaving sensibly" sense, not the "adult" sense where I had put it, and its *so* clashed with that sense's "not gradable" code. I replaced the example. A usage note also wrongly called a whole sentence a past participle; I removed the note and moved its useful fact to the variant.
- *Identification*: the "feeling" sense (*a strong identification with the character*) was coded uncountable while its own examples used *a*. It is now coded countable or uncountable. The fact that people usually say *ID* was stated twice; it now appears once.

Of the 43 issues I applied 38 and rejected 5, each with a logged reason. The useful minor fixes included clearer definitions of *hurricane* and *immigration*, shorter signposts (the one-to-three-word labels that help readers pick a sense), and replacing examples that showed the wrong sense. One rejection: a reviewer wanted the general "recognizing" sense of *identification* first. I kept "papers that show who you are" first, because that is the sense learners meet most often (*Can I see some identification?*).

**Cross-references.** When a new entry names another entry as a synonym or a word to compare, the other entry should point back. The tool added four of these links itself. Six needed a person to choose the right sense, and I placed them by hand on *adult*, *storm*, *expression*, *phrase*, and *consequence*.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: re-reading entries that are already published to make them better. There were no unfinished drafts and no entry reports (public issues saying an entry is wrong), so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That is a reading of the most important everyday entries, the most common words in the dictionary, to check that each one has every current meaning and the idioms a learner will meet. The pass goes through entries with five senses, in alphabetical order, skipping any read since 24 October. This cycle took the next four: *load* (noun), *look* (noun), *lot* (noun), and *man* (noun). The pass has now read 271 entries.

**What I added before the reviewers saw the entries.**
- *Load*: a technical subsense for the weight that a wall or a bridge supports. The washing sense now covers dishes as well as clothes (*a full load in the dishwasher*).
- *Look*: the style sense now covers rooms and designs, not only clothes and hair (*We gave the kitchen a whole new look*).
- *Lot*: a subsense for a film studio's lot, the land and buildings where films are made.
- *Man*: two missing senses. One is the sense of courage or strength that people expect of a man (*be a man and tell her the truth*, *man enough to admit it*). The other is *man* as a very informal word for speaking to anyone (*Hey, man, what's up?*). I also added the idiom *a man of the world*.

**What the reviewers found.** The two automated reviewers raised 41 issues, 2 of them blocking (serious enough to stop an entry from being published until settled). Both were on *man*:
- One reviewer said that *man* used to address someone (*Hey, man*) is an interjection and should not be a noun sense. I rejected this. A word used to address a person is still a noun, just as a name is. The interjection is the separate exclamation *Man, that was close!*, which the usage note already sends to its own entry.
- A note claimed that *the history of man* was an exception to the rule that this sense takes no article. It is not an exception, since *the* belongs to *history*. I corrected the note.

Of the 41 issues I applied 32 and rejected 9, each with a logged reason. The most useful fixes:
- *Lot*: the British uses *the lot* (*he ate the lot*) and *you lot* were only mentioned inside a note. They are now proper phrases with their own definitions and British labels. The "batch" sense (*the next lot of visitors*) is now labeled British. A grammar fact that appeared twice now appears once. The example *"A lot!"* went, because used alone like that it is not this noun. *Mass* went as a synonym, along with the matching link on the *mass* entry.
- *Man*: the old sense meaning "all people" (*made by man*) moved from second place to fifth, because the newer senses are more useful to a learner. I added the idioms *the odd man out* and *the old man* (someone's father or husband). The definition of the worker sense no longer uses the word *man* itself.
- *Look* and *load*: several definitions are tighter, and the note for translators on *look* now names the three meanings that often need separate words.

Among the rejections: *loads of* is common in informal American speech, not only British, so it keeps no region label. The worry sense of *load* stays separate from the work sense, because it has its own collocations (*lighten the load*).

**A small catch.** One fix wrote *auction* into the definition and the core idea of *lot*. The dictionary has no entry for *auction* yet, and a definition must use only words that the dictionary can explain. The vocabulary check caught it, and both now say "a public sale" instead.
