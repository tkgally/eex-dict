# Journal, 2026-11-13 (second run of the day, run v4afhu)

## Cycle 1: closure

**What the cycle was for.** The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from what is most overdue) chose *closure*: building entries for words the dictionary already uses but has no entry for. The first closure source (words used in definitions) is empty, so the twelve words came from the *family* source: derived or related words that existing entries already point to in their word-family lists. All twelve are in frequency band 2 (common words, just below the core vocabulary).

**What was built.** Twelve new noun entries, all now marked `reviewed` (they passed every step of the checking pipeline and appear on the site):

- **nursery**: a baby's room, a place that looks after young children (labelled British, with a note that American English says *day care* or *preschool*), and a place where young plants are grown.
- **opening**: a hole or gap, the first part of a story or speech, the moment something opens or becomes available (*the opening of the new bridge*), an opening ceremony, a job that is free, and a chance to say or do something.
- **operator**: a person who works a machine, a telephone operator, a company that runs a service (*a tour operator*), and the informal *a smooth operator*.
- **outline**: the shape of an edge, and a short summary, with the phrase *in outline*.
- **ownership**: owning something, and the feeling of being responsible (*take ownership of*).
- **packaging**: the materials a product is sold in, and the way something is presented to the public.
- **painter**: an artist, and a person who paints buildings.
- **paperwork**: office work with forms and records, and the documents needed for something, with a learner error (*paperworks*).
- **partnership**: working together, and a business owned by partners, with a sub-sense for the position of being a partner.
- **pass**: a travel or entry document, a pass in sports, an exam result (British), a mountain pass, and one movement over something, with the phrases *make a pass at someone*, *take a pass on something*, and *give someone a free pass*.
- **passage**: a narrow way between walls, a part of a text or piece of music, a way through (with a sub-sense for the tubes in the body that air goes through), the passage of time, a journey by ship, the right to travel through a place, and the passing of a law.
- **paste**: a soft mixture, a food paste (*tomato paste*), and a paste for sticking paper.

All 24 pronunciations (American and British) were agreed by the pronunciation panel, and the plurals came from the regular rules (**ownership**, **packaging**, and **paperwork** have none).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 55 issues, 5 of them blocking (a blocking issue must be settled before an entry can be published). All five were right:

- *The team made a strong opening* is not natural English; it became a movie example. (Both reviewer records counted it, so it is two of the five.)
- *She hopes to make partnership* is wrong; the idiom is *make partner*. The example was replaced.
- In **passage**, the law sense was marked uncountable, but its example said *an easy passage*. It is now marked singular only, and *through the government* became *through parliament*.

Of the minor issues, I applied 47 and rejected 3. The main changes: **passage** was restructured, because two senses each joined two different meanings; the text sense moved up to second place, and a steam example that made a health claim was replaced. **opening** gained a sense for the act of opening (*the opening of a bank account*). **nursery** now puts the baby's-room sense first, since American English is the dictionary's main variety. Two examples that used British wording (*mobile phone operator*, and *math* in a British-labelled sense) were changed. I rejected a call to label a sense "figurative" (no such label exists in the closed list), a complaint that **ownership** is defined through **own** (defining a derived word through its base word is house style), and a request to put *rite of passage* under **passage** (idioms go under their first noun, so it belongs to **rite**, which I queued).

Six cross-references from the new entries pointed at entries where the script could not tell which sense to link back from, so I placed those back-links by hand (in **end**, **opportunity**, **shape**, **corridor**, **hallway**, and **glue**).

**Spend.** US$0.42 on the review panel and pronunciation checks, out of a US$3.00 ceiling for the cycle; the day stands at US$1.18 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing broke. Before review, two definition words were outside the defining vocabulary (*acceptance* in **ownership**, *sexual* in a **pass** phrase); I reworded both.

**What is next.** More family rows, then cross-reference rows. *rite of passage* waits for **rite**.

## Cycle 2: build

**What the cycle was for.** The mode selector chose *build*: new entries taken in order from the main word queue (the list of headwords waiting to be written, ordered by importance). I took ten rather than the allowed twelve, so that each entry could be written in full.

**What was built.** Ten new noun entries, all band 2 (common words) and all now `reviewed`:

- **occasion**: a particular time (*on several occasions*), a special event (*a special occasion*), and a formal sense of reason or need (*I've never had occasion to use it*). It has four phrases: *on occasion*, *on the occasion of something*, *rise to the occasion*, and *a sense of occasion*.
- **onion**: the vegetable, with a sub-sense for the long green kind (called *green onion* in American English and *spring onion* in British English).
- **opponent**: someone you play or compete against, and someone who argues against an idea or a plan.
- **opposite**: a person or thing as different as possible from another, with the saying *opposites attract*.
- **option**: a choice (with a sub-sense for an extra feature you can choose when buying something such as a car), the right to choose (*we had no option but to wait*), a choice on a computer menu, and the financial right to buy or sell at a fixed price. It has the phrase *keep your options open*.
- **orchestra**: the group of musicians, and the American use for the theater seats nearest the stage. A note for translators warns that the **ch** sounds like **k**.
- **outcome**: a result.
- **outfit**: a set of clothes, and an informal word for a small company or team.
- **outlet**: a wall socket for electricity (American), a store, a way to release feelings (*an outlet for stress*), a news company, and a pipe through which liquid flows out.
- **overtime**: extra working time, the money paid for it, and extra time in a sports game (American). Its adverb use (*she works overtime*) belongs to a separate entry, which I added to the queue.

All 20 pronunciations were agreed by the pronunciation panel, and all plurals come from the regular rules (**overtime** has none).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 17 issues, none blocking. I applied 16:

- Both reviewers noticed that *green onion* and *spring onion* were listed under a definition that says "a round vegetable", although those onions are long and thin. They now have their own sub-sense.
- **option** gained the "optional extra" use and a wider signpost (a short label that tells senses apart) for the financial sense.
- **occasion** gained the formal *on the occasion of something*.
- A British *smart outfit* became *a nice outfit*. The sports sense of **overtime** now has the domain label *sport* instead of *games*, and several collocations were moved to the right type.
- An example (*I thought he would be angry, but he did the opposite*) was changed, because being angry is not something you "do". The saying *opposites attract* is now defined with the house formula for sayings: "used to say that ...".

I rejected one issue. Reviewer B wanted *option* to be marked uncountable because of *no option but to*. But *no option* is an ordinary countable noun after *no*, just like *no choice*.

Before the panel I caught two definition words outside the defining vocabulary (*classical* in **orchestra** and *electrical* in **outlet**) and rewrote both definitions. I also changed an example's British *at the weekend* to American *on the weekend*. Four back-links that the cross-reference script could not place were placed by hand (**reason**, **choice** twice, **result**).

**Spend.** US$0.30; the day stands at US$1.48 of US$15.

I caught it at once and restored the file from the merged version before anything was committed. No harm reached the dictionary.
