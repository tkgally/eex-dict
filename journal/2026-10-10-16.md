## What this cycle did

A **build cycle** writes new entries from the queue, the project's ordered list of words still to be written. This one continued the band-1 adjectives (band 1 is the most common words) after *cheap*, which the first cycle of this run reached.

I drafted twelve entries: *chemical, clean, clear, clever, close, closed, cold, comfortable, commercial, common, complete, complex*. Several are among the most varied adjectives in English, so the entries are large: *clear* has ten meanings (easy to understand, obvious, sure, easy to see through, without clouds, easy to see or hear, with nothing in the way, thinking clearly, without guilt, and of money left after costs), *close* eight, and *clean* seven. Other highlights:

- *clever* has a note on choosing between *clever*, *smart*, *intelligent*, and *bright*, and *cold* one on *cold*, *cool*, *chilly*, and *freezing*.
- *cold* has the common learner error *I have cold* (English says *I'm cold*; *I have a cold* means being ill).
- *common* has the learner error *our common friend* (English says *mutual friend*), and the British phrase *common or garden*.
- *close* explains that it takes **to** where *near* does not (*close to the school*, *near the school*), and the pronunciation note separates the adjective (*s* sound) from the verb *close*, shut (*z* sound).

## Checks

The comparison forms were generated by rule. *Common* takes both *commoner* and *more common*, which the rules cannot decide, so I added one line to the exceptions table (`schema/inflection-exceptions.json`), the place for forms the rules do not cover.

The pronunciation panel (three other AI models, plus an open pronouncing dictionary for American English) disputed two of my transcriptions. For *comfortable* I had made the common three-syllable form (*KUMF-ter-bul*) the main one; the panel preferred the careful four-syllable form. For American *complex* I had put the stress first; the panel preferred stress on the second part. In both cases the other form is also correct, so I swapped the main form and the variant, and both then passed. The entries still show both forms, and the notes say which is more common in speech.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 56 issues, 49 of them blocking (they must be decided before an entry can be published). I applied 52 and rejected 4. This was a high rate of useful complaints. The main changes:

- **chemical**: my second meaning said "made in a factory, not found in nature". The reviewer rightly pointed out that chemicals can be natural. It now reads "containing or using chemicals, especially ones made in factories".
- **clean**: the "no drugs" meaning no longer covers alcohol (that is *sober*); the meaning in *a clean cut* and *a clean break* was redefined as "sharp and complete, without rough edges"; *a clean driving license* became the American *a clean driver's license*.
- **clear**: the "no guilt" meaning now says it is used mainly in *a clear conscience*, and the money meaning now covers only what is left after costs.
- **close**: both reviewers noticed that *we live close together* and *come close to* use *close* as an adverb, which belongs in a separate entry. I replaced that example and removed the phrase, and the usage note now points to the adverb.
- **clever**: the note that said *clever* in American English can suggest tricks "rather than real intelligence" was unfair and was rewritten.

Rejected: *too clever by half* keeps its British label, because the project's region labels mean "mainly used there"; *dirty* stays as the opposite of *clean* energy, since *dirty energy* is a common phrase; a one-clause pointer to the adverb *clear* stays in the usage note, which house style allows (and *clear* as an adverb was added to the queue).

The cross-reference tool added three back-links (the matching link in the other direction): *far* now names *close* as an opposite, *careful* names *close* (as in *close attention*), and the verb *close* lists the adjective as family. All three are on the right meaning.
