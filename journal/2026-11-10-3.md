A lint pass writes no new dictionary content. **What the checks found and what changed.**

- *Size caps and links.* Every capped file is under its limit, and every link inside the knowledge base resolves.
- *Mirror cross-references.* When entry A names entry B as a synonym, antonym, compare word, or word-family member, B should usually name A back. The cross-reference tool added these mirror links to six entries built earlier today: *imagination*, *impression*, *indication*, *lab*, *landing*, and *landlord*. In five cases the tool could not tell which sense of the target entry the link belonged to, so it wrote nothing and left them for me. I placed each by hand: *introduction* (the first part of a book or speech) now has *conclusion* as its opposite; *impression* (an idea formed quickly) lists *feeling* as a synonym; *imagination* (thinking of new ideas) lists *invention*; *kingdom* (a country ruled by a king or queen) points to *republic* for comparison; and *grown-up* (having become an adult) lists *small* as an opposite, matching *small* in the sense "young and not yet grown up". These are link-only changes; no definition or example was touched.
- *Claim files.* When a run reserves words to write, it leaves a small "claim" file so two runs cannot take the same word. Six of these, from runs that have finished and merged, were removed.
- *Frequency bands.* Every word to be written is given a band from 1 (most common) to 5 by a vote of three inexpensive language models. Forty-two newly queued words were still waiting for their band; the vote cost less than one US cent. Only one word split widely among the voters: *barbershop*, voted 2, 3, and 4. I kept the middle vote, 3, because it is an everyday place word but less frequent than *barber*. None of the 42 words has an entry yet, so no existing entry needed its band updated.
- *Reviewer precision.* For each of the two automatic reviewers, the project tracks what share of their objections the session accepted, broken down by type of objection. A type that falls below 30 percent after twenty or more decisions is switched off for that reviewer, because its objections cost more to read than they return. Nothing fell under the line this time. The second reviewer's objections about inflected forms (plurals, past tenses) have now passed twenty decisions at 35 percent, just above the line, so they stay on. Its spelling-and-format objections remain low, at 34 percent, but stay on by your earlier ruling.
- *Reports for later review cycles.* The checks list 320 definitions that use their own headword (most are harmless), 997 pairs of entries whose cross-references may point at the wrong sense, and 6 apparent broken promises (all already known to be false alarms). These feed future review cycles; they are not errors to fix today.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: re-reading entries that are already published to make them more complete. There are no unfinished drafts and no reader reports open, so the work came from the coverage pass you asked for on 2026-10-24 (ruling B1): a second, careful reading of the most important everyday words (frequency band 1), starting with those that have the most senses, to add any common meaning, phrase, or use that the first drafting missed. This cycle took the next four entries in that order: *measure* (noun), *mix* (verb), *new* (adjective), and *oil* (noun). The pass now stands at 279 entries.

**What I added before the reviewers saw the entries.**

- *measure*: a proposed law that people vote on (*Voters will decide on the measure in November*), marked as mainly American; a fixed amount of strong alcohol served in a bar, marked as mainly British; and the phrases *take the measure of someone* and *beyond measure*.
- *mix*: the use in *Drinking and driving don't mix*, meaning that two things cannot go together without causing problems. I also removed a usage note that said the same thing as the entry's learner-error note (the *mix up* trap). The house rule is that one fact lives in one place.
- *new*: *new potatoes* (vegetables picked early in the season) and the phrase *like new*, as in a used bike still in very good condition.
- *oil*: *an oil*, meaning a painting done in oil paint; the phrase *like oil and water*; and *essential oils* as a collocation. I removed a usage note that repeated the learner-error note, and cut a generic "translate each sense separately" clause from the note for translators. Your notes rules of 2026-10-24 treat that kind of clause as an issue.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two automatic reviewers (two different language models that check every field and give closed verdicts) raised 44 issues, none of them blocking. I accepted 31 and rejected 13, each with a logged reason. The useful ones:

- *measure*: the first definition said *an official action*, but ordinary people take measures too, so *official* went. I had first written the phrase as *get the measure of someone*, but that is mainly British. It is now *take the measure of someone*, with the British form named in its explanation. Both reviewers caught an example with British *had got*. Reviewer A also pointed out that *made to measure* was missing, and it is now added as a British phrase.
- *mix*: both reviewers said the everyday sense *mix a drink, mix cement* should come before the specialist sense about mixing recorded music. It now does. The vague definition *do or have two or more different things together* became *combine different activities, ideas, or styles in one thing or at one time*, which fits both *mix business and pleasure* and *a book that mixes history with fiction*.
- *new* and *oil*: both reviewers said *recent* is not a true synonym of *new* in *a new car* (no one says *a recent car*), so it now appears as a word to compare instead. *fat* and cooking *oil* were treated the same way. In both cases the link in the other entry was changed to match. Reviewer A noticed that *strike oil* was missing, and it is added. Both reviewers saw that the learner-error note called cooking oil simply uncountable while the sense allows *different oils*; the note now says that the liquid is uncountable and that *an oil* means one kind of oil. Two examples that did not fit their senses were replaced: oiling a door now oils its *hinges*, and *Happy New Year!* is gone from the sense *just beginning*.

The rejections were mostly requests the house rules settle: a second example for subsenses (one is allowed), the format of a translator note's opening word (allowed by the reviewers' house rules), and a request to drop *former* as the opposite of *new* in *my new address / my former address*, where it is the natural pair. Reviewer A also wanted the *new/news* usage note removed. I kept it, because learners really do write *a good new*.
