# 2026-10-16, cycle 4 of the 00:44 UTC run: twelve adverbs, *mostly* to *now*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: twelve new entries drafted from the queue (the list of words still to be written), continuing the very common (band 1) adverbs after *most*. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build again because it was still the job furthest behind its share. The twelve new entries, all adverbs:

*mostly*, *much*, *naturally*, *near*, *nearly*, *necessarily*, *never*, *next*, *no*, *normally*, *not*, and *now*.

This batch holds some of the hardest grammar words in English. *Not* explains how English makes negatives (*do not* with ordinary verbs, the short form *n't*, *I hope not*), with the classic learner errors *I not like it* and *I think he is not coming*. *Never* and *no* both warn against a second negative (*I don't never eat meat*). *Much* explains why English says *very hot* but *much hotter*, and where *very much* goes (*I like football very much*, not *I like very much football*). *Nearly* has a short box on choosing between *nearly* and *almost*. *Mostly* corrects the very common *Mostly people like it* (it should be *Most people*).

Every entry went through the full pipeline (format check, inflections, pronunciation, both outside reviewers, my decision on each issue, vocabulary and cross-reference checks). All 24 transcriptions were confirmed on the first try. Only *much* (*more*, *most*) and *near* (*nearer*, *nearest*) have their own comparative forms, both from the project's table of irregular forms.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 41 issues and reviewer-b 7. I accepted 24 and rejected 24.

One reply was incomplete. reviewer-a's first answer on *near* left fourteen fields without a verdict, which the project does not accept. I ran reviewer-a again on that entry alone, and its second answer was complete.

Accepted, among others:

- *Never*, sense 2 (*I never knew you could sing!*, where *never* just means *didn't*): the definition is now simply *did not; not at all*, and the detail moved to the explanation. The *informal* label was dropped, since this is ordinary speech.
- *Next*: the opening summary used *the next step* and *the next time* as examples, but those are adjective uses. It was rewritten.
- *No*: the explanation now says what *no more than* (*only*) and *no less than* (*as much as*, often in surprise) mean before a number.
- *Now*: *then* is no longer listed as its opposite. A second learner error that read unnaturally was removed. The conjunction *now that* was added to the queue as its own entry.
- *Not* and *nearly*: two rules that were stated too absolutely now say *not normally* and *usually*.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- reviewer-a argued, eight times on *near* and three times on *next*, that *near impossible* and *the next best thing* use those words as adjectives. They don't: *near* modifies the adjective *impossible*, and *next* modifies *best*, and a word that modifies an adjective is an adverb.
- reviewer-a also objected several times to short two-part definitions such as *usually; most of the time*. That form is normal house style.
- reviewer-b wanted the usage note on *now* to leave out *now that*. The note only points the reader to that separate word, which is what the style guide asks for.

One cross-reference needed attention. *A lot* (adverb) already listed *much* as a synonym, while the new *much* had *a lot* under *compare*. I matched the older entry, so both now list each other as synonyms.
