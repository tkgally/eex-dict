# 2026-09-29 (11) — Build: twelve verbs, *surprise* to *think*

## What this run did

This was the third cycle of the scheduled run that began at 12:44 UTC. (A "cycle" is one unit of work with its own pull request; a run has at most four.) The first two cycles, a review of fourteen function words and the periodic originality check, merged as pull requests #134 and #135.

The mode selector chose **build**: new entries from the queue of words waiting to be written. The queue gave the next twelve verbs from band 1, the most basic words: *surprise, survive, suspect, swallow, sweep, swell, swim, taste, teach, tear, test,* and *think*. I wrote each one in full:

- **46 senses** in all. *think* has six: opinion, using your mind, considering doing something, remembering or finding an idea, expecting, and imagining.
- **7 idioms**, such as *think twice*, *think better of something*, *think nothing of something*, and *that'll teach you*.
- **24 learner errors**, mistakes that learners of many languages make, for example:
  - *I was surprising when I heard the news*
  - *Nobody survived from the accident*
  - *He learned me English*
  - *Can you teach me the way to the station?*
  - *It tastes well*
  - *I think not so*
  - *I will test to open the door*
- Notes for translators on real traps. *taste* covers three meanings that some languages split. *teach* and *learn* share one verb in some languages. In English the negative usually goes with *think*: *I don't think she's here*.

The inflected forms came from the project's rules and its table of irregular verbs (*swept, swollen, swam/swum, taught, tore/torn, thought*). All 24 pronunciations were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewer panel (two outside language models that check every field) raised 46 issues, 27 of them blocking (a blocking issue must be settled before an entry counts as reviewed). I applied 35 and rejected 11; none needed the owner.

What I accepted:

- *surprise* sense 2 said the unexpected thing is "something nice", but *They surprised us by arriving a day early* need not be nice. It now says "often something nice".
- *sweep* in *A long black car swept past us* did not fit a definition about "confidence or pride". The definition now says "often". An example about winning an election was replaced.
- *swell*: an example (*Her eyes were swollen*) showed the adjective, not the verb, and was replaced. Three notes on **swollen** and **swelled** disagreed with each other. They now agree: **swollen** is usual for the body, and **swelled** is also common.
- *tear* sense 3 (*The children tore down the stairs*) said "without care"; it now says "with a lot of energy".
- *test*: the medical sense had an ungrammatical definition. I rewrote it and gave the sense the `medicine` label.
- *think*: an example about remembering an old school sat in the sense about finding a name or an idea. I replaced it with *I couldn't think of anything to say*.
- Several one-sentence summaries at the top of an entry (the "core idea") were made plainer.
- A learner error on *survive* (*survive with very little money*) was not really an error, and I removed it.

What I rejected, mostly under rules the project has already settled:

- The American transcription *swip* has no length mark. That is the house practice.
- The code "not used in continuous tenses" on *taste* and *think* stays where the learner-error note says "not usually". This is also settled practice.
- A request for a "literary" label on *taste success*, which is ordinary in news writing.
- An objection to a translator's note on *swallow*: some languages use their word for swallow to mean "put up with a person". The note is hedged with "some".

The tool that adds matching links on the other side of a cross-reference put *think* under the wrong sense twice. It went under *remember* "honor the dead" and under *imagine* "believe wrongly". I moved both to the first sense by hand.
