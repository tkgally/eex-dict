## What the run did

The selector, the script that picks each cycle's task, chose **lint**. It forces a lint pass after five cycles without one. A lint pass is a check of the whole dictionary and its notes. Part of it is mechanical, done by scripts: word limits, links, entry validity, and the defining vocabulary. Part of it is done by reading: whether the notes contradict each other, whether the catalog of notes is complete, and whether old questions have gone stale. No entries were written and nothing was spent.

**Mechanical checks.** All were clean.

- Every file is within its word or line limit.
- All 240 pages' internal links resolve.
- All 873 entries pass validation.
- The defining-vocabulary gate has no failures. This gate checks that every word used in a definition is either one of the roughly 2,400 basic defining words or has its own entry.
- A full scan of every entry found no new words to add to the queue.
- Four old claim files were deleted. A claim file is a temporary record that a run is working on certain words; these four belonged to cycles that had already been merged.

**Back-links.** The cross-reference tool mirrors each link from one entry to another. If *bed* lists *bottom* as a synonym, *bottom* should list *bed* in return. Across the whole dictionary, the tool made three new back-links this time. In earlier passes it sometimes attached a back-link to the wrong sense of the target word, so I checked each one by hand. All three are under the right sense:

- *bottom* now points to *bed*, under its sense "the ground under water".
- *boss* now points to *head*, under its sense "leader".
- *bottom* now points to *head* as an opposite, under its sense "lowest part", matching *head*'s sense "top or front".

**Reviewer precision.** The two outside reviewer models each raise issues in categories called families. Precision is the share of a reviewer's issues that I end up applying rather than rejecting. Under a standing rule, a family is switched off for a reviewer when its precision falls below 30 percent over twenty or more decisions. This time no active family is below the line. The closest is the first reviewer's pronunciation family, which stands at exactly 30 percent (47 applied out of 156). Its rejections come almost entirely from one settled rule: American transcriptions here leave out the length mark, and the reviewer keeps asking for it (*weave* and *boot* in this run). If the next pass finds it below 30 percent, it will be switched off. Overall, the first reviewer's precision is 71 percent and the second's is 60 percent. The figures are recorded in `wiki/notes/reviewer-precision.md`.
