## What this run did

This was the second cycle of the run that began at 12:43 UTC on 2026-09-30. (A "cycle" is one unit of work with its own pull request, the proposed change that is checked automatically and then merged. The first cycle, a review of twelve verbs, merged as #150.) The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose **build**: new entries taken from the queue in order.

I claimed and drafted the next twelve band-1 words (band 1 is the most basic level of the dictionary's word list). All twelve are nouns: *attempt, attention, attitude, audience, aunt, author, authority, autumn, baby, back, bacteria,* and *bag*. Each has senses with examples and collocations (words that typically go together), and where they help, idioms, a usage note, notes for translators, and learner errors (mistakes learners of many languages make, each with a correction and a one-line reason).

Some points worth knowing:

- *back* is the largest entry, with five senses (the body, the rear part, the other side, a chair's back, a defending player) and twelve idioms whose key noun is *back*, such as *behind someone's back*, *turn your back on someone*, *know something like the back of your hand*, and the American *have someone's back*.
- *bacteria* is recorded as a plural-only noun. Its usage note and learner errors deal with *this bacteria is* and *bacterias*.
- *autumn* explains that American English mostly says *fall*, and has learner errors for *in last autumn* and for writing the season with a capital letter.
- *attention* warns that in some languages a similar word is shouted to warn of danger, where English says *Watch out!*
- *baby* has idioms such as *throw the baby out with the bathwater* and the British *be left holding the baby*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Every entry went through the full pipeline. The pronunciation check agreed with all 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word). Then two outside language models, reviewer-a and reviewer-b, read every field. One of reviewer-b's replies (on *baby*) could not be read by the tool, so I ran that review again. In total they raised 48 issues, 27 of them marked blocking. I read each one myself: 32 applied, 16 rejected, none sent on to the owner.

Applied, among others:

- *attitude*: the informal sense (*don't give me attitude*) is now defined as an unfriendly or rude way of acting, and it can take *an*. The learner-error note now accepts *toward*, *to*, and *about*.
- *bag*: the travel sense is now defined in the plural as the cases you carry when you travel, the *bags under your eyes* sense no longer says the skin is always dark, and *not my bag* lost its *old-fashioned* label.
- *aunt* (a family friend called *Aunt Sara*) and *author* (the writer of a report or plan) lost *informal* and *formal* labels that did not fit.
- *back to back* now defines both of its meanings, the physical one and "one straight after another", with an example of each.
- *baby* used to a loved partner (*Come on, baby*) now has an ordinary noun definition, with the way it is used moved into an explanation.
- *attempt*: a claim that the **p** is often weak was removed, and *an attempt on the record* is now defined as an effort to *break* a record.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- That *stand at attention* is a countable use. It has no article, so the noun stays uncountable.
- Pronunciation objections to *bacteria* that proposed exactly the transcription we already had.
- Five objections to adaptation notes (notes for translators) that open with the headword in quotation marks. House style allows that.
- That collocations should not carry an article (*a world authority*). That is house practice.

**Cross-references.** The tool that adds matching links in the other direction added one: the noun *permission* now lists *authority* as a synonym. I checked it; it is right.
