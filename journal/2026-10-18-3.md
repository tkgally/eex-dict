# 2026-10-18, cycle 3 of the 00:43 UTC run: twelve new entries, *up* to *yet*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries drafted from the queue (the list of words still to be written, ordered by how basic they are). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build because it was the most overdue kind of work. All twelve words are in band 1, the most basic words, and all are in the defining vocabulary (the plain words the dictionary's own definitions are written in):

- nine adverbs: *up, upstairs, usually, well, when, where, why, yesterday, yet*;
- two numbers: *eight* and *first* (the second and third number entries after *one*);
- *yes*, as an interjection.

The queue had *yes* listed as an adverb. Every use of *yes* is an answer word or an exclamation, and the dictionary already treats *No.* as an answer the same way (the adverb *no* points to a separate interjection entry). So I marked the adverb row as a duplicate and drafted `yes-interj` in its place, the same decision an earlier run made for *please*.

Each entry was drafted from my own knowledge under the style guide, then went through the full pipeline: format and schema checks, inflections from the rules (only *well* changes form: *better*, *best*), the pronunciation check, both outside reviewers, my adjudication, and the vocabulary and cross-reference checks.

## Pronunciation

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 39 issues; reviewer-b raised 5, with complete verdicts. I applied 24 and rejected 20; nothing was escalated. *Yes* drew no issues.

Applied, the main ones:

- ***first***: the third sense joined two meanings, *best in a competition* and *most important* (*first priority*). It now covers only rank: *first prize*, *first place*, *my first choice*. The usage note said *first* usually comes after *the* or *my*; it now also names the fixed phrases without it (*first prize*). The translator's note no longer says *never* about the order *the two first*.
- ***up***: *up to* had one definition for an amount (*up to two hundred people*) and a time (*up to now*); they are now two phrases. The note on word order with phrasal verbs now says it applies to verbs such as *pick up* and *turn up*, not to every phrasal verb. One example of *up and down* had an object after it, which makes it a preposition; it was replaced (*The boat moved up and down on the waves*). The northward sense no longer says *a bigger town*: it now reads *to or in a place that is higher on a map, or to a city or another important place*.
- ***why***: the sense for *the reason why* and *that's why* said the clause gives a reason; in *That's why I'm late* it says what the reason explains. Reworded.
- ***when***: the learner error and translator's note said a *when* question never takes the present perfect; that is too strong (*When have I ever lied to you?*). Both now speak of a single finished event.
- ***where***: the usage note now calls *Where are you at?* common in informal American speech, to be left out of writing, rather than simply nonstandard.
- ***yet***, ***usually***, ***well***: an unnatural example replaced, two notes softened, a collocation (*more than usually*) removed, a semicolon definition fixed.

Rejected, the main ones:

- *What's up?* and *well done* were called interjections that do not belong in adverb entries. The house rule is that idioms stay under their keyword entry whatever their part of speech, so they stay.
- The date use (*the first of June*, *due on the first*) was called a noun. I kept it in the number entry, as `one-num` keeps its pronoun uses; if the owner prefers a separate noun, it is a small move later.
- *The two first chapters* stays as a learner error: it is a common mistake, and the correction is the normal form.

Three definitions also used words outside the defining vocabulary (*north*, *thoroughly*, *superlative*); I reworded them.
