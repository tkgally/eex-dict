This was the fourth and last cycle of the scheduled run that began at 06:44 UTC (a "cycle" is one unit of work, merged on its own; a run may have at most four). The first three cycles' pull requests, #114 to #116, merged before this one started.

The mode selector (the script that decides what each cycle does) chose **review**: a second reading of entries that the reviewer panel (two outside language models that check every field) has read only once. The block was the oldest group still at one round, ten entries written on 2026-09-21: *act* (noun and verb), *activity, address, adjective, advertise, affect, aim* (noun), *air,* and *alarm*.

These entries were among the first written by the scheduled runs, and several were missing common material. Before the panel, I added:

- **act** (noun): a sense for behavior that is not real (*Don't worry; it's all an act*) and three phrases: *get your act together*, *catch someone in the act*, and *a hard act to follow*. One example mentioned "Parliament", an institution of particular countries, and now says "the government".
- **act** (verb): a sense for having an effect (*This medicine acts quickly*), a short note on choosing between *act* and *behave*, and links to the phrasal verbs *act on*, *act up*, and *act out*. An example about a vitamin in the body (a science claim, which house rules keep out of examples) was replaced.
- **activity**: a sense for the things a group does (*criminal activities*, *political activities*). The odd opposite *stillness* became *inactivity*.
- **address**: the American pronunciation *uh-DRESS* recorded beside *AD-dress*, and the spelling error *adress*.
- **affect**: a subsense for illnesses (*The disease mainly affects older people*), and a first definition without the semicolon. There is also a translator's note that *affect* and *effect* sound almost the same.
- **adjective**, **advertise**, **aim**: learner errors (*goods students*; the spelling *advertize*; *with the aim to*).
- **aim**, **air**, **alarm**: phrases such as *with the aim of doing something*, *clear the air*, *out of thin air*, *put on airs*, *raise the alarm*, and *alarm bells ring*.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 20 issues, 6 of them "blocking" (the entry cannot stay marked reviewed until I log a decision). I decided all 20: 11 applied and 9 rejected.

What I accepted:

- **address**: "the number, street, and city" was too narrow; an address can include a flat number, a postal code, or a country. It now reads "the building number, street, town, and other information that show where someone lives or works".
- **aim**: the target-shooting sense now covers both pointing or throwing at something and the skill of doing it. My new phrase *take aim at* mixed a literal and a figurative use. It now covers only the figurative one (*The article takes aim at the city's leaders*), since sense 2 already covers the literal one.
- **air**: the one-sentence summary at the top listed three noun phrases and one that did not match; it is now parallel.
- Five collocation fixes: *act like a child* for the vague *act like something*; collocations for the new *act* sense; *advertise online* moved to the right group; *airfare*, a single word, replaced; *raise the alarm* removed from the collocations because it is now a phrase.

What I rejected, and why:

- The second reviewer asked for *theater* instead of *theatre* in two subject labels. That label value comes from the dictionary's fixed list, so it cannot change.
- The first reviewer said *affect* is also an adjective, as in *an affected person*. That word is *affected*, a different entry.
- It called *They are goods students* an awkward error example. Adding a plural ending to an adjective is a real and common learner error.
- The usual objections to the quoted headword in translators' notes and to the bold words named as words in an example. Both are house style.
