# Run report, 2026-10-28 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What a build cycle is.** A build cycle drafts new dictionary entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to write). Each entry then goes through the full pipeline: a format check, generated word forms, a pronunciation check by outside models and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary, and a field-by-field reading by two reviewers from different labs (reviewer A, a Claude model; reviewer B, Gemini 4 Argon). I settle every objection myself before an entry is marked reviewed. The selector tool chose build because it had the largest backlog among the modes it could pick, and allowed up to twelve entries.

**What was built.** Eleven band-1 words (band 1 is the most frequent group): the nouns *supermarket*, *T-shirt*, *tonight* (as in *tonight's game*), and *west*, and the adjectives *all right*, *dear*, *essential*, *excellent*, *horrible*, *huge*, and *left*. All twenty-two transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed. The fullest entries are *dear* (four senses: the letter greeting, *loved*, the British *expensive*, and an old-fashioned *sweet*), *horrible* (four senses, from *horrible weather* to *I feel horrible about it*), and *west* (three senses, including *the West* as a group of countries, with a note for translators that this is a cultural idea, not a compass direction).

The twelfth word claimed, *TV* as a noun, was not built: the dictionary already has an entry *TV* filed as an abbreviation with both of its senses (the machine and the programs). I marked the queue row as a duplicate. The *television* entry still points to the noun form that will never exist; the site hides such links, and a later review should remove it.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** I made 57 decisions: 38 accepted, 19 rejected. Five objections were "blocking", meaning the entry could not be marked reviewed until they were settled.

- Accepted (blocking, both reviewers): the example *You passed the test? Excellent!* shows *excellent* used alone as an exclamation, which is not the adjective's sense. I replaced it with an ordinary adjective use.
- Rejected (blocking, reviewer B, three times): a request for a *remaining* sense in *left* (*Is there any milk left?*). That use is already sense 7 of the verb *leave*, and the house rules treat such participles as verb uses. The entry's usage note points the reader there.
- Accepted (minor): *left* was defined only for the body, though its examples were a car tire and a road lane; the definition now covers both. *Horrible* gained the *feel ill or guilty* sense that *terrible* already has. *Dear* gained its *sweet* sense, labeled old-fashioned. A translator's note on *all right* that only listed the senses was removed as too general.
- Rejected (minor): idioms whose keyword is another word (*for dear life* goes under *life*, *have two left feet* under *foot*), and reviewer A's repeated claim that *comparative with more* is not a grammar code (it is, in the closed list).

After the reviews, the vocabulary check refused two definitions with words outside the defining vocabulary (the plain word list definitions must use): *collar* in *T-shirt* and *Europe* and *North America* in *the West*. I removed *collar* and redefined *the West* as a group of mostly rich countries sharing ideas about politics and ways of life, with the continents named in the explanation instead. The owner's open question about continent names in definitions would settle this kind of case.

Nine cross-references could not be mirrored automatically (the tool could not tell which sense of the other entry matched), so I placed them by hand: for example, *cheap* sense 1 now names *dear* as its opposite, and *mean* sense 1 names *horrible*.

**Spend.** US$0.31 this cycle; US$2.72 of the US$15 daily budget used so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. One small inconsistency is now visible: *west* has a *the West* sense but *east* has no matching *the East* sense; a review of *east* should add it.

**What is next.** The selector picks the next cycle's mode. Standing items: the coverage pass over the most important band-1 entries, the closure queue, and the cross-reference audit.

**For the owner.** Nothing new. Open items are unchanged: names after titles (*Ms. Kim*), continent names in definitions, *the former* and *the following*, and the line in `PROJECT.md` about non-Anthropic reviewers.

## Cycle 2: review

**What a review cycle is.** A review cycle rereads entries that are already published, sends each one to the same two reviewers, and fixes what they and I find. The selector chose review this time. It works through a fixed order: drafts first (there were none), then known problems, then the "coverage pass". The coverage pass is your ruling B1 of 2026-10-24. It goes through the most important band-1 entries, those with the most senses first, and checks that no common meaning is missing.

**What was read.** Six entries in all.

- *television* and *east*: two problems I found while building in cycle 1. *Television* pointed to a noun entry *TV* that will never exist, because *TV* is filed as an abbreviation. It now points to that entry. It also stated one fact three times (programs are *on* television, not *in* it); that fact now lives only in its learner-error box. *East* had no sense for *the East*, which *west* now has for *the West*. I added it. Its definition avoids continent names, because the vocabulary check rejects them, and the explanation says it usually means Asia.
- *regular*, *read*, *range*, and *pull*: the next four in the coverage pass. That makes seventy-three entries read in the pass so far.

**What was added.**

- *Regular*: an army sense (*the regular army*) and an informal, dated use, *a regular disaster*.
- *Read*: a sense for machines (*the machine couldn't read my card*) and a sub-sense for writing that *reads well* or *reads like a complaint*.
- *Pull*: a sense meaning *attract* (*the concert pulled a huge crowd*) and the idiom *pull a fast one*. Its "stop selling or showing" sense was mixing in a second use, taking someone out of a game, so that use is now a separate sub-sense.
- *Range*: an example of a car's range on a full charge, and a British label on the product-line sense (*our garden range*).

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** I made 75 decisions: 57 accepted, 18 rejected. Five objections were blocking, and I accepted all five:

- *Range* listed *A range of products are on sale* as a learner error. Both reviewers pointed out that a plural verb here is standard English, so I removed it.
- *Read* had an example claiming that cats sleep most of the day. That is a checkable science claim, which the style guide does not allow, so I replaced it.
- *Read* also said its "the sign reads 'Closed'" sense is used mainly in the past tense. That is not true, so I removed the claim.
- *Regular*'s new emphatic sense was first defined with the *used to ...* formula, which is reserved for function words. It now has a plain phrasal definition and a *dated* label.

I also removed two learner errors that the house rules forbid: *readed* (a regularized form) and *I go to the gym regular* (an adjective swapped for an adverb). I rejected requests to add idioms whose keyword is another noun, since they already live in other entries: *read between the lines* is in *line*, and *pull strings* and *pull someone's leg* are under *string* and *leg*. I also rejected two requests for an American *back East* sense. The eastern part of a country is already sense 2.

**Spend.** US$0.30 this cycle; US$3.03 of the US$15 daily budget so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. Four new definitions first used words outside the defining vocabulary (*attract*, *disk*, *impression*, *cultures*). I reworded them before the vocabulary check would let the cycle finish.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *position*, *part*, *out* (adverb), and *open* (adjective). Under your ruling E5, I will tell you when the pass reaches about one hundred entries.

**For the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: closure

**What a closure cycle is.** A closure cycle builds entries for words the dictionary already uses but has not defined yet: words that appear in its own definitions (the `closure` source), related forms named in an entry's word family (`family`), and words named in cross-references (`crossref`). It runs the same full pipeline as a build cycle. Your ruling A3 keeps it to bands 1 and 2 for now.

**What was built.** Eight entries. The closure source had only three rows left: *American* (a person from the US), *net*, and *soccer*. The other five came from word families: the verbs *bat*, *lack*, *level*, *market*, and *number*, each the verb partner of an existing noun. All sixteen transcriptions were confirmed. *Net* has four senses (for catching or covering, in sports, the cloth, and the dated informal *the net* for the internet) and two idioms, *cast your net wide* and *slip through the net*. *Level* has six senses, from *level the ground* to *I'll level with you*.

*American* raised one small problem. The vocabulary check refuses words outside the defining vocabulary unless they have an entry, and country names have none. So the definition says *a person who comes from the US*. A note for translators says that people elsewhere in the Americas are Americans too, and that some of them object when the word is used only for people from the US.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** I made 57 decisions: 51 accepted, 6 rejected. Sixteen objections were blocking, more than usual, and fifteen of them were right:

- *bat* was badly organized. Hitting a ball with a bat, its commonest use, was only an example under "hit lightly". It is now sense 1. The *bat your eyelashes* sense now shows its object.
- *level*'s core sentence mentioned pointing something at someone, but no sense covered it. I added *aim a weapon* (*she leveled the gun at him*). I also replaced *level the match* with the natural *level the score*.
- *net* defined its sports sense as *a piece of net*, which defines the word with itself. I rewrote it.
- *lack* listed *need* as a synonym. *Need* means *require*, not *be without*, so I removed it. Its *lack for* sub-sense now has its own grammar (no direct object).
- *number*'s "include in a group" sense did not cover *she numbers among the best players*, where the subject is the member. It now does.

The one blocking objection I rejected asked to move a grammar restriction out of an explanation box. The house rule says such restrictions belong there. I also rejected two idioms whose keyword is another noun: *not bat an eye* belongs in *eye*, and *level the playing field* under *field*. I rejected *bat around* too, because phrasal verbs get their own entries. The *eye* entry does not yet have *not bat an eye*. That is noted for a later review.

**Spend.** US$0.24 this cycle; US$3.27 of the US$15 daily budget so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The closure source keeps running dry after each build, because each new entry adds only a few new defining words. Family rows now carry most of the closure work.

**What is next.** The selector picks the next cycle's mode.

**For the owner.** Country and continent names in definitions remain your open question. *American* and *the West* both worked around it this run.
