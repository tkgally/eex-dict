Some details worth knowing:

- *push* has three senses: a physical push, a big effort (with a military subsense, *the army's push toward the coast*), and encouragement (*she just needed a little push*). It has four idioms. Two are general: *at the push of a button* and *when push comes to shove*. Two are British and informal: *at a push* and *give someone the push*.
- *pull* has five senses: the movement, a force or attraction (*the pull of home*), informal influence (*she has a lot of pull with the manager*), a drink or a breath of smoke, and a muscle injury.
- *railroad* (labeled American) and *railway* (labeled British) mirror each other. Each has a track sense and a system sense, and each lists the other as a synonym.
- *raise* gives the American pay sense (British English says *a pay rise*) plus the senses for card games and for exercise (*leg raises*).
- *rating* covers quality ratings, television ratings, age ratings for films, and the British navy sense, a sailor who is not an officer.
- *ray* has two senses: light and *a ray of hope*. It also has the idiom *a ray of sunshine*. The fish called a ray is a different word with a different origin, so it will need an entry of its own (*ray-n-2*). That entry is noted for a later build.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 46 issues in total, and 2 of them were blocking. I applied 40 and rejected 6. Both blocking issues were right:

- In the sense of a certificate or exam pass (*she left school with no qualifications*), *qualification* is mainly British. That sense now carries a British label. The general sense, the skills that make someone fit for a job, now comes first.
- I had marked *reach*, meaning the distance an arm can stretch, as uncountable. That clashed with *a long reach*. It is now countable or uncountable.

Several smaller points improved the entries:

- *reach* gained the informal American sense in *that's a bit of a reach* (a claim that goes too far). Its subsense *within easy reach of the beach* moved under the distance sense, where it belongs.
- American wording replaced British wording in a few examples and collocations: *auto racing* for *motor racing*, *in sports* for *in sport*, and *host* for *presenter*.
- I removed the usage notes from *railroad* and *railway*. They only repeated what the region labels and synonyms already say, and part of the *railway* note made a doubtful claim.
- The card-game examples for *raise* now use the words players actually say: *call a raise* and *fold*.

These are the 6 rejections:

- A phrase named as a word keeps the double mark, under the house style.
- *Draw* the noun means a tie or a lottery, so the verb is the right related word for *pull*.
- *Railroad* keeps the system and the company in one sense, since the two do not differ in grammar or collocations.
- A *see also* link from *racing* to *track* stays, since a see-also link only needs a related entry.
- The sense *the pull of the current* stays, because the definition covers forces on things.
