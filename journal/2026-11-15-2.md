Some details worth knowing:

- *push* has three senses: a physical push, a big effort (with a military subsense, *the army's push toward the coast*), and encouragement (*she just needed a little push*). It has four idioms. Two are general: *at the push of a button* and *when push comes to shove*. Two are British and informal: *at a push* and *give someone the push*.
- *pull* has five senses: the movement, a force or attraction (*the pull of home*), informal influence (*she has a lot of pull with the manager*), a drink or a breath of smoke, and a muscle injury.
- *railroad* (labeled American) and *railway* (labeled British) mirror each other. Each has a track sense and a system sense, and each lists the other as a synonym.
- *raise* gives the American pay sense (British English says *a pay rise*) plus the senses for card games and for exercise (*leg raises*).
- *rating* covers quality ratings, television ratings, age ratings for films, and the British navy sense, a sailor who is not an officer.
- *ray* has two senses: light and *a ray of hope*. It also has the idiom *a ray of sunshine*. The fish called a ray is a different word with a different origin, so it will need an entry of its own (*ray-n-2*). That entry is noted for a later build.

**Pronunciation.** The pronunciation panel confirmed all 24 transcriptions.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 46 issues in total, and 2 of them were blocking. I applied 40 and rejected 6. Both blocking issues were right:

- In the sense of a certificate or exam pass (*she left school with no qualifications*), *qualification* is mainly British. That sense now carries a British label. The general sense, the skills that make someone fit for a job, now comes first.
- I had marked *reach*, meaning the distance an arm can stretch, as uncountable. That clashed with *a long reach*. It is now countable or uncountable.

Several smaller points improved the entries:

- *reach* gained the informal American sense in *that's a bit of a reach* (a claim that goes too far). Its subsense *within easy reach of the beach* moved under the distance sense, where it belongs.
- American wording replaced British wording in a few examples and collocations: *auto racing* for *motor racing*, *in sports* for *in sport*, and *host* for *presenter*.
- I removed the usage notes from *railroad* and *railway*. They only repeated what the region labels and synonyms already say, and part of the *railway* note made a doubtful claim.
- The card-game examples for *raise* now use the words players actually say: *call a raise* and *fold*.

These are the 6 rejections:

- A phrase named as a word keeps the double mark, under the house style.
- *Draw* the noun means a tie or a lottery, so the verb is the right related word for *pull*.
- *Railroad* keeps the system and the company in one sense, since the two do not differ in grammar or collocations.
- A *see also* link from *racing* to *track* stays, since a see-also link only needs a related entry.
- The sense *the pull of the current* stays, because the definition covers forces on things.

The tool that adds back-links (a matching reference written into the other entry) could not choose the right sense for seven links, so I placed them by hand. *Pull* is now a synonym in *influence*, *push* in *drive*, *campaign*, and *effort*, and *rating* in *grade*. *Press* and *push* now name each other as synonyms in both entries. After the panel, the vocabulary check found two definitions using words that have no entry yet, *certificate* and *sailor*. I rewrote both definitions without them.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The mode selector chose **review**. Review mode is a second reading of entries already published. No entries were waiting as drafts, and no reader reports were open. So the cycle continued the **coverage pass**, a check of the most important band-1 words for missing senses, idioms, and grammar, which the owner asked for on 2026-10-24. The pass has now finished the five-sense words and has started on words with four senses. I read four entries this cycle: *account*, *activity*, *ahead*, and *air*. Long, many-sense entries take more care, so I kept the block small.

What I added before the reviewers saw the entries:

- *account* gained a subsense for a store account (*put it on my account*) and a new sense for a business's regular customer (*the agency lost two big accounts*). It also gained four idioms: *by all accounts*, *on no account*, *on someone's account*, and *give a good account of yourself*. Its usage note only repeated a grammar code, so I removed it. I also removed a learner error that named a real bank, which the example rules forbid.
- *activity* gained a subsense for physical activity, meaning exercise.
- *ahead* gained a subsense meaning "in advance" (*call ahead to reserve a table*).
- *air* gained two senses: the informal American use for air conditioning (*turn the air up*) and an old-fashioned sense meaning a tune. It also gained four idioms: *into thin air*, *hot air*, *in the open air*, and *walk on air*.
- I replaced examples in *activity* and *air* that made science claims or claims about trends.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 53 issues in total, and 4 of them were blocking. I applied 49 and rejected 4.

Three of the blocking issues concerned one example in *ahead*: *stay ahead of other companies*. It uses *ahead of*, which is a preposition with its own entry, and the entry's own usage note says so. That example is now *The other team scored twice and went ahead*. The fourth blocking issue was in *air*: I had defined the idiom *in the air* as something felt "although nobody says it". Its own example, *excitement in the air before the game*, showed that this was wrong. It is now defined as "felt to be present, or likely to happen soon".

The reviewers' other points led to further changes:

- *account* gained *hold someone to account*. A note now says that *take account of something* means the same as *take something into account*.
- The senses were reordered in two entries. In *account*, the online sense now comes second, since it is now very common. In *activity*, the kinds-of-action sense (*criminal activity*, *economic activity*) now comes before the technical sense of a natural process. That sense now allows uncountable use.
- *ahead* gained a fifth sense for *press ahead*, *push ahead*, and *forge ahead*.
- I removed three loose synonyms: *story* for *account*, *hobby* for *activity*, and *manner* for *air*. Each was removed in both entries.
- *tune* now lists *air* as an old-fashioned synonym.

I rejected 4 issues:

- *Full speed ahead* and *a breath of fresh air* belong under *speed* and *breath*, the first nouns in each phrase, under the house rule for placing idioms.
- In *ahead*, the code "not gradable" stays. It stops the inflection tool from producing false comparative forms, and *well ahead* is a modifier, not a comparison.
- *Get ahead* keeps its own sense in *ahead*, with a clearer signpost (*to success*).
