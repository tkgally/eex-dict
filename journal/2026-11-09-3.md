**Checks before the reviewers.** The pronunciation check (three models plus an open pronouncing dictionary) agreed with every transcription but one. *Human being* was first written with full stress on both words. The panel disagreed, and I agreed with them: the usual pattern puts lighter stress on *human*. I changed it, and it then passed.

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewer models raised 44 issues. Four were blocking (they must be settled before an entry can be published):
- *hoodie*: my sound-out spelling *HUD-ee* would be read with the vowel of *cud*, not of *good*. It is now *HOOD-ee*.
- *humanity* (three reports of one problem): the forms table said "no plural", but *the humanities* is a plural. The tool that builds the forms table gives a plural only when some sense is countable. I recoded the *humanities* sense as countable and usually plural, which is accurate (*Philosophy is one of the humanities*), and the table now lists *humanities*.

I applied 40 issues in all and rejected 3. Fixes I accepted:
- American usage: *backyard* for *garden*, *cane* for *stick*, and *on our honeymoon* for the British *on honeymoon*.
- *hen*: the second sense now says the female of *some* kinds of bird, not *any*. An example claiming that hens are browner than males was replaced, since examples must not state checkable facts.
- New idioms: *mother hen*, *joined at the hip*, and a mention of *hen party* and *bachelorette party* under *hen night*.
- Duplicate cross-references and notes removed, so that each fact lives in one field.

I rejected three suggestions:
- Defining *the honeymoon is over* with *used to say that*: the house keeps that formula for function words.
- Dropping the *informal* label on *hoodie*: *hooded sweatshirt* is still the formal name.
- Treating *household chores* as a different meaning: it is the ordinary use of the word before another noun, and the grammar code already says so.

**Cross-references.** The cross-reference tool adds the matching link in the other entry when one entry points to another. In four cases it could not tell which sense to link, so I placed them by hand:
- *person* now compares with *human being*;
- *kindness* lists *humanity* as a synonym;

*Hike* had called *walk* a synonym while *walk* called *hike* a word to compare. Now both say compare: a hike is a particular kind of walk.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose another build: twelve more words in queue order. This time they included two verbs, *hammer* and *hike*, and ten nouns: *barber*, *chick*, *earphones*, *first floor*, *grocery store*, *hell*, *hiking*, *hood*, *hunger*, and *hunt*. The pronunciation check agreed with every transcription the first time.

Some entries needed particular care:
- *first floor* means different floors in American and British English, so it has one sense for each, with region labels. A note for translators warns that many countries count floors the British way.
- *Hell* is a mild swear word in many phrases. Each phrase carries an informal or very informal label, and the usage note tells learners to avoid these phrases in formal situations. *Go to hell* is also labeled offensive.
- *Chick* for a woman is labeled slang and offensive, with a usage note advising learners to avoid it.

**What the reviewers found.** The two reviewer models raised 63 issues. Four were blocking, in two pairs:
- *hammer*: an example about a heart hammering sat under the sense "hit something hard many times". A heart doesn't hit anything, so it now has its own subsense: beat very fast and hard.
- *hunt*: an example said that lions hunt together. That states a fact about animals, which examples must not do, and the sense was about people, so I replaced it.

I applied 51 issues in all. The main fixes:
- *hammer* gained a sense for damaging something badly (*the storm hammered the coast*). *hammer away at* and *hammer out* are phrasal verbs, so they were queued as entries of their own.
- *hell* gained *like hell*, *as hell*, *to hell with*, and *raise hell*.
- *hunger* and *hell* list no plural, now that their senses are coded correctly.
- *earphones* is now defined as a pair. Its usage note says that one of the two pieces is an *earphone*.

I rejected 12. Most of these asked for hand marks on word forms in examples, which the site adds itself, or for a different form of definition for whole-clause idioms such as *all hell breaks loose*. One reviewer wanted the neighborhood meaning of *hood* (*the hood*) added. It comes from a different word, so it belongs in a separate entry; I added it to the to-do list.

**Cross-references.** In seven cases the cross-reference tool could not tell which sense to link back to, so I placed the links by hand on *bang*, *beat*, *walk*, *raise*, *increase*, *grocery*, and *desire*.
