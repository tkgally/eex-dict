# 2026-10-05, run at 04:44 UTC, cycle 4: *alike* and eleven verbs, *bomb* to *bubble*

## What this cycle did

This was a closure cycle, the fourth and last of this run. A closure cycle drafts words the dictionary already needs: words used in existing definitions that have no entry yet, and members of a word's family (here, verbs whose nouns already have entries). The scheduler, the small program that picks each cycle's work, chose it.

The closure source gave only four words. Three of them were *Christian*, *Islam*, and *Judaism*. They wait on open question 7, which asks whether proper names may appear in definitions, so I released them back to the queue without drafting them, as earlier runs have done. The fourth was *alike*. It had been queued as a noun by a tool that guesses the part of speech, but the word used in the definition of *kind* (*a group of people or things that are alike*) is the adjective. I marked the noun row as a duplicate and drafted `alike-adj`. It explains that *alike* comes only after a verb (*the twins look alike*, never *the alike twins*). It points to an adverb entry for *treat everyone alike*, which the cross-reference tool queued.

The family source then gave eleven verbs whose nouns already have entries:

- *bomb*: attack with bombs; informally, fail badly; British, move very fast.
- *bone*: take the bones out of fish or meat.
- *book*: reserve a table or a seat, hire a band, have your name taken by the police or a referee.
- *boot*: kick, start a computer, force someone out, fix a clamp to a car's wheel.
- *boss*: give orders in an annoying way, usually *boss someone around*.
- *bottle*: put into bottles; British informal *bottle it*, lose your courage.
- *box*: fight as a sport, put into boxes, draw lines around something.
- *brainstorm*: think of many ideas quickly, often in a group.
- *branch*: divide into parts that go different ways.
- *bridge*: make a difference smaller, as in *bridge the gap*; go across something.
- *bubble*: make bubbles; be full of a feeling, as in *bubbling with excitement*; grow quietly under the surface.

Each verb records whether it takes an object, its typical sentence patterns, and examples that show them. Each points to its phrasal verbs (*boss around*, *bottle up*, *branch out*).

## What the checks found

The pronunciation panel and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary confirmed all 24 transcriptions. All the past forms are regular (*booked*, *bossed*, *bridged*) and came from the spelling rules.

The two reviewers raised 35 issues, 20 of them blocking. I applied 27 and rejected 8. Each decision is logged.

Reviewer A was right on several points:

- The police sense of *book* said the person had broken the law. Being booked means the police believe they have, and the definition now says so.
- The sport sense now says the referee officially warns a player.
- The wheel-clamp sense of *boot* is mainly American. It is now labelled, and the usage note gives the British word, *clamp*.
- Three notes made too-general claims about grammar. They are narrowed or removed. For example, *boot* takes an object only in the kicking sense, since a computer can simply *boot*.
- A *bridge* example quietly used a money meaning that the definition did not cover, so it was replaced.
- A usage note on *bottle* was missing the word *to*, and it is fixed.

I rejected:

- the claim that the American transcription of *boot* is the word *but*, because house style writes the long vowel without a length mark;
- a call to change the American vowel of *boss*, because the panel and CMU both agreed with the draft and the other vowel is listed as a variant;
- removing the British label from *book* in sport, because a region label means *mainly used there*;
- objections to the house's quoted-headword style;
- an objection to a *usually passive* code that is in our fixed list;
- an objection to a short pointer to *box in*.
