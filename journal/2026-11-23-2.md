# Report for 2026-11-23 (second run)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from what is most overdue) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words the dictionary still needs. Its reason was that building had the highest "debt" among the modes it could choose. Its limit was twelve new entries and US$3 of paid model calls. Before that, the run checked for unfinished work from earlier runs: there were no open pull requests (proposed changes waiting to be merged), the four old working branches on GitHub hold nothing that is not already in the main copy and are already listed for deletion in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` (this tool set cannot delete branches), the inbox was empty, and there were no open entry reports from readers.

The twelve words, all nouns in band 2 (the second of five editorial frequency groups), were taken in queue order:

- *triangle*: the shape, the relationship sense (*a love triangle*), and the musical instrument.
- *trousers*: labeled mainly British, plural only, with a usage note on American *pants* and the form *trouser* before another noun (*a trouser pocket*), two learner errors (*a trousers*, *my trousers is*), and the informal British idiom *wear the trousers*.
- *trunk*: the tree, the American car sense (British *boot*), the elephant's nose, the storage box, swimming trunks, and the main part of the body.
- *tub*: an open container, a small food container (*a tub of ice cream*), and the informal American bathtub sense.
- *tummy*: labeled informal, with a usage note that it is used especially with children.
- *twist*: a turning movement, an unexpected change (*a plot twist*, *a twist of fate*), a new way of doing something familiar (*with a modern twist*), a bend, and a curled piece (*a twist of lime*); phrases *twists and turns* and the British *drive someone round the twist*.
- *underground*: the British train system, a secret group fighting an occupying power, and the world of new and unusual art and music.
- *understanding*: knowledge, belief (*my understanding is that...*), patience with other people (*thank you for your understanding*), and an informal agreement; the formal phrase *on the understanding that*.
- *unemployment*: the general situation and the informal American sense of government payments (*on unemployment*).
- *union*: workers' organizations, joining, a union of countries, marriage (formal), and a student union.
- *update*: news, and a newer version, especially of software.
- *username*.

**Checks.** All inflections (the plural forms) were generated by rule and verified. All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were verified by the pronunciation panel, a group of three models plus an open pronouncing dictionary for American English. My first British transcription of *union* split it into three syllables; none of the three panel models agreed, so I changed it to the common two-syllable form, which all three then confirmed.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers (two language models from different companies that read every field) raised 66 issues, 5 of them blocking (an entry cannot be finished until a blocking issue is settled). All five were real and I fixed them:

- A *triangle* example, *She didn't want to be part of a triangle*, does not read as a relationship without the word *love*. It is now *caught in a love triangle*.
- A *triangle* pronunciation note compared the vowel to "the **i** of **try**", but *try* has no letter *i*. It now compares the sound with *try* directly.
- *twist* sense 4 was defined as "a sharp bend", but its example was about a twisted cable. Both reviewers' objections were settled by widening the definition to cover a place where a wire or cable has turned around itself.
- *underground* sense 3 had a plural definition ("groups of people") for a noun that is used only in the singular. It now reads "the world of artists and musicians whose work is new and different".

In all I logged 74 decisions (some issues were raised by both reviewers): 71 applied, 3 rejected. Rejections: the British label on *trousers* stays, because a region label means "mainly used in"; the bathtub sense of *tub* stays third, because it is regional; and the explanation on the *belief* sense of *understanding* stays, because how the phrase softens a claim is a point of use that a definition cannot carry. Accepted changes worth naming: a student union sense added to *union* (I then had both reviewers read *union* again, and fixed a vague usage note they found); *body* replaced by *torso* as a synonym of *trunk*; reordering *trunk* and *triangle* so the more common senses come first; several generic or repeated translator notes removed.

**Links.** The cross-reference tool adds the matching link on the other entry when it can tell which sense to use; twelve times it could not, and I placed those links by hand (for example, *understanding* now appears as a synonym on *knowledge* and *agreement*). I removed *tube* as a synonym of the British *underground*, because our *tube* entry does not yet have the train sense; that is noted in `NEXT.md` for its next review.

**Spend.** US$0.46 for this cycle (reviewers and pronunciation panel), US$1.21 for the day so far against the day's cap of US$15.

**What did not work.** The elephant sense of *trunk* could not use the word *elephant* in its definition, because *elephant* is outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions are written in) and has no entry yet. The definition describes the animal instead; *elephant* is queued.

**What is next.** Another cycle, chosen by the selector.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle.

## Cycle 2: closure

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *closure*: building entries for words the dictionary already uses or points to but has not yet written. Closure has three sources, tried in order. The first, words used in definitions but missing (`closure`), is empty. The second, `family`, holds related words named in other entries' word-family lists, and it supplied all twelve: *voter*, *wait*, *waiter*, *waitress*, *walking*, *washing*, *weekday*, *whisper*, *white*, *win*, *wonder*, and *yellow*, all nouns in band 2.

Highlights:

- *wait*: one sense, plus the phrase *lie in wait* and a learner error (*a long waiting*).
- *waiter* and *waitress*: usage notes say that many people now use *server* for anyone doing the job.
- *walking*: walking for pleasure, walking in general, and *within walking distance*.
- *washing*: the mainly British sense of clothes to be washed (*hang out the washing*), and the act of washing.
- *whisper*: a quiet voice, a secret story (*whispers that...*), a soft sound (literary), and a small trace (*a whisper of a smile*).
- *white*: the color, egg white, the white of the eye, a white person, white wine, and white clothes.
- *wonder*: the feeling, an amazing thing, and four phrases: *no wonder*, *it's a wonder*, *work wonders*, and the joking *wonders never cease*.

**Checks.** All inflections and all 24 pronunciations were verified on the first try.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** There were 61 issues, 18 of them blocking. The blocking count is high, but most came from a few real faults that both reviewers saw at once. I accepted every blocking issue:

- **Wrong part of speech in examples.** In *The traffic light turned yellow*, *yellow* is an adjective, not a noun. It now reads *changed to yellow*, the form the *red* entry already uses. Likewise, *The baby has started walking* shows the verb, not the noun, and was replaced.
- **A sense that does not exist.** I had given *win* a mainly British sense meaning "money won". One reviewer pointed out that the word for this is *winnings*, and that *he spent his win* is not natural English. I agree, and the sense is gone.
- **A circular definition.** *White* (wine) was defined as "white wine". It now describes the color of the wine.
- **A missing sense.** Both reviewers said *white* lacked its use for a person. I added it with a usage note: the plural *whites* is neutral in reports and counts, but the singular *a white* can sound cold or rude, and *a white person* is usual.
- **A grammar code.** The act sense of *washing* was coded uncountable, but its example says *a good washing*. It is now countable or uncountable, usually singular.
- **A pronunciation note that did not help.** My note on *wonder* respelled *wander* as *WON-der*, which reads just like *wonder*. It now says that *wander* has the vowel of *hot* in American English.

Because *white* and *whisper* gained senses during adjudication, both reviewers read those two again. They found nine smaller points, which I fixed. For example, the warning about *whites* had been too broad, and one *whisper* example fitted the "secret story" sense rather than the new "small trace" sense.

In all I logged 72 decisions: 66 applied, 6 rejected. The rejections, with their reasons:

- Defining *voter* with the verb *vote* is not circular: the base word defines the person who does it.
- *Humorous* is a register label in this dictionary's closed list, so it stays there.
- The leisure sense of *walking* and the clothes sense of *washing* stay first, because they are the main noun uses.
- *Monday* stays as a related entry for *weekday*.
- No label was added to the person sense of *white*: the narrowed usage note says what a label would only hint at.

**Links.** Five matching links were added automatically. I placed two by hand: *voter* appears under compare on *citizen*, and the wine sense of *red* now points to *white*.

**Spend.** US$0.43 for this cycle. The day so far is US$1.65, against a cap of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The lesson is about my drafting. Twice I wrote a noun entry example that actually showed the adjective or the verb, and once I wrote a sense from a guess about British usage. The reviewers caught all three.

**What is next.** Another cycle, if the run clock allows.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
