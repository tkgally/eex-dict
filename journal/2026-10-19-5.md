# 2026-10-19, run at 12:44 UTC, cycle 1: closure

## What this run did

This was a **closure** cycle. Closure means writing entries for words that the dictionary already uses inside its own definitions, examples, or word-family lists but does not yet define, so that a reader who clicks such a word finds an entry. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose closure because it was the job most overdue. It allowed up to twelve new entries and US$3.00 of paid reviewer calls.

The first source, words found inside definitions, had only three rows. Two were ordinary headwords: **greet** (verb) and **romantic** (adjective). The third was *impressed* listed as a verb, which is only the past form of *impress*. As earlier runs have done with inflected rows, I marked that row as a duplicate and instead queued and wrote the two real headwords behind it: **impress** (verb) and **impressed** (adjective, as in *I was impressed by her speech*). The second source, words from the word families of existing entries, gave six verbs: **embody**, **empower**, **endanger**, **energize**, **enlarge**, and **enrich**. So the cycle produced ten new entries, all now `reviewed` (published).

Some points about the content:

- *greet* has three senses: saying hello to someone, reacting to news (*the plan was greeted with silence*), and being what you see or smell when you arrive (*a terrible smell greeted us*). It has a short note on choosing between *meet*, *greet*, and *welcome*.
- *romantic* has four senses: about love (*a romantic relationship*), full of love (*a romantic dinner*), not realistic (*a romantic idea of country life*), and the art style written with a capital R (*the Romantic poets*).
- *impressed* carries the common learner error *I was very impressive by the show*, and a note that *impressed* is the person's reaction while *impressive* is the thing that causes it.
- *enlarge* has a note on choosing between *enlarge* (size), *expand* (spreading, businesses), and *increase* (numbers and amounts).

## Checks

All twenty pronunciations (American and British for each entry) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, a group of language models whose votes are compared with each other and with an open pronouncing dictionary consulted at run time. All verb and adjective forms were generated by the inflection script and confirmed.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from different companies read every field of every entry. They raised 28 issues; I applied 26 and rejected 2. Every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

- The most common objection (six issues on three entries) was that examples like *I always feel energized after a swim* and *made her feel empowered* show the word used as an adjective, not as a verb. The reviewers were right under the house rule that each entry holds only its own part of speech, so I replaced them with real verb uses (*Nothing energizes me like a cold swim in the morning*; *Good managers empower their staff to make decisions*).
- One reviewer said the art-style use of *romantic* deserved its own sense rather than a line in the usage note. I agreed and added it as sense 4.
- On *embody*, a reviewer pointed out that the continuous form is possible (an actor *is embodying* a role), so I removed the claim that it is never continuous, and the learner error that depended on it.
- Rejected: a complaint that the *enlarge* note names *Asia* (the style guide allows continent names), and a request for a bare "verb" pattern on *enlarge*, which the closed list of patterns does not have; the sense's transitivity value already says it can be used without an object.

One reviewer's reply on *impressed* came back as broken data the first time; a single re-run gave a clean, complete reply.

Two definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (the list of plain words definitions are written from): *react* in *greet* and *photo* in *enlarge*. Both were reworded.

The cross-reference script adds back-links automatically, always to the first sense of the target. Four landed on the wrong sense and I moved them by hand: *embody* on *represent* and *endanger* on *threaten* (both to the "be a sign" or "be a danger" sense), *romantic* on *practical* (to "likely to work"), and *empower* on *authorize* (to "give the right").
