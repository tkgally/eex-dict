## What this cycle did

An **originality check** tests whether the dictionary's wording is its own. The project's rules say no definition may be copied from a published dictionary. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) forces this check every tenth run, and this was run 330.

The check works like this:

1. A tool picks ten fields at random from across the dictionary: sense definitions, idiom definitions, and usage notes (the short boxes of advice inside an entry).
2. Each one is searched on the web as an exact phrase, to see whether a published dictionary uses the same or nearly the same words.
3. One outside reviewer model (reviewer-a, asked with its extended reasoning switched off) is asked whether each one reads as copied. If it says "copied", it must quote the published wording.
4. The search results decide the verdict, not the reviewer. In past checks the reviewer has often claimed copying with "quotes" that were really our own wording.

Before the check, the start-of-run sweep found no open pull requests and one leftover branch, `claude/wonderful-bardeen-7mgq5n`, from an earlier run. The branch-checking tool found nothing in it that is not already on `main`, so it only needs deleting. This session's tools cannot delete branches, so a one-line note was added to `reviews/needs_curator.txt` (the list of things only the owner can settle).

## Results

Five of the ten are **original**: no match was found for the *head* idiom *bite someone's head off* ("answer someone angrily for no good reason"), the *humor* spelling note, the *between*/*among* usage note, the permission sense of *may*, or the sense of *hope* meaning a person or thing that may bring help or success.

Three are **generic overlap**: short, plain definitions that any dictionary would write in much the same words. These were *country* ("an area of land that has its own government and borders"), the idiom *come into effect* ("start to apply, as a law or a rule does"), and *hit rock bottom* ("reach the lowest possible level"). Search found similar wording in several places, but nothing distinctive enough to need a change.

Two needed a **rewrite**, because search found wording that was too close:

- *a clean break* (an idiom in the entry *break*). A dictionary page quoted "a sudden complete end to something such as a relationship"; ours was "a complete end to a relationship or situation, with nothing left to connect you". It now reads *a way of ending something, such as a marriage or a period of your life, so that nothing is left to link you to it*.
- *frame*, sense 3 (the body). Ours was "the size and shape of a person's body, especially its bones". A learner's dictionary uses almost the same opening words to define *physique*. It now reads *a person's body seen as a whole, especially how big or small its bones make it*. This fits all the examples (*a large frame*, *her small frame*, *his thin frame*).

The reviewer said "copied" for three items: *hope*, *may*, and *frame*. Each time it named one dictionary and gave a "quote", and each time it added that its quote did not match our wording exactly. The question it was asked tells it not to answer "copied" unless it can quote the matching wording, so this is a new kind of mistake. Search did not confirm any of its quotes. The *frame* rewrite came from search, which found the match under a different headword. The pattern is now recorded in `wiki/notes/reviewer-noise.md`.

## The re-review

A rewrite counts as a review of that entry, so *break* and *frame* went through the whole checking pipeline again: format validation, inflections, pronunciations (all verified), and both reviewers. Reviewer-b found nothing. Reviewer-a raised six issues, four of them marked blocking, all on parts of *break* that this cycle did not change. I rejected all six and logged a reason for each:

- It said *a clean break* belongs only with the idiom, not as a collocation of the "broken bone" sense. But *a clean break* is the normal way to describe a bone broken in one clean line, so it stays in both places.
- It said the "broken bone" definition wrongly calls the break an injury. A broken bone is an injury, and the definition also covers the broken place itself.
- It said *We have a pause of ten minutes between classes* is not a real learner error because the sentence is grammatical. It is grammatical, but it is the wrong word: a scheduled stop between lessons is a *break*. Learner-error boxes are allowed to cover word choice.
- On *frame*, it asked for *a steel frame* to be listed as noun + noun, which it already is, and for the game sense to mention darts, which counts its score in legs and sets, not frames.
