# 2026-10-16, cycle 2 of the 00:44 UTC run: twelve adverbs, *largely* to *most*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries drafted from the queue (the list of words still to be written), in queue order among the very common words (band 1). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose it because build was the job furthest behind its share of the work. The adverbs continued from where the last build stopped (*just*). The twelve new entries:

*largely*, *last*, *late*, *later*, *least*, *left*, *less*, *mainly*, *maybe*, *merely*, *more*, and *most*, all as adverbs.

Several of these are grammar words a learner needs badly. *More* and *most* now have entries that explain when English uses **more**/**most** and when it adds **-er**/**-est**, with the typical errors (*more better*, *the most tallest*). *Less* and *least* do the same in the other direction. *Later* has a learner error that is very common: saying *I'll call you two hours later* where English needs *in two hours*. *Maybe* separates **maybe** (one word, an adverb) from **may be** (two words, a verb). *Late* warns that **lately** means *recently*, not *in a late way*.

Every entry went through the full pipeline (the fixed sequence of checks each entry passes before it is published): format check, the inflection script, the pronunciation check, both outside reviewers, my decision on every issue they raised, then the vocabulary and cross-reference checks.

- **Pronunciation**: all 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the panel of models and an open pronouncing dictionary on the first try.
- **Inflections**: only *late* has its own forms (*later*, *latest*). The others are marked as having no *-er* or *-est* forms, because they are already comparatives, superlatives, or words that do not compare.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The first reviewer (reviewer-a) raised 24 issues, 20 of them marked blocking; the second (reviewer-b) raised 5, all on *late*. I accepted 15 and rejected 14. No entry needed an outside ruling.

Accepted, among others:

- *More*, sense 2, was defined with the words *more often*, which uses the headword in its own definition. It now says *to a greater degree, or a greater number of times*.
- *More*, sense 3 (*She doesn't live here any more*), also offered **no more** as the same use. That was misleading, since **no more** in this meaning is rare. It now covers only **not ... any more**.
- The phrase *more than happy to do something* is now the more general *more than* before an adjective (*more than happy*, *more than enough*).
- *Most* in informal American English, as in *most everyone*, now covers **any** and the words formed from **all**, **every**, and **any**, and has its collocations.
- The first sense of *more*, *most*, *less*, and *least* had the label *before adjectives*, but some of the examples were adverbs (*more slowly*). The label now says *adjectives and adverbs*.
- *Late* lost the collocation *late for something*, which is mostly an adjective use (*be late for work*).

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- Three objections said *at last* belongs in the adverb entry *last*, and one said *at least* belongs in *least*. The house rule keeps a fixed phrase under one keyword entry, and these phrases already live in *last* (adjective) and *least* (determiner).
- reviewer-b said *I'm running late* uses *late* as an adjective. It doesn't: *late* tells how the action goes, as in *run fast*.
- reviewer-a claimed *more clearer* is standard English. It is not.
- An objection to marking *later* as having no further comparative: the marking only stops the script from producing a form like *laterer*.

One cross-reference had to be moved by hand. The script that adds the matching link in the other entry put *merely* beside *just* in its *a moment ago* sense. It belongs beside the *only* sense.
