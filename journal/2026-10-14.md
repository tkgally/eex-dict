## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries written from the queue of words the dictionary still needs. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose it because building was furthest behind its share of the work. The queue is working through the most common adjectives (band 1, the words every learner meets first), and this cycle took the last twelve in that run of the alphabet: *white*, *whole*, *wide*, *wild*, *wise*, *wonderful*, *worried*, *worth*, *written*, *wrong*, *yellow*, and *young*.

Some points worth knowing:

- *wild* has eight senses, from animals and plants through behavior (*a wild party*), strong feeling (*the crowd went wild*), *not wild about* (not keen on), and careless guesses (*a wild guess*), to stormy weather (*a wild sea*).
- *worth* behaves almost like a preposition: it is followed directly by an amount, a noun, or an *-ing* form (*worth ten dollars*, *worth seeing*). The entry explains that the *-ing* form has a passive meaning (*The film is worth seeing*), and the phrase *for what it's worth* is included.
- *wrong* has four senses lined up with the existing entry for *right* (not correct, not suitable, not fair, not working), so the opposites point at each other sense by sense.
- Before running the link tool, I listed every existing entry that already pointed at one of the twelve words and added the return links on the right senses myself (for example, *nervous* lists *worried* as a synonym, so *worried* now lists *nervous* as one too).

## Pronunciation and word forms

All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation check, which compares several models with an open pronouncing dictionary. The comparison forms (*whiter*, *wider*, *younger*, and so on) came from the project's spelling rules; *wrong* takes *more wrong* from the project's table of exceptions; *whole*, *worth*, and *written* are marked as having no comparative.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from other companies read every field of every entry. They raised 53 issues, 41 of them marked blocking (meaning the entry must not be published until each is settled). I accepted 37 and rejected 16. None needed the owner. *wrong* and *yellow* came back with no issues from either reviewer.

Accepted, among others:

- *white*, the sense for people, first said *belonging to a race of people who have pale skin*. A reviewer objected that the group is defined by society, not only by skin color. It now reads *belonging to a group of people who have light skin*, and the explanation adds that which people are called *white* depends partly on the society and the time.
- *whole* sense 2 (*in one piece*) used *The snake swallowed the egg whole*. Both reviewers called this an adverb use. Grammarians differ on it, but the point was not worth a dispute: the examples are now plainly adjectival (*a whole chicken*, *Is the vase still whole?*).
- *wonderful* called *very wonderful* a learner error. A reviewer pointed out that it is used, just less often. The error was removed, and the usage note now says *really* or *absolutely* is more usual.
- *young* senses 2 and 3 were redefined: the second no longer requires that the thing is still growing, and the third now says that the person seems to have lived less time than they really have.

Rejected, among others:

- A reviewer said *wild* cannot mean stormy. It can (*a wild sea*, *wild weather*); only the example *a wild night*, which suggests a party, was changed.
- A reviewer said *narrow* is not the opposite of *wide* in *a wide range*. It is (*a narrow range*), and the *narrow* entry already says so.
- The second reviewer asked for a grammar code *usually before noun*, which does not exist in the project's fixed list of codes, and called *comparative with more* invalid, which is in the list.
