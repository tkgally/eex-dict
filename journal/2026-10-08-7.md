## What went into the entries

Most entries have two to four senses, word combinations for each sense, and a learner-error box where learners commonly go wrong. Some points worth noting:

- **Idioms.** By the keyword rule (an idiom goes under its first noun), *a snake in the grass*, *as white as snow*, *knock someone's socks off*, *pull your socks up* (British), *for a song* (very cheaply), and *make a song and dance about something* (British) were entered as phrases.
- **Learner errors.** *Society* meaning people in general takes no *the* (*society is changing*); *software* has no plural (*two pieces of software*); you sit *on* a sofa but *in* an armchair; *a solution to* a problem, not *of*; *music* never takes *a*, so one piece with words is *a song*; and *snake* and *snack* are often confused in speech.
- **Senses.** *Society* has four (people in general, one particular society, a club, and high society); *solution* has three (a way out of a problem, the answer to a question in a game, and a liquid mixture); *song* has three (a piece with words, singing in general, and a bird's song); *soil* has a formal sense for a country's land (*on foreign soil*).

All twenty-four pronunciations were verified by the panel, and the American ones also agreed with the open pronouncing dictionary. The inflection tool gave *software* no plural, as intended.

My first drafts used three words outside the defining vocabulary (the 2,394 plain words that definitions must use): *opera* and *everyday* in the television sense of *soap*, and *puzzle* in *solution*. The vocabulary check caught them and I reworded the definitions.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 28 issues, 21 from reviewer-a and 7 from reviewer-b. I agreed with 21 and fixed the entries in my own words, for example:

- *snow*: snow falls when it is cold *enough*, not only when it is *very* cold. A translator's note had described the verb (*it is snowing*); it now speaks only of the noun.
- *soap*: *a soap opera* was listed as a word combination with *soap*, but it is the full name of the thing, so it moved into the explanation (*short for soap opera*). The learner error's correction now reads *some soap*, which fits a general request.
- *society*: high society was defined as rich and important; it now says fashionable and well known, and often rich.
- *soldier*: the definition said a soldier is usually not an officer, but officers are soldiers too; it now says simply *a person who is a member of an army*.
- *solution*: *problem* was listed as its opposite; a problem is what a solution answers, not its opposite, so it was removed.
- *software*: *hardware* was listed as a family word (a word built from the same root); it is a contrasting word and stays only as a comparison.
- *soil*, *sofa*: a comparison note that called *earth* more everyday than *soil*, and a usage note that called *couch* the more common American word, were both softened.

I rejected 7. Reviewer-a said the British labels on *pull your socks up* and *make a song and dance about something* are misleading; the house rule is that a region label means *mainly used there*, which is true of both. It wanted the chemistry sense of *solution* to include gas and solid solutions, which are technical; learners meet liquid ones. Reviewer-b objected to the term-of-address sense of *son* (*What's your name, son?*) being defined with *used by an older man...*; this is the house pattern for words used to address someone. One reviewer-b objection withdrew itself in its own text.

The cross-reference tool added three back-links (from *rain*, *community*, and *club*), each on the right sense. It also reported that *dirt* and *ground* list *soil* as a synonym while *soil* listed them only for comparison; I made them synonyms on both sides.
