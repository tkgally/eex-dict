# Run of 2026-11-21

## Cycle 1: lint

**What the cycle was.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose *lint*, because ten cycles had passed since the last one. A lint cycle builds no new entries. It tidies the dictionary as a whole: it checks word caps and links, completes cross-references, refreshes the queue of words still to write, and reads the wiki (the project's own rule book and notes) for contradictions. This was the hundred-and-seventeenth such pass. Before it, because a week had passed, the run wrote the weekly digest for you: `journal/digest-2026-11-21.md`.

**Housekeeping first.** No pull requests were left open by earlier runs. Three old working branches still exist on GitHub; all three are already fully merged into `main` and were already listed in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` (the list of things for you), since the tools available to a run cannot delete branches. No entry reports (problem reports filed from the site's "Report a problem" link) were open, and `inbox/` was empty.

**Cross-references.** When entry A names entry B as a synonym, an antonym, a word to compare, or a member of its word family, B should name A back. A script adds these mirror links when it can tell which sense of B to attach them to. This time it added them in five entries: *stripe* now lists *band* as a synonym, *subway* lists *way* in its word family, *suitcase* lists *bag* and *case*, *sunlight* lists *sun*, and *sweatshirt* lists *hoodie*. In four more pairs the script could not tell which sense was meant, so I placed them by hand after reading both entries: *battle* goes on the first sense of *struggle* (a long, hard effort), *instruction* on the first sense of *teaching* (the work of helping people learn), *reach* on the fifth sense of *stretch* (something hard to believe, as in *that's a bit of a stretch*), and *victim* as an antonym on the first sense of *survivor*. Nine entries changed in all; no definition, example, or other written content was touched.

**The queue.** The vocabulary check found no new words to queue. Seven claim files (records of which words a past cycle had reserved) were spent and removed. Twenty-seven new queue rows had no frequency band yet. Bands run from 1 (most frequent) to 5, and they come only from a vote of three outside models, so the vote was run, at a cost of half a US cent. Two words had votes more than one band apart, which a session must settle by judgment: *dress down* (votes 4, 4, 2) and the verb *effect* (votes 3, 4, 2, as in *effect change*). I put both in band 4, because both are uncommon compared with their related words. All 27 rows are now ready to build.

**Reports that a review cycle will use.** About 1,052 cross-reference pairs still need a person to check which sense they belong to; 317 definitions use their own headword, which is usually harmless (*water a plant*: *pour water on plants*); and the same six notes mention words the dictionary does not have yet. None of these numbers moved much from the last pass.

**The reviewers.** Each entry is checked by two reviewer models, and every issue they raise is decided as applied (they were right) or rejected (they were wrong). Over all decisions so far, reviewer A (a Claude model) has been right 85 percent of the time and reviewer B (a Google model) 84 percent. The rule is that any kind of issue where a reviewer is right less than 30 percent of the time, over at least twenty decisions, is switched off for that reviewer. No new kind fell under the line; the three already off stay off. Reviewer B's spelling-and-format flags (39 percent) stay on by your earlier ruling.

**The wiki.** Every page is listed in the index and every index line points to a real page; every cycle since the last lint has a log entry; no open question is stale; the curator list has no duplicates.

**Spend.** US$0.005 (the band vote). The day's cap is US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** The selector should go back to building and reviewing: the main queue resumes after *sympathy*, and the coverage pass (re-reading the most important everyday words for missing senses and idioms) resumes at *far*, *feed*, and *film*. The next lint is due after ten more cycles.

**What needs you.** Nothing new beyond the digest: the pronunciation disputes and the four sets of ten sample entries still wait for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle was.** The selector chose *review*: reading existing entries again with both reviewer models, rather than writing new ones. No entries were waiting as drafts and no problem reports were open, so the cycle continued the *coverage pass* of your ruling B1. In that pass the most important everyday words (band 1, the most frequent of five bands) are re-read to find senses and idioms that are missing. This cycle took the next four words with four senses each in alphabetical order: *far* as an adjective, *far* as an adverb, the verb *feed*, and the noun *film*.

**What I added before the reviewers saw the entries.**
- *far* (adverb): four fixed phrases, *far and away* (*far and away the best*), *far and wide*, *far from it*, and *so far so good*. The "progress" sense now also covers degree (*How far can we really trust them?*).
- *feed*: a use for plants (*feed the roses once a month*), a use for information (*someone was feeding the newspaper secret information*), and a new sense for things that flow into something larger (*streams feed into the lake*; *the results will feed into the report*).
- *film*: the phrase *on film* (*caught on film*) and the clear plastic used to wrap food.
- I removed two notes that repeated a fact already given in another field. The house rule is "one fact, one field".

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 36 issues, 5 of them *blocking* (an issue that must be settled before an entry can count as checked). I applied 27 and rejected 9, each with a logged reason.

The useful catches:
- The translator's note on *feed* had the grammar backwards. In *feed bread to the ducks* the eater follows *to*, not the food. I rewrote the note.
- The "supply" sense of *feed* spoke only of machines, yet one of its examples was *feed the wire through the hole in the wall*. Both reviewers caught this, and the definition now covers moving something through a space. A fire example was added at the same time.
- Both reviewers wanted *as far as*, which is very common. It is now a phrase under *far* with *as far as I can tell*, *as far as I'm concerned*, and *as far as it goes*. *As far as I know* already lives under *know*.
- Three "learner errors" were not errors. *My house is far* and *We walked far yesterday* are acceptable English, only less natural than *a long way*, so the entries now say that in the explanation instead. *Feeded* was removed because the style guide forbids invented regular forms.
- Two *far* senses wrongly said the word has no comparative, though *get further with the work* is normal. Wrapping film became its own sense, and *develop a film* became *develop film*.

The rejections mostly follow the standing *keyword rule*: an idiom goes under its first noun, and if it has no noun, under its first verb. That puts *go far* and *go too far* under *go*. *Go too far* is already there; *go far* is now on the list for *go*'s next review. I also rejected the sports use *feed the ball* as too narrow for now. *Distant* stays a synonym of *far*, because the entry for *distant* already explains how the two differ. The search for these idioms also showed that *bite the hand that feeds you* is missing from *hand*; it is listed for that entry.

**Spend.** US$0.17 for the reviewers and the pronunciation check. The day's total is about US$0.18 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. A note in `NEXT.md` (the file each run reads to learn what comes next) ran 7 words over its 1,000-word cap and was trimmed.

**What is next.** The coverage pass goes on with *flight*, *foreign*, *forget*, and *friend*; 397 words are done.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle was.** The selector chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the list of words still to be written. It allowed up to twelve. I claimed six and wrote five, keeping the batch small so that each entry could be complete. The entries are the nouns *first name*, *last name*, and *burger*, and the verbs *stock* and *substitute*. Each one went through the full pipeline: the format check, the inflection tool (which writes forms such as *stocked* from rules), the pronunciation check (a panel of models plus an open pronouncing dictionary, all ten transcriptions verified), both reviewers, my decisions on what they found, and the cross-reference tool.

**One row was not a real word.** The sixth row was *cookies*, a plural. A tool had queued it after finding *cookies* in the definition of *sweet* and taking the plural for a headword. This is a known weakness of that tool, recorded in `wiki/notes/lint-vocab-pos-guess-gap.md`. I marked the row as a duplicate and queued *cookie* instead. *Cookie* has no frequency band yet; the next lint's band vote will give it one.

**What the entries say.**
- *first name* defines the name given at birth. It has the phrase *on first-name terms* and notes the American *on a first-name basis*. A usage note says that using a first name is friendly, while a formal setting uses a title and family name (*Dr. Chen*).
- *first name* and *last name* each have a culture note for translators. In some languages the family name comes first, so on a form, *last name* means the family name, not whichever name comes last in the reader's own order.
- *burger* has two senses: the usual beef one, and burgers made from other food (*a veggie burger*, *a bean burger*).
- *stock* has two senses: having goods for sale (*Do you stock children's shoes?*) and filling a place with a supply (*stock the fridge*).
- *substitute* has three senses: using one thing instead of another, taking someone's or something's place (*Nothing can substitute for real experience*), and taking a player off in a game.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 27 issues; only 1 was *blocking* (an issue that must be settled before an entry can count as checked). I applied 26 and rejected 1.
- The blocking issue was a fair catch: an example used the name *Annie*, which is not on the dictionary's short list of allowed names. The example was replaced.
- The most useful catch was on *substitute*. In *substitute honey for sugar* the object is the new thing, but in *substitute sugar with honey* it is the old thing. The draft gave only the first order, and its definition did not fit the second. That point now sits once, in the explanation of the first sense, and two notes that had repeated it were removed.
- *Replace* had been listed both as a synonym and as a word to compare. It is now only a word to compare, with a note that its objects go the other way.
- Other fixes: *store* instead of the British *shop*, *veggie burger* instead of *vegetable burger*, and forms like *well stocked* moved to the right kind of collocation.
- The one rejection: a reviewer said the culture note on name order repeated the definition. It does not. The definition gives the English order, and the note warns about the readers for whom that order is reversed.

**Something I caught myself.** The vocabulary check flagged *English* in two definitions (*which in English comes before your family name*). *English* is outside the plain defining vocabulary, so the definitions now say *usually*, as the existing entry for *surname* does. The cross-reference tool could not decide where four mirror links belonged, so I placed them by hand on the right senses of *carry* (have goods for sale), *fill*, *supply*, and *replace*. I also dropped *sell* as a synonym of *stock*, because selling is not the same as having goods in the store.

**Spend.** US$0.13; about US$0.31 for the day so far.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** Building resumes from the queue after *substitute*.

**What needs you.** Nothing new.
