# 2026-10-13, 04:44 UTC run, cycle 1: eleven adjectives, *strong* to *thin*

## What this cycle did

This cycle was a **build**: it wrote new dictionary entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The selector, the script that picks each cycle's job, chose build because it was the job most overdue. Twelve words were claimed (reserved so that no other run takes them); eleven were written and finished:

*strong*, *stupid*, *sudden*, *suitable*, *sure*, *surprised*, *sweet*, *tall*, *terrible*, *thick*, *thin*.

All eleven are band 1 adjectives (band 1 is the most common words, the ones every learner meets first). The twelfth word claimed, *third* as an adjective, was not written. Its uses (*the third day*) are ordinal numbers, and the dictionary already treats *first* and *second* the same way: the adjective row waits until the number entry exists. So *third* (adjective) is now marked deferred, and *third* (number) was added to the queue at band 1.

The entries are full-sized. *Strong* has ten senses, from the body (*strong arms*) to the use after a number (*a choir forty strong*). *Sure* has six senses and two phrases (*for sure*, *sure enough*). *Thick* has seven senses and two phrases (*through thick and thin*, *thick and fast*), and *thin* has six. Every entry has its collocations (the words it usually goes with), cross-references, and at least one learner error, a mistake learners often make, such as *strong rain* for *heavy rain* or *a thin road* for *a narrow road*.

## Checks

- **Pronunciation.** All 22 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, three outside models plus a check against an open pronouncing dictionary, on the first try.
- **Inflections** (the forms *stronger*, *strongest* and so on) came from the spelling rules. *Stupid* needed one line in the exceptions table, the hand-kept list of forms the rules cannot decide: *stupider* and *stupidest*, with a note that *more stupid* is also common.
- **Review panel.** Two reviewer models from other companies read every field. They raised 58 issues; 40 were blocking, meaning the entry could not be published until each was decided. I applied 30 of the blocking issues and rejected 10, each with a logged reason. None needed the owner. *Strong* came back with no issues.

## What the reviewers found, and what I decided

The reviewers were right about these, and the entries were changed:
- *sure*: an example (*It's sure to rain if we leave the windows open*) made no sense and was replaced. The note on *surely* said too much and was softened. The sense *a sure sign*, *a sure way* was redefined as "certain to show something or to have a particular result".
- *thick* and *thin*: the liquid senses claimed something about water content that is not always true. Both now compare with a familiar liquid instead (*like cream or honey*, *like water*). The *thick accent* sense was split, and *a voice thick with sleep* now has its own subsense.
- *terrible*: the sense *feel terrible* was widened beyond regret for your own actions, and one learner error was removed because *feel terrible for him* is good English.
- *tall*: two learner-error notes said "always" and "never" where that was too strong. *A tall tale* now comes first as the American form.
- *sweet*: the claim that American English prefers *cute* was removed. The reply *Sweet!* now points to a separate interjection entry, which was queued.

Rejected, with reasons in the decision log:
- Moving *thick and fast* to an adverb entry. House rule: an idiom stays under its keyword whatever its part of speech.
- Calling *comparative with more* a non-code. It is one of the closed grammar codes.
- Several objections to cross-references (*thin* and *fat*, *thick* and *heavy*, *tall* and *low*) that the target entries already contain in the matching sense.

One reviewer's reply on *thin* came back unreadable. It was re-run once and then came back complete.

After the review, the cross-reference tool added mirror links in eight existing entries. Three landed on the wrong sense and were moved by hand: *terrible* on `good-adj` (to sense 2, *skilled*), *sweet* on `nice-adj` (to sense 2, *kind*), and *thick* on `strong-adj` (to sense 4, *taste, smell, light*).
