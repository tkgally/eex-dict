# Run report, 2026-11-21 (second run of the day)

## Cycle 1: originality

**What this cycle was.** Every twentieth scheduled run is an *originality check*: the selector (the script that picks each cycle's job) forces it so that we keep testing whether any of our wording has drifted towards a published dictionary's. This was run 860, so it was due. The check takes ten fields at random from the 4,637 finished entries, searches the web for each one's exact wording, asks one of the two outside reviewer models whether the text reads as copied, and then I give my own verdict on each. Pre-flight was clean: no pull requests were left open by earlier runs, no reader has filed an entry report (a GitHub issue that points out a mistake in an entry), and the three leftover branches on GitHub were already listed for you to delete.

**The sample.** The ten fields were: the phrase *to a man* under **man**, the usage note on **patient** (noun against adjective), the musical-ear sense of **ear**, the stretchy-cloth sense of **stretch**, the suffix **-ist**, the usage note on **more** (determiner), the first sense of **away**, the phrase *on someone's side* under **side**, the phrase *read someone like a book* under **book**, and the phrase *go off the deep end* under **end**.

**What the searches and the reviewer found.** None of the ten exact-phrase searches turned up a published dictionary with the same wording. The nearest results were dictionary entries for related words (*go berserk*, *support*) and grammar pages that state the same facts in other words. Reviewer B (the non-Anthropic reviewer model) was asked once and called all ten original.

**My verdicts.** Six original; three *generic overlap*, meaning short, plain definitions that any dictionary would phrase about the same way (**-ist**, **away**, *on someone's side*); and one rewrite. The rewrite is *read someone like a book*. Its definition, *understand easily what someone is thinking or feeling*, was not found by the search, but reading it myself I thought it sat too close to a wording a well-known learner's dictionary uses. The rule is that a distinctive match gets rewritten, and when in doubt I lean towards rewriting. The new definition is *know what someone is thinking or feeling just from their face or the way they act*, and the phrase gained a second example (*She tried to look calm, but her mother could read her like a book*).

**The pipeline on the rewritten entry.** A rewritten entry goes through the full checking pipeline: format checks, inflections, pronunciation, and both reviewer models reading every field. I put **book** through the reviewers twice, once after the rewrite and again after my fixes. They raised eight minor issues and nothing blocking. I accepted six:

- *Open your books at page 20* became *to page 20*, since the dictionary's main variety is American English.
- An example using *exercise book*, which is mainly British, was replaced with a neutral one. Both reviewers flagged this one.
- The common idiom *off the books* (paid or done without official records, often to avoid tax) was missing, so I added it, labelled informal.
- The usage note and a learner-error note both said that *by* names a book's author. I cut the usage note back so that the fact appears in one place only, as the style guide requires.

I rejected two. *The oldest trick in the book* and *take a leaf out of someone's book* belong under **trick** and **leaf**, because by house rule an idiom goes under its first noun. And mentioning the opposite phrase (*in someone's bad books*) in an explanation is allowed and useful.
