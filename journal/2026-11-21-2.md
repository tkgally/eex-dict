# Run report, 2026-11-21 (second run of the day)

## Cycle 1: originality

**What this cycle was.** Every twentieth scheduled run is an *originality check*: the selector (the script that picks each cycle's job) forces it so that we keep testing whether any of our wording has drifted towards a published dictionary's. This was run 860, so it was due. The check takes ten fields at random from the 4,637 finished entries, searches the web for each one's exact wording, asks one of the two outside reviewer models whether the text reads as copied, and then I give my own verdict on each. Pre-flight was clean: no pull requests were left open by earlier runs, no reader has filed an entry report (a GitHub issue that points out a mistake in an entry), and the three leftover branches on GitHub were already listed for you to delete.

**The sample.** The ten fields were: the phrase *to a man* under **man**, the usage note on **patient** (noun against adjective), the musical-ear sense of **ear**, the stretchy-cloth sense of **stretch**, the suffix **-ist**, the usage note on **more** (determiner), the first sense of **away**, the phrase *on someone's side* under **side**, the phrase *read someone like a book* under **book**, and the phrase *go off the deep end* under **end**.

**What the searches and the reviewer found.** None of the ten exact-phrase searches turned up a published dictionary with the same wording. The nearest results were dictionary entries for related words (*go berserk*, *support*) and grammar pages that state the same facts in other words. Reviewer B (the non-Anthropic reviewer model) was asked once and called all ten original.

**My verdicts.** Six original; three *generic overlap*, meaning short, plain definitions that any dictionary would phrase about the same way (**-ist**, **away**, *on someone's side*); and one rewrite. The rewrite is *read someone like a book*. Its definition, *understand easily what someone is thinking or feeling*, was not found by the search, but reading it myself I thought it sat too close to a wording a well-known learner's dictionary uses. The rule is that a distinctive match gets rewritten, and when in doubt I lean towards rewriting. The new definition is *know what someone is thinking or feeling just from their face or the way they act*, and the phrase gained a second example (*She tried to look calm, but her mother could read her like a book*).

**The pipeline on the rewritten entry.** A rewritten entry goes through the full checking pipeline: format checks, inflections, pronunciation, and both reviewer models reading every field. I put **book** through the reviewers twice, once after the rewrite and again after my fixes. They raised eight minor issues and nothing blocking. I accepted six:

- *Open your books at page 20* became *to page 20*, since the dictionary's main variety is American English.
- An example using *exercise book*, which is mainly British, was replaced with a neutral one. Both reviewers flagged this one.
- The common idiom *off the books* (paid or done without official records, often to avoid tax) was missing, so I added it, labelled informal.
- The usage note and a learner-error note both said that *by* names a book's author. I cut the usage note back so that the fact appears in one place only, as the style guide requires.

I rejected two. *The oldest trick in the book* and *take a leaf out of someone's book* belong under **trick** and **leaf**, because by house rule an idiom goes under its first noun. And mentioning the opposite phrase (*in someone's bad books*) in an explanation is allowed and useful.

**Spend.** US$0.14 this cycle: half a cent for the originality question, and about US$0.07 for each of the two reviewer rounds. The day's total is US$0.45 of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. One point to keep in mind: the reviewer has now called every sampled field "original" in several checks running, so the web search and my own reading do most of the real work in this check. That is in line with what earlier reports said about reviewer precision.

**What is next.** The selector picks again for the next cycle. The queue in `NEXT.md` still leads with the cross-reference audit, closure building, and the coverage review of band-1 words (the 500 or so most important words), next from **flight** onward.

**What needs you.** Nothing new. Still waiting for a session with you: the pronunciation disputes in `reviews/needs_curator.txt` and the ten-entry samples in the weekly digests. The full record of this check is in `reviews/originality/2026-11-21.md`.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle was.** The selector chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words still waiting for an entry. It allowed up to twelve. I took six, because a full entry for each word matters more than the count. All six came from words that existing entries already point to: **dawn** (named by **sunrise**), **family name** (by **surname** and **last name**), **hashtag** (by **tag**), **metro** (by **subway**), **tablet** (by **pill** and **laptop**), and **takeaway** (by the phrasal verb **take away**). Each now has a page of its own instead of a grey link.

**What was written.**

- **dawn** has two senses: the early-morning one and the figurative *the dawn of a new age*. It also has two idioms, *at the crack of dawn* and *since the dawn of time*, and a note on how it differs from **sunrise**.
- **family name** has a note for translators. In names written with the family name first, as many East Asian names are, the family name is not the *last* name, so **family name** is the clearer term.
- **hashtag** covers the word on social media and the sign # itself. A usage note gives the other names for that sign.
- **metro** covers the city train system and, after review, the American use for a city together with its surrounding towns (*the metro area*).
- **tablet** has three senses: the computer, the piece of medicine, and the stone with writing on it. It also has a short note on choosing between **pill** (everyday American) and **tablet** (more British, and the word on medicine packets).
- **takeaway** has the British meal and the shop that sells it, plus the newer sense of an important point you remember from a meeting or a course.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 32 issues across the six entries. That includes a second round for **metro**, because its new sense had not been reviewed. Only one was *blocking*, meaning the entry could not be finished until it was settled. The adaptation note on **takeaway** (a note for translators) said the main-point sense is "a newer use". That is a claim about history, and by house rule such claims belong only in the etymology field, so I removed it. I accepted 24 issues in all. The main ones:

- I replaced examples that were unnatural or that stated a checkable fact: *Kim is a very common family name*, *press the hashtag key*, and a casual *like a tablet or something*.
- I replaced the British *sleeping tablets* with *iron tablets*.
- I made the meal sense of **takeaway** countable or uncountable, since people say both *a takeaway* and *we had takeaway*.
- I added the missing American city-area sense of **metro**, which both reviewers asked for.

I rejected eight, each with a logged reason:

- One reviewer called *takeaway food* an adjective use. House rules treat a noun before another noun as a noun.
- One reviewer said the bold mark is only for single words. The style guide applies it to any word or phrase named as a word.
- One reviewer wanted the American main-point sense of **takeaway** listed first. Senses are ordered by how useful they are, and the meal sense is the one most learners will meet.
- One reviewer called **tablet**'s translator note generic. It names one specific split, between the computer and the medicine senses, in the same way as the style guide's own model note for **bank**.

**Links.** The cross-reference tool added matching links back from nine existing entries, for example **computer** and **laptop** now point to **tablet**. One link it could not place automatically: **beginning** now lists **dawn** as a synonym, under its first sense, placed by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.20 this cycle. The day's total is US$0.65 of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. Two decisions first failed to log because one reviewer had raised two issues on the same field. The tool asks for the issue's number in that case, and both were then logged.

**What is next and what needs you.** The queue continues in the order set out in `NEXT.md`. Nothing new needs you.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle was.** The selector chose *review*: rereading entries that are already finished. There were no unreviewed drafts and no reader reports, so the cycle continued the *coverage pass*. That is the slow reread of the most important words (band 1) that you asked for in October, checking two things. Is anything a learner needs missing? And do the notes follow the rule that each fact is stated in one field only? This cycle took the next four words in the pass: **flight**, **foreign**, **forget**, and **friend**. About 400 band-1 words have now been reread this way.

**What I changed before the reviewers saw the entries.**

- **flight** gained the collocation *on a flight*.
- **foreign** gained *foreign investment*. Its translator note on pronunciation had repeated the pronunciation note word for word, so I cut it to the one point the other note does not make.
- **forget** stated the difference between *forget to do* (it was not done) and *forget doing* (the memory is gone) in three fields: the usage note, a learner-error note, and a note for translators. It also gave the stress pattern twice. Each fact now lives in one place.
- **friend** lost a repeated sentence about romantic partners. It gained the idiom *just friends* (friends and not a couple) and the real fixed pair *friend or foe* in place of *friend or enemy*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 33 issues. Two were *blocking*, meaning the entry could not be finished until they were settled, and both concerned **forget**. The entry listed *I forgot my keys at home* as a learner error and said to use **leave** whenever a place is named. Reviewer A objected that this sentence is natural English. I agree: it is common in everyday American speech, even though *I left my keys at home* is the more careful choice. So I removed the error. The advice now sits as a short note on the entry's *compare* link to **leave**, where it guides without calling a common sentence wrong.

I accepted 25 issues in all. The main ones:

- Both reviewers called the translator notes on **flight** and **foreign** generic, with no specific trap named. I dropped them, since such notes are empty by default.
- In **foreign**, the "unfamiliar" sense lost its *formal* label and its *predicative only* code, because people also say *a foreign idea* before a noun. A usage note that suggested *visitor* as a politer word was rewritten, since a visitor does not live in your country.
- The one-sentence summaries that open **forget** and **friend** now cover every sense; before, they missed forgetting on purpose and supporting a cause.
- **friend** now notes *my friend* used when speaking to someone directly (*thank you, my friend*).

I rejected eight, each with a logged reason:

- **local** stays as an opposite of **foreign**, because *local food* against *foreign food* is a real contrast.
- The running-away sense of **flight** stays labelled *formal*.
- *Forget about it* is the same idiom as *forget it*, which the entry already has, not a missing one.
- **companion** stays as a near-synonym of **friend**.

**Spend.** US$0.18 this cycle. The day's total is US$0.83 of the US$15 daily budget.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The coverage pass has no tool that lists its order, so I worked out the next four words the same way the earlier runs did: band-1 words with four senses, alphabetical, skipping any reread since 24 October. They are **funny**, **gas**, **general**, and **girl**.

**What is next and what needs you.** This run ends here, after three cycles. The conversation has grown long, and the rules say to stop rather than write thinner entries late in a run. Nothing new needs you.
