# 2026-09-30 (3) — Build: twelve verbs, *warn* to *work*

## What this run did

This was the third cycle of the run that began at 00:43 UTC on 2026-09-30. (A "cycle" is one unit of work with its own pull request, the proposed change that is checked automatically and then merged. The first two cycles, a site check and a lint pass, merged as #142 and #143.) The mode selector (the script that picks each cycle's kind of work) chose **build**: write new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words waiting to be written. The limit is twelve entries a cycle.

I claimed the next twelve words in band 1 (the most common words, the ones the definitions themselves are written with). All are verbs: *warn, wash, watch, water, wear, weigh, whisper, win, wipe, wish, wonder,* and *work*. I drafted each from my own knowledge and the house style guide, never from a published dictionary. Together they have 51 senses (numbered meanings), 9 phrases (fixed expressions such as *you can't win them all*), and 22 learner errors (mistakes learners of many languages make, each with a correction and a one-line reason).

Some highlights:

- *work* has nine senses. They run from having a job, through a machine working and a plan working, to shaping clay and a screw working loose.
- Eight entries have a synonym discrimination, a short note on how to choose between close words: *warn / caution / alert*, *wash / clean / rinse*, *watch / look / see*, *wear / put on / dress*, *whisper / murmur / mutter*, *win / beat / earn*, *wish / hope / want*, and *work / function / operate*.
- The learner errors target common traps: *I washed my teeth* (teeth are brushed), *We won the other team* (you beat a team), *I wish you will pass* (use *hope*), *I wonder what time is it* (statement word order), and *I saw TV last night* (you watch television).

## Checks

Every inflection (forms such as *wore, worn* and *won*) comes from the project's rules and its table of irregular forms. All were confirmed. All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each verb) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, which is several models voting, and by the open CMU pronouncing dictionary.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two outside language models, reviewer-a and reviewer-b, read every field of every entry. Between them they raised 41 issues. I read each one against the entry and decided it myself: 29 applied, 12 rejected, none sent on to the owner.

The most useful corrections:

- *warn* sense 3 had been "tell someone about something before it happens". That was too broad, since a warning is about something that may be a problem or unwelcome. The definition now says so.
- *watch* in the sense "take care of" said "for a short time". The entry's own example, *My sister watches the kids on Tuesdays*, is a regular arrangement, so the time limit is gone.
- *water*, of eyes, said the tears never come from sadness. That was too strong. It now says the usual causes are smoke, wind, pain, or strong smells, and that *cry* is the usual word for tears of sadness.
- *wipe* had a memory use (*wipe something from your memory*) inside its computing sense. That put a computing label on something that has nothing to do with computers. It is now its own sense.
- *work* in the sense of shaping clay also had the example *worked the land*, which the definition did not cover. The definition now includes using land to grow food.
- *wonder*: a learner-error note said *wonder* never takes a *that*-clause. That is not quite true: a rare, formal *wonder that* means "be surprised that". The note now says so.

The rejections were mostly reviewer-b's repeated objection to the quoted headword at the start of an adaptation note (the short notes for translators and for learners from particular language backgrounds). That use is house style and has been rejected many times before (seven times this cycle). I also kept the *formal* label on *as you wish*, and I kept *wipe a computer clean* as a computing collocation, since it is ordinary usage.

**Cross-references.** A script adds a matching reference to each word an entry names as a synonym or a word to compare. Two of its eight guesses landed on the wrong sense, and I moved them: *wear* now sits beside *dress* in the sense "put clothes on", and *wipe* beside *rub* in the sense "press and move". In three cases an older entry already called the pair "compare" while the new one called them synonyms (*clean* with *wash* and *wipe*, *earn* with *win*). I changed the new entries to match.

Six definition words were outside the defining vocabulary (the roughly 2,400 plain words definitions should use), such as *admiration* and *luck*. I reworded each definition with plainer words.
