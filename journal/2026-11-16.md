Twelve entries were written, all from the queue's band 2 (the second most important of five frequency bands): three verbs, **prioritize**, **proceed**, and **puzzle**, and nine nouns, **certificate**, **compliment**, **fantasy**, **front desk**, **inspiration**, **mining**, **mystery**, **receptionist**, and **recycling**. Several fill gaps that existing entries already pointed to: *reception* named *front desk* and *receptionist*; *praise* named *compliment*; *priority* named *prioritize*; *puzzle* (the noun) named *mystery* and the verb.

**Pronunciation.** All 24 transcriptions (American and British for each entry) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, three outside models voting with an open pronouncing dictionary as a fourth voice. No disputes.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** Each entry was read field by field by two reviewer models from different labs. Together they raised 39 issues, 4 of them blocking (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). I applied 37 and rejected 2.

- The main blocking problem was in **mystery**. I had given it a subsense for uses like *a mystery guest* and *a mystery prize*, defined as "someone or something whose name is not known". Both reviewers pointed out that in those phrases the word works only as a modifier before another noun, so a definition of a person or thing misleads. I removed the subsense and moved the use into the first sense's collocations, with one example and a short note. While there, I widened the first sense to cover people (*he's a bit of a mystery to me*), which one reviewer said was missing.
- In **compliment**, the sense "an action that shows respect" had the example *I'll take that as a compliment*, which is about something said. I moved it to the first sense and wrote a new example about an action. The formal plural sense (*my compliments to the chef*) was redefined as praise or good wishes rather than thanks, and *a backhanded compliment* was added.
- In **proceed**, the reviewers noted that the first sense covered only a person continuing an action, while the examples also had the activity as subject (*the work is proceeding slowly*). The definition was broadened and a one-line note on the two kinds of subject added. *Proceed with caution* moved to that sense.
- Smaller fixes: **fantasy** now allows *a complete fantasy* (countable) and no longer says fantasy is "the activity of imagining", which contradicted its own note; **front desk** gained a subsense for the people who work there (*the front desk said our room was ready*); **certificate** lost an example about cars that depended on local law, and *diploma* became a "compare" rather than a synonym.
- The two rejections: one reviewer called *often before another noun* an invalid grammar code, but it is in the closed list; the other said *puzzle about* is too rare to list beside *puzzle over*, but it is real and *over* already comes first.

The cross-reference checker asked for two links to be placed by hand. *Fantasy* had named *dream* as a synonym, but no sense of *dream* really matches (a dream is usually something you hope to achieve), so I removed the link. *Confuse*, first sense, now lists *puzzle* as a synonym. One definition word, *dig*, was outside the defining vocabulary, so *mining* now says "taking ... out of the ground".

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose a second build, again because building had waited longest among the kinds of work it could pick. Twelve band-2 nouns were written, all from the next stretch of the queue: **red** (the noun: the color and, informally, red wine), **reduction**, **referee**, **reference**, **reflection**, **reform**, **refrigerator**, **refugee**, **refund**, **regard**, **register**, and **registration**. Most had been waiting for a while as links from existing entries: the verbs *reduce*, *refer*, *reflect*, *reform*, *refund*, *regard*, and *register* all listed these nouns as family members, and *fridge* named *refrigerator* as its full form.

**Pronunciation.** All 24 transcriptions were confirmed. For *refugee* the panel split, because some American speakers stress the first syllable; the house form stresses the last, and the entry's pronunciation note mentions the other.

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 46 issues, 12 of them blocking. I applied 43 and rejected 3. This batch drew more blocking issues than the first, and most were fair.

- **Red.** Both reviewers said the idiom *in the red* (owing money) was missing. It existed, but under the adjective entry. The house rule puts an idiom under its first noun, and in *in the red* the word is a noun, so I moved it to the new noun entry and added an example (*go into the red*). The wine sense had been defined as "red wine", which uses the headword; it now says "wine with a dark color, made from dark grapes".
- **Reflection** needed the most work. Its "sign" sense had an example, *the accident is no reflection on the driver*, whose note gave the wrong meaning: the phrase means the accident does not make people think badly of the driver. That use now has its own subsense. The "careful thought" sense, which is common, moved up to second place, and its countable use (*his reflections on his first year*) became a subsense. One reviewer said *mirror* is not really a synonym here; the other agreed, so the link was removed from both entries.
- **Regard.** The script that writes word forms had recorded "no plural", although the good-wishes sense is always *regards*. This is a known gap in the tool: when one sense is plural and the others uncountable, it needs a line in the table of exceptions. I added that line, and the entry now lists *regards*. A British legal pattern, *have regard to*, was removed.
- **Registration** defined its car-document sense with *registered*, which is circular; it now says the document shows who owns a car and that it may be driven. **Reference** had a clumsy definition for a reference number, now rewritten.
- Several smaller issues were British uses in an American-first dictionary: *take the register* (checking which pupils are present), *booking reference*, *in the shops*. These were removed or changed.
- The three rejections: a reviewer wanted *saw* in *I saw red* marked as a form of the headword, but the headword is *red*, not *see*; *terms of reference* is specialist and mainly British, so I did not add it; and the first sense of *reduction* keeps "price", because prices falling in general belongs there, while the second sense is money off one item.

The cross-reference checker added two mirror links (*increase*, *freezer*) and left two for me, which I placed by hand: *respect* now lists *regard* in its first sense, and *reference* (the letter for a job) lists *referee*, the British word for the person who writes it.

**What did not work.** Two definitions used words outside the defining vocabulary (*electrical*, *license*) and one used *source*; all were reworded.

## Cycle 4: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose a review cycle: rereading existing entries instead of writing new ones. The order of review work is set by the owner's ruling of 2026-10-24. First come draft entries; there were none. Next come reports from readers; there were none. After that comes the coverage pass, which rereads the most important (band 1) words, starting with those that have the most senses, and asks whether each entry is complete and its examples fit. This cycle took the next four words in that pass, each with four senses: **arrive**, **attention**, **bank**, and **bath**. The pass has now covered 349 entries.

**Before the reviewers.** I read the four entries myself first. The commonest problem was a fact stated in two or three places, which the house rule "one fact, one field" forbids, because copies drift apart. Examples: the rule *arrive at* or *arrive in*, never *arrive to*, appeared in a usage note, a learner-error note, and a note for translators. Two definitions began with *the act of*, a phrase the style guide bans as padding. I also added what was missing: *attention span* and *attention to detail*; *bubble bath*; and a new sense of **bath**, the British *public baths* (a building with pools).

**What the reviewers found, and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 68 issues, 8 of them blocking. I applied 64 and rejected 4.

- **Arrive.** Two subsenses began their definitions with a limit on the subject (*of a baby, be born*). A house rule puts that limit in the explanation instead, so the definitions now read *be born* and *reach the person it was sent to*, with the subject stated beneath.
- **Attention.** The sense for a soldier's stance had the example *"Attention!" shouted the officer*. A reviewer pointed out that the shouted word is an interjection, a different part of speech, and each entry holds one part of speech. The example was replaced, the usage note now says in one clause that *Attention!* is an interjection, and that entry is queued to be written.
- **Bank.** The note on *bank holiday* said the name has nothing to do with banks being open. That is wrong: on a bank holiday the banks are closed. The fact is now stated correctly, once, in the culture note for translators. A grammar note said English says *blood bank*, not *a bank of blood*, while the same sense lists *a bank of questions*; it is now limited to stores of blood, food, and seeds. An example claimed that "anyone healthy can give blood", a medical claim that is not quite true, so it was rewritten.
- **Bath.** Most fixes removed duplicated facts. The *public baths* sense I had just added had an unnatural example (*hot baths in the hills*), now replaced.
- The four rejections: two objections to values that are in the closed lists (*often before another noun*; *humorous* as a register); a request to move *a bank of snow* out of the river sense, where it shares the idea of a raised edge; and a request to mark *took* in *took a bath*, which is a form of *take*, not of the headword.
