They were thin, so before the panel I added by hand:

- **Missing senses.**
  - *buy*: paying someone to do something dishonest (*buy votes*), and what an amount of money is enough for (*ten dollars doesn't buy much*).
  - *calculate*: the passive *be calculated to*, meaning "planned to have an effect" (*his words were calculated to hurt*).
  - *call*: four new senses. Describing someone (*she called me lazy*), arranging an event (*call a meeting*, *call a strike*), deciding a result in a game or vote (*too close to call*), and, mainly in British English, making a short visit.
  - *calm* without an object (*the wind calmed*).
- **A new phrase**: *call it quits*.
- **Learner errors.** These are mistakes learners commonly make:
  - *I brought a new car* when **bought** is meant
  - *She bought to me a present*
  - *I called to her yesterday* (meaning a phone call)
  - *My name is called Ana* and *How are you called?*

  - *Either Kim or her sisters is coming* (the verb agrees with the nearer subject)
  - *three times as bigger as*
- **Usage notes and other fixes.**
  - How *buy* marks the person who gets the thing (*buy her a coat*, *buy a coat for her*).
  - *calculated* versus *calculating*.
  - A short comparison of *call*, *phone*, and *ring*.
  - *twice* instead of *two times*.
  - **Removed.** *buy time* came out of *buy*: the house rule files an idiom under its first noun, and it is already under *time*. The one-sense entry *either* (conjunction) lost a "signpost" (a short sense label, which a one-sense entry should not carry); 37 such entries remain.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The panel raised 23 issues, 17 of them blocking (a blocking issue must be settled before an entry counts as reviewed). I applied 11, rejected 10, and escalated 2 (sent them to the owner).

What I accepted:

- *call*: the summary sentence at the top (its "core idea") was too long. The "ask to come" sense said "come to you", which does not fit calling an ambulance. The "arrange" sense needed to cover calling a strike.
- *call*: my note said *phone* was more British. It is common everywhere.
- *calculate*: a learner-error note claimed the verb never takes a preposition. That was too broad, so I narrowed it.
- *times*: my usage note said *three times bigger* and *three times as big* mean the same. They usually do, but some careful writers avoid the first, because it can be read as four times the size. The note now says so.

What I rejected:

- The first reviewer called *I don't like either tea or coffee* nonstandard. It is ordinary English, meaning you like neither.
- The first reviewer objected to the British label on the "visit" sense of *call*. A region label here means the use is *mainly* found there, which is the house rule.
- The first reviewer said *call it quits* means only a permanent stop. *Let's call it quits for today* is common.

What I escalated: the second reviewer argued that *three times bigger* uses **times** as a noun, not a preposition. It could be moved to the noun *time*. No open data settles this. I kept the use in *times* (preposition), marked the entry `grammar-disputed`, and added a line to the curator queue (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`).

The British pronunciation of *calculate* failed its first check (one vote of three) and passed a second (two of three).

The cross-reference tool added four matching links, in *pay, count, describe,* and *multiply*. I checked each one, and each is on the right sense.
