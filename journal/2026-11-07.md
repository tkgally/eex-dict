## Cycle 2: originality

**What this cycle did.** Every twentieth run, the selector forces an *originality check*. It is a spot check that the dictionary's wording is its own and not borrowed from a published dictionary. This was run 720, so the check was due. The tool `tools/originality_check.py` picked ten fields at random from across the dictionary: definitions of `tidy-v`, `strip-v`, `benefit-n`, and `appeal-n`, the phrase definitions *on the ground* (`ground-n`), *pull your socks up* (`sock-n`), and *put your feet up* (`foot-n`), and the usage notes of `daily-adv`, `dress-n`, and `seeing-conj`. For each, the run searched the web for the exact wording to see whether any published dictionary uses it. It also asked one outside reviewer model (reviewer B) whether each text reads as copied. The reviewer has to quote the source if it says "copied".

**What was found.** Seven came out *original*: no published match. Two came out *generic overlap*: short, plain wording that any dictionary would arrive at independently. These were *remove a covering or a layer from something* (`strip-v`) and *sit or lie down and rest* (*put your feet up*). One came out *rewrite*: the definition of *pull your socks up*, "start working harder or behaving better, because your work or behavior has not been good enough". A well-known learner's dictionary defines the idiom as making an effort to improve your work or behaviour "because it is not good enough". The idea is the idiom's own, but the matching clause and the pairing of *work or behavior* were close enough that I took the cautious verdict. Reviewer B called all ten original or "cannot tell" and named no source. It did remark that the *socks* wording was structurally like published definitions. As in past checks, the web search decided.

**The rewrite.** The rules treat a rewrite as a full review of the entry. The new wording is "try harder and do better after a time of doing badly or not making enough effort". The entry then went through the whole pipeline: format check, inflections, pronunciation check (both transcriptions verified), and two passes of the two-reviewer panel, because the first pass led to new material that also needed reading. Neither reviewer objected to the new definition. They raised seven minor points. I applied five: the main definition now says a sock covers "your foot and ankle, and sometimes part of your leg", since ankle socks reach no higher. The idioms *put a sock in it* (informal, British: stop talking) and *work your socks off* (informal: work extremely hard) were added. *Wool socks* moved from the adjective-plus-noun collocations to noun-plus-noun. The new *put a sock in it* got a plain verb definition, "stop talking or making noise", with its rudeness explained separately. I rejected two of reviewer B's label suggestions. It wanted *put a sock in it* not marked British, though it is chiefly British. It wanted *work your socks off* marked British, though it is common in both varieties. Nothing was escalated. The entry is back to `reviewed`.

**Spend.** About US$0.06 (US$0.005 for the originality question, the rest for the two panel passes).

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose `build`: write new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written), at most twelve in a cycle. Twelve band-2 nouns were claimed in queue order. (Band 2 is the second of five editorial frequency bands; band 1 holds the commonest words.) One of the twelve, *facts*, turned out to be just the plural of *fact*, which already has an entry. A cross-reference in the entry `data-n` had queued it by mistake. It was marked a duplicate, and fixing that link is listed for `data-n`'s next review. The other eleven were drafted from scratch: *expression*, *extension*, *extent*, *factor*, *fame*, *fare*, *farming*, *faucet*, *favorite*, *feel*, and *female*, all as nouns.

Some highlights. `extent-n` has a single sense, but the word is mostly used in fixed phrases, so it carries five: *to some extent*, *to a large extent*, *to what extent*, *to a lesser extent*, and *to the extent that*. It also has a learner-error note on the common slip *in some extent*. `favorite-n` covers the thing you like best, the competitor expected to win, a person treated better than others, and saved websites, plus the American idiom *play favorites*. `feel-n` separates how something feels to the touch (*the feel of cotton*) from the character of a place (*a relaxed feel*), with the idioms *get the feel of something* and *have a feel for something*. `female-n` has a usage note: as a noun for people, *female* belongs to science and official reports and can sound cold or rude in conversation.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Each entry went through the full pipeline: the format check, generated plural forms, the pronunciation check, and the two-reviewer panel. Five entries changed enough after the first panel pass that the panel read them a second time. In all, the reviewers raised 78 issues, 11 of them *blocking* (an issue that must be settled before an entry can be published). I applied 60 and rejected 18; none needed escalating to you. The important fixes:

- `extension-n` lacked the computing senses: the letters at the end of a file name (*.txt*), and a small program added to a web browser. Both were added. So was a subsense for the figurative *an extension of her personality*.
- `factor-n` had the sun-cream sense (*factor 30*) marked uncountable even though its examples said *a high factor*. That was corrected. The second pass caught that the maths examples stated checkable facts (*Three is a factor of twelve*), which house rules forbid, so they became classroom tasks (*Find all the factors of thirty*). A subsense for *the wow factor* and the phrase *the X factor* were also added.
- `fare-n` had tucked the entertainment use (*the usual holiday fare*) into an explanation. It now has its own subsense, and the food sense moved ahead of the taxi-passenger sense.
- `favorite-n` listed *a favorite among someone*, which is ungrammatical. It became *a favorite among students*.

The rejections were mostly one repeated objection: that an ordinary noun should never have an explanation field. House style puts a restriction on use there (*usually with an adjective*, *only in the phrase by a factor of*), so I kept those. *Claim to fame* was asked for under `fame-n`, but the keyword rule (an idiom goes under its first noun) puts it under `claim-n`.

All 22 transcriptions (American and British for each entry) were verified by the pronunciation panel and the open CMU data. Three cross-references needed a matching link placed by hand in the other entry, because the tool could not tell which sense to attach it to: `equation-n`, `addition-n`, and `ticket-n`.
