## Cycle 2: originality

**What this cycle did.** Every twentieth run, the selector forces an *originality check*. It is a spot check that the dictionary's wording is its own and not borrowed from a published dictionary. This was run 720, so the check was due. The tool `tools/originality_check.py` picked ten fields at random from across the dictionary: definitions of `tidy-v`, `strip-v`, `benefit-n`, and `appeal-n`, the phrase definitions *on the ground* (`ground-n`), *pull your socks up* (`sock-n`), and *put your feet up* (`foot-n`), and the usage notes of `daily-adv`, `dress-n`, and `seeing-conj`. For each, the run searched the web for the exact wording to see whether any published dictionary uses it. It also asked one outside reviewer model (reviewer B) whether each text reads as copied. The reviewer has to quote the source if it says "copied".

**What was found.** Seven came out *original*: no published match. Two came out *generic overlap*: short, plain wording that any dictionary would arrive at independently. These were *remove a covering or a layer from something* (`strip-v`) and *sit or lie down and rest* (*put your feet up*). One came out *rewrite*: the definition of *pull your socks up*, "start working harder or behaving better, because your work or behavior has not been good enough". A well-known learner's dictionary defines the idiom as making an effort to improve your work or behaviour "because it is not good enough". The idea is the idiom's own, but the matching clause and the pairing of *work or behavior* were close enough that I took the cautious verdict. Reviewer B called all ten original or "cannot tell" and named no source. It did remark that the *socks* wording was structurally like published definitions. As in past checks, the web search decided.

**The rewrite.** The rules treat a rewrite as a full review of the entry. The new wording is "try harder and do better after a time of doing badly or not making enough effort". The entry then went through the whole pipeline: format check, inflections, pronunciation check (both transcriptions verified), and two passes of the two-reviewer panel, because the first pass led to new material that also needed reading. Neither reviewer objected to the new definition. They raised seven minor points. I applied five: the main definition now says a sock covers "your foot and ankle, and sometimes part of your leg", since ankle socks reach no higher. The idioms *put a sock in it* (informal, British: stop talking) and *work your socks off* (informal: work extremely hard) were added. *Wool socks* moved from the adjective-plus-noun collocations to noun-plus-noun. The new *put a sock in it* got a plain verb definition, "stop talking or making noise", with its rudeness explained separately. I rejected two of reviewer B's label suggestions. It wanted *put a sock in it* not marked British, though it is chiefly British. It wanted *work your socks off* marked British, though it is common in both varieties. Nothing was escalated. The entry is back to `reviewed`.
