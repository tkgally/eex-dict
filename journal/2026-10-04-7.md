The block was the next twelve by creation date, all verbs written on 2026-09-29: *understand*, *upset*, *use*, *visit*, *vote*, *wake*, *walk*, *want*, *warm*, *warn*, *wash*, and *watch*.

## What I added before the panel

I read each entry in full first. These were among the fullest verbs in the dictionary: most already had synonym discussions, usage notes, learner errors, and phrases. Two changes:

- **walk**: a new sense, *walk someone through something*, meaning explain something to someone one step at a time (*Can you walk me through the form?*). It is common and learners would not guess it from the "on foot" sense; the entry's one-line summary (its *core idea*) was widened to cover it.
- **vote**: the collocation *vote by hand* is not something English speakers say; it was replaced with *vote by mail*.

## What the panel found and what I decided

The two reviewer models raised 27 issues, 19 of them marked blocking. I applied 16 and rejected 11. Every decision is logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

Real catches, applied:

- **wake**: a learner-error box marked *I waked up late* as wrong, but **waked** is a real (if much less common) past form, and the entry's own inflection table lists it. I replaced the box with a mistake learners really make, *I was woke up by the noise* (correct: *was woken up*), and the translator's note now names **waked** as much less common.
- **want**: the advice sense (*You want to be careful with that knife*) was defined with a modal verb, "would be wise to", against house style; it now reads *need to do something because it is wise or safe*. The police sense (*wanted by the police*) said only "may have done a crime"; people are also wanted because they may know something about one. The note comparing *want* and *wish* now allows *wish* for future events (*I wish it would stop raining*).
- **use**: *could use* (meaning *need*) was said to occur "usually in positive sentences"; questions such as *Could you use some help?* are just as natural.
- **understand**: the polite sense *I understand you're leaving* had a "formal" label, but it is common in ordinary speech; the label came off.
- Smaller fixes: a British *pay rise* in an American example (now *pay raise*), a grammar pattern in *wash*, a definition in *warm*, and a note in *upset* moved to its proper field.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- Four flags on the drug-taking sense of *use* (*He says he stopped using*). The reviewer read the sense as intransitive only and called the transitive example wrong; the sense is marked as both.
- Calls to remove links between *use* and *exercise*, and between *understand* and *read*, *take*, and *see*. Each is the mirror of a link in the other entry, and the dictionary keeps such links on both sides.
- A flag against a quoted headword at the start of a translator's note in *warn*, which house style allows.
