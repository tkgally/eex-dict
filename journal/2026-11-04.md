- The translator's view (a toggle that shows notes meant for translators) opens, stays open after a reload, and closes again.
- Dark mode has a dark background. The sense menu on long entries jumps to the right meaning.
- A cross-reference to a word that has no entry yet is shown in grey, not as a link, with the legend that explains it.

No JavaScript errors were reported during any of these steps. Nothing was broken, so nothing in the site builder or its templates changed. The built site (`docs/`) is never committed: GitHub Actions rebuilds it after the merge.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build cycle, which writes new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). Its limit was twelve new entries and US$3 of paid calls. I wrote ten, the next ten band-2 nouns in queue order after *bride*. Band 2 is the second of the dictionary's five frequency groups: common words, but less central than band 1. The ten are **broadcast**, **brown**, **browser**, **budget**, **builder**, **bulb**, **bull**, **burden**, **businessman**, and **butcher**, all nouns. I took ten, not twelve, so that each entry got a full draft.

**What the entries contain.** Each entry has signposted senses (a short label that helps the reader pick the right meaning), examples, collocations (the words a noun usually goes with, grouped by type), and cross-references. Some points worth knowing:

- *budget*: money to spend, and a plan of money coming in and going out. The idiom *on a budget* is included, and a note points to the adjective use (*a budget hotel*), which was added to the queue as `budget-adj`.
- *bull*: the farm animal, with a sub-meaning for other large male animals (*a bull elephant*); the stock-market buyer who expects prices to rise; and informal *bull* for nonsense. Three idioms: *take the bull by the horns*, *like a bull in a china shop*, and the American informal *shoot the bull*.
- *burden*: a worry, duty, or cost (or a person) that is hard to deal with, plus the literary meaning of a heavy load, and the legal phrase *the burden of proof*.
- *browser*: the computer program, and a person looking around a store with no plan to buy.
- *butcher*: the meat seller (with the store as a sub-meaning: *a butcher shop* in American English, *the butcher's* in British English), and a cruel killer.

All 20 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, a group of language models whose votes are checked against the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. Plurals came from the inflection rules; *businessmen* is handled by the rule for words ending in *-man*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers, language models from two different companies that check every field, raised 42 issues, 2 of them blocking (they must be settled before an entry is published). I applied 34 and rejected 8.

- *Blocking, applied:* the carried-load meaning of *burden* was labeled formal. It is literary, and is now labeled that way.
- *Blocking, rejected:* a reviewer objected to the note on the butcher's-store sub-meaning that says which form each country uses. The style guide (section 4) says that when a sub-meaning is limited to one form, the limit goes in exactly that note.
- *The biggest change* was to *builder*. Both reviewers saw that my examples showed the British everyday use (*the builders are coming to redo the kitchen*), while the entry's own note said that American English usually says *contractor* for that. The entry now leads with the American use (a company that puts up new houses), marks the kitchen example as British, and keeps the *contractor* fact in one note only. Both reviewers also pointed out a missing use, which I added as a second sense: *a confidence builder*, *a team builder*.
- *Other fixes:* *budget* is now "countable or uncountable" because of *over budget* and *within budget*. The definition of *burden* now covers a person (*I don't want to be a burden to my children*). An example that stated a fact about animal size was replaced. Several collocations were moved to the right type (*a web browser* is noun + noun). A note on *brown* that only repeated other fields was removed.
- *Rejected:* *beast of burden*, because under the keyword rule an idiom belongs to its first noun, here *beast*; its queue row is deferred with that reason. Also rejected: a syllable break inside the *ch* sound of *butcher* (the transcription is verified, and house form keeps the sound whole), and a request to hand-mark *businessmen* in an example (the site marks listed plurals itself).

The vocabulary check found four words outside the defining vocabulary (the plain words definitions are written in) in two definitions: *onions*, *tulips*, *elephants*, *whales*. I reworded those definitions; the words stay in the examples. One cross-reference back-link, from *program* (the TV or radio sense) to *broadcast*, was placed by hand because the tool could not tell which sense to use.

## Cycle 3: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a review cycle. There were no unreviewed drafts and no entry reports from readers, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. That pass is a careful reading of the most important everyday words (band 1), asking of each entry whether it is missing a meaning that a learner will meet. This cycle read the next four six-sense entries not read since your rulings of 2026-10-24: **language** (noun), **knock** and **kill** (verbs), and **key** (noun). That brings the pass to 181 entries.

**What was added.**
- *knock*: a new meaning, *the bad result knocked her confidence*, and a sub-meaning for hitting something on purpose so that something comes off it (*she knocked the snow off her boots*).
- *kill*: two new informal meanings, *you're killing me* (make someone laugh a lot) and *I'd kill for a cold drink* (want something very much). Also a sub-meaning for stopping a pain or taste (*these pills will kill the pain*) and the idiom *kill it* (do something extremely well, *she killed it at the audition*).
- *key*: the idiom *off key* (sounding higher or lower than the right notes). The answer key (*the answer key is at the back of the book*) is now a sub-meaning under the map key, not part of one definition joined by "or".

**What was cut or corrected.** All four entries stated the same fact in two or three places, against the "one fact, one field" rule. For example, *knock on the door* was explained in the usage note, a learner-error note, and a translator's note; *key to* was explained three times. Each fact now lives in one field. In *knock*, two examples were really the phrasal verbs *knock over* and *knock down*, which have entries of their own, so they were replaced. The "criticize" meaning (*don't knock it until you've tried it*) moved above the rarer "make a hole" meaning. In *language*, the meaning covering sign language and body language said "without words". That is wrong for sign languages, which have words, and it now says "without spoken words". An example claiming that bees have a language was replaced, because examples may not state science facts. *Tongue* was removed as a synonym of *language*, since in that meaning it is literary (*mother tongue*). Following the rule that a removed cross-reference loses its mirror too, the link back from *tongue* became a plain "see also".

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers raised 51 issues, 8 of them blocking. I applied 47 and rejected 4. The blocking issues were all fair, and all were applied:
- In *kill*, the threat meaning (*Mom will kill me*) was defined as being angry *and punishing*, which contradicted its own explanation that it is an exaggeration.
- *I'd kill for a drink* was marked as taking an object, but it takes *for* or *to*.
- The note comparing *kill* and *murder* said *murder* is "only" used for a crime, yet the entry itself lists *murder* as an informal synonym in *Mom will murder me*.
- *kill it* was missing.
- In *knock*, the example *the builders knocked two small rooms into one* did not show "make a hole".
- In *language*, the "without words" definition, described above.

Rejected:
- *kill two birds with one stone*: it is already in the entry for *bird*, the idiom's first noun.
- A learner error, *a bad language*: one reviewer said it only repeats the countability, but it is a real mistake and the note gives the reason.
- A request for a no-object verb pattern: no such pattern exists in the list, and the transitivity field already records the use.
- A complaint that the translator's note on *kill* is generic: it names a specific trap, which is not to use a word meaning "cause death" for the pain, time, and laughter meanings.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. More than a third of the issues on *knock* were minor, but they were mostly accurate, so this does not count as reviewer noise.

## Cycle 4: closure

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a closure cycle. Closure fills gaps in the words the dictionary already points to. Here that meant "family" rows: words listed in another entry's word family (its derived and related words) that had no entry of their own yet. Under your ruling A3, closure takes only band 1 and 2 words for now. This was the run's fourth and last cycle, so I kept it to six entries: **calculation**, **calculator**, **cashier**, **catch**, **celebration**, and **chemical**, all nouns. They complete the families of *calculate*, *cash*, *catch*, *celebrate*, and *chemical* (adjective).

**What the entries contain.**
- *catch* is the largest, with six meanings: catching a ball (and, informally, noticing a mistake, *Good catch!*), a hidden problem (*what's the catch?*), the game (*play catch*), an amount of fish caught, a small part that keeps a door or box closed, and a person thought to be a good partner (*he's a real catch*). It has the phrase *a catch in someone's voice* and a translator's note: in many languages the "hidden problem" meaning is a different word from catching.
- *calculation*: working with numbers (*by my calculations*), and careful thought about likely results (*a political calculation*).
- *calculator*: the machine or a program on a phone.
- *cashier*: the person who takes your money in a store or bank.
- *celebration*: a party, the act of showing joy (*a cause for celebration*), and a work that praises something (*a celebration of friendship*).
- *chemical*: a substance, with a translator's note. In everyday English, *chemicals* often suggests harmful factory-made substances (*grown without chemicals*), even though in science everything is a chemical.

All 12 transcriptions were confirmed by the pronunciation panel.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 22 issues, 1 of them blocking. I applied 21 and rejected 1.
- *Blocking, applied:* the example *she opened the catch* was unnatural. One undoes or releases a catch, so it now reads *undid*.
- *Catch:* both reviewers objected that *lock* is not a synonym of *catch*. A lock needs a key; *latch* is the near-synonym. *Latch* is now the synonym, and *lock* became a "compare" link with a short note on the difference. I placed the matching link in the *lock* entry by hand. *Safety catch* was removed from the collocations (it is part of a gun, not a fastener), and the opening definition no longer uses *catching* to define *catch*.
- *Calculation:* both reviewers pointed out that the second meaning is also neutral and countable (*a political calculation*, *make the calculation that it was worth the risk*). I widened it and removed the "disapproving" label.
- *Calculator:* the definition said only "a small electronic machine", but one example was a calculator on a phone. The definition now includes a program.
- *Rejected:* one complaint that *careful calculation* was too neutral for a disapproving meaning. After the widening, that label is gone and the collocation fits.
- *Cashier* and *chemical* drew no issues at all.

**Spend.** US$0.18. The day's total after four cycles is US$0.68 of the US$15 cap.
