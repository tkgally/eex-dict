A site cycle writes no entries. It rebuilds the public website from the entry files and then opens the result in a real browser to check that it works.

**The check.** `tools/site_check.py` drives a headless Chromium browser at a phone width (360 pixels) and a desktop width (1,280 pixels). It ran 79 checks and all 79 passed. Among them:

- No page scrolls sideways at phone width. All 3,089 pages were tested, not a sample.

- The address of a missing page (for example, `w/runxx.html`) gives a styled 404 page that searches for the name.
- Hovering over a linked word in a definition opens a short preview.
- The translator's view (a toggle that shows notes meant for translators) opens, stays open after a reload, and closes again.
- Dark mode has a dark background. The sense menu on long entries jumps to the right meaning.
- A cross-reference to a word that has no entry yet is shown in grey, not as a link, with the legend that explains it.

No JavaScript errors were reported during any of these steps. Nothing was broken, so nothing in the site builder or its templates changed. The built site (`docs/`) is never committed: GitHub Actions rebuilds it after the merge.

## Cycle 2: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose a build cycle, which writes new entries from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). Its limit was twelve new entries and US$3 of paid calls. I wrote ten, the next ten band-2 nouns in queue order after *bride*. Band 2 is the second of the dictionary's five frequency groups: common words, but less central than band 1. The ten are **broadcast**, **brown**, **browser**, **budget**, **builder**, **bulb**, **bull**, **burden**, **businessman**, and **butcher**, all nouns. I took ten, not twelve, so that each entry got a full draft.

**What the entries contain.** Each entry has signposted senses (a short label that helps the reader pick the right meaning), examples, collocations (the words a noun usually goes with, grouped by type), and cross-references. Some points worth knowing:

- *budget*: money to spend, and a plan of money coming in and going out. The idiom *on a budget* is included, and a note points to the adjective use (*a budget hotel*), which was added to the queue as `budget-adj`.
- *bull*: the farm animal, with a sub-meaning for other large male animals (*a bull elephant*); the stock-market buyer who expects prices to rise; and informal *bull* for nonsense. Three idioms: *take the bull by the horns*, *like a bull in a china shop*, and the American informal *shoot the bull*.
- *burden*: a worry, duty, or cost (or a person) that is hard to deal with, plus the literary meaning of a heavy load, and the legal phrase *the burden of proof*.
- *browser*: the computer program, and a person looking around a store with no plan to buy.
- *butcher*: the meat seller (with the store as a sub-meaning: *a butcher shop* in American English, *the butcher's* in British English), and a cruel killer.

All 20 transcriptions (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, a group of language models whose votes are checked against the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. Plurals came from the inflection rules; *businessmen* is handled by the rule for words ending in *-man*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewers, language models from two different companies that check every field, raised 42 issues, 2 of them blocking (they must be settled before an entry is published). I applied 34 and rejected 8.

- *Blocking, applied:* the carried-load meaning of *burden* was labeled formal. It is literary, and is now labeled that way.
- *Blocking, rejected:* a reviewer objected to the note on the butcher's-store sub-meaning that says which form each country uses. The style guide (section 4) says that when a sub-meaning is limited to one form, the limit goes in exactly that note.
- *The biggest change* was to *builder*. Both reviewers saw that my examples showed the British everyday use (*the builders are coming to redo the kitchen*), while the entry's own note said that American English usually says *contractor* for that. The entry now leads with the American use (a company that puts up new houses), marks the kitchen example as British, and keeps the *contractor* fact in one note only. Both reviewers also pointed out a missing use, which I added as a second sense: *a confidence builder*, *a team builder*.
- *Other fixes:* *budget* is now "countable or uncountable" because of *over budget* and *within budget*. The definition of *burden* now covers a person (*I don't want to be a burden to my children*). An example that stated a fact about animal size was replaced. Several collocations were moved to the right type (*a web browser* is noun + noun). A note on *brown* that only repeated other fields was removed.
- *Rejected:* *beast of burden*, because under the keyword rule an idiom belongs to its first noun, here *beast*; its queue row is deferred with that reason. Also rejected: a syllable break inside the *ch* sound of *butcher* (the transcription is verified, and house form keeps the sound whole), and a request to hand-mark *businessmen* in an example (the site marks listed plurals itself).

The vocabulary check found four words outside the defining vocabulary (the plain words definitions are written in) in two definitions: *onions*, *tulips*, *elephants*, *whales*. I reworded those definitions; the words stay in the examples. One cross-reference back-link, from *program* (the TV or radio sense) to *broadcast*, was placed by hand because the tool could not tell which sense to use.
