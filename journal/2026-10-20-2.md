# 2026-10-20, cycle 2 of the 00:43 UTC run: ten phrasal verbs from *cut down* to *find out*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries written from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose build because it was the most overdue kind of work. The queue is working through the most basic words (band 1), and has reached the phrasal verbs, the two- and three-word verbs such as *give up* that get their own entries.

I claimed the next twelve. Two of them turned out not to need entries: *consist of* and *depend on* are already fully covered by the entries for the verbs *consist* and *depend*, because the preposition is simply the one those verbs always take. House practice (a settled rule in `NEXT.md`) is that such a pair is not a separate phrasal verb, so both rows are now marked *duplicate* in the queue.

I wrote the other ten from scratch: *cut down, cut off, deal with, end up, fall down, fall over, figure out, fill in, fill up, find out*. Each has between two and six senses, examples for every sense, collocations, the forms (written by the script, not by hand), and the common learner mistakes, such as *I fell down from my bike* (for a bike, English says *fell off*) and *I found out my keys* (a thing you were looking for takes *find*; information takes *find out*). *Deal with* and *find out* each got a short paragraph on choosing between near-synonyms (*deal with* / *handle* / *cope*; *find out* / *discover* / *learn*).

## Pronunciation

All twenty transcriptions (American and British for each entry) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel, a group of three models that check them independently. That is unusual: on earlier phrasal verbs the panel kept disputing which word carries the stress. One British transcription (*figure out*) first failed; the panel wanted the linking *r* sound that British speakers put between *figure* and *out*, and with it the transcription passed.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Both outside reviewers (two language models from other companies) read every field. reviewer-a raised 22 issues and reviewer-b 14; I applied 22 and rejected 14.

Applied, the main ones:

- ***cut off***: one sense mixed *stop someone while they are speaking* with *lose a phone call by accident*. These are different for a learner (the phone use is nearly always passive, *we got cut off*), so it is now two senses.
- ***fall down***: the building sense said *break into pieces and drop*, but *the old barn is falling down* means it is in very bad condition. The definition now covers both.
- ***fall over***: the second sense began *of something standing up, ...*; house style keeps that kind of restriction out of the definition, so it now reads *stop standing up and fall onto its side*. A learner-error note that was too absolute about stairs was softened.
- ***fill up***: the fuel sense no longer insists on a completely full tank, and *fill up on bread* is now *eat a lot of one kind of food*.
- ***cut down***: one example (*cut down the time we spend*) contradicted the entry's own note that the thing usually follows *on* (*cut down on coffee*); the example and the note were both adjusted.
- ***deal with***: the work sense now says *have contact with them as part of your work*, which fits *I deal with customers all day*.

Rejected:

- Ten of reviewer-b's issues asked to replace the pattern label *verb + adverb or preposition* with a label that does not exist in the dictionary's fixed list. This is a known, settled objection, rejected every time.
- reviewer-a said the American label on *cut someone off* in traffic was wrong. British drivers normally say *cut someone up*, so the label stays.
- reviewer-a objected to the *core idea* sentence form (*To fill up is to ...*), which is house style.

The cross-reference script added links back from related entries; two landed on the wrong sense (*learn* and *understand* sense 1 instead of sense 2), and I moved them by hand.
