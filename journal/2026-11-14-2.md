# Run report, 2026-11-14 (second run of the day, e2gonv)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector (the script that picks each cycle's job from what the dictionary most needs) chose *build*: new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words still to be written. Its limit was twelve entries and US$3 of paid model calls. I claimed the next twelve band-2 words in queue order (band 2 is the second most important of five frequency bands) and wrote all of them. Every one went through the full pipeline and is now `reviewed`, meaning it is published on the site.

The twelve nouns are *photo*, *physics*, *piano*, *pie*, *pile*, *pit*, *pitch*, *playground*, *plenty*, *plot*, *poem*, and *poet*. The bigger ones:

- **pitch** has five senses: how high a sound is, a talk meant to sell something (*a sales pitch*), a sports field (British) with a subsense for a camping or market place, a throw in baseball (American), and the level of a feeling.
- **pit** has five senses: a hole in the ground (with the orchestra pit as a subsense), the hard seed of a fruit (American; British says *stone*), a coal mine (British), the stopping area beside a race track, and a small hollow mark on a surface. It also has three idioms: *the pits*, *the pit of your stomach*, and *a bottomless pit*.
- **pie** covers the food and the idea of a share (*a bigger slice of the pie*), plus *pie in the sky*, *as easy as pie*, and *eat humble pie*.
- **plot** covers the story, a secret plan, and a piece of land, plus *the plot thickens* and *lose the plot*.
- **plenty** as a noun is the literary use (*a land of plenty*). The everyday use (*plenty of time*) is the existing pronoun entry, and each entry points to the other.

All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary. Plural forms came from the rules. *Physics* and *plenty* correctly have none.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models (one from Anthropic, one from Google) raised 76 issues. Eight were *blocking*, meaning the entry cannot be published until they are settled. I applied all eight:

- In *pile*, I had added the soft surface of a carpet as a third sense. Both reviewers were right that this is a different word with a separate history, so it must be its own entry (a *homograph*). I removed it, together with the summary sentence and the translator's note that tried to tie the two meanings together.
- In *pitch*, the opening summary sentence forced unrelated meanings into one idea. I rewrote it to say plainly that the word has several separate uses. I also moved the British sports-field sense below the senses an American-based dictionary should list first.
- In *a bottomless pit*, one example (a teenager who eats all day) did not fit a definition that only mentioned money and effort. I widened the definition, because the hungry-person use is common.

Of the minor issues I applied most. Mostly I cut notes that stated the same fact twice, which the house rule "one fact, one field" forbids: in *photo*, *physics*, *poem*, *pitch*, and *plenty*. I also dropped weak collocations (*a fresh pie*, *a hard pitch*) and moved noun-before-noun compounds (*apple pie*, *sales pitch*) to the right collocation type. I added coverage the reviewers found missing: *the physics of something*, *eat humble pie*, the small-mark sense of *pit*, and the camping pitch. I rejected five:

- a request for a second example on a subsense (the style guide allows one);
- two requests to hand-mark *lost the plot* (the site already marks the headword);
- a request for a "figurative" label, which is not in the closed label lists;
- a complaint about using an explanation to mention the short form *easy as pie*.

All 76 decisions are logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl`.

The cross-reference tool adds a link back from each entry the new words point to, but it could not pick the right sense for 15 of them. I placed those by hand: for example, *physics* in the science senses of *chemistry* and *biology*, and *plot* in the "area of ground" sense of *land*.

**Spend.** US$0.34 for this cycle (the reviewer model from Google and the pronunciation panel; the Anthropic reviewer costs nothing in the ledger). The day's total is US$1.53 of the US$15 daily cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. A small slip in the decision logging: I first tried to tell apart two issues on the same field by quoting the reviewer's comment, but the tool matches the quoted entry text. I used the position number instead. No tool change is needed.

**What is next.** The queue continues with *pace* and the following band-2 words. I left three items for later, recorded in `NEXT.md`: a separate `pile-n-2` entry for the carpet surface, a `pitch-n-2` entry for the black tar (which carries *pitch-black*), and the idiom *have a finger in every pie*, which belongs under *finger* by the rule that an idiom goes under its first noun.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new from this cycle. The pronunciation disputes and the ten-entry samples in the weekly digests are still waiting for a session with you.

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *review*: rereading entries that are already published. No entries were waiting as drafts and no reader reports were open, so the cycle continued the coverage pass. This is the owner's 2026-10-24 instruction to reread the 500 most important entries for missing senses and idioms, taking the words with the most senses first. I read the next five, each with five senses: *together* (adverb), *top* (noun), *train* (verb), *use* (noun), and *value* (noun). The pass now stands at 329 entries.

Before sending them to the reviewers, I added what was missing:

- *together*: a subsense for being in one place or group (*stay together so nobody gets lost*, *close together*).
- *top*: a subsense for the leaves of plants such as carrots (*carrot tops*) and the idiom *from the top* (start again from the beginning).
- *use*: the idiom *have no use for something* and collocations such as *drug use* and *land use*.
- *value*: collocations such as *shock value*.

I also cut notes in *use*, *value*, and *train* that repeated a fact stated in another field.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 50 issues. One was blocking: the *use* entry listed *make the most use of* as a pattern of *make use of*. That is not natural English (the phrase is *make the most of*), so I removed it. I applied 39 issues in all:

- *use*: two learner errors were invented or too strict, so I removed them, and the fact that *it's no use* takes *-ing* now lives only in that phrase.
- *value*: the "good value for money" sense now comes after the more common importance and beliefs senses, and its article note moved into the usage note.
- *together*: the couple sense is now defined as an adverb phrase.
- *top* and *train*: generic translator notes were removed or narrowed.
- Removing *exercise* as a synonym of *train* also meant removing the matching link back from *exercise*.

I rejected 11:

- The reviewers called the definitions of *use* circular because they contain the verb *use*. That verb is in the defining vocabulary (the word list definitions are written from), and any paraphrase would be harder for an intermediate reader.
- *at face value* belongs under *face*, the first noun, where it already is.
- Two requests to hand-mark *tops*: the site already marks that plural.
- A request for a "gardening" label, which is not in the closed label list.

Both reviewers asked for *off the top of your head* under *top*. The style guide files that idiom under *head*, so I rejected the request here. But I checked, and the idiom is in fact missing from *head*. It is now on the to-do list in `NEXT.md`.

**Spend.** US$0.28 for this cycle. The day's total is US$1.81 of US$15.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The style guide's own model-entry section says *head* holds *off the top of your head*, and it does not. The next review of *head* should add it.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *voice*, *walk*, and *want*. The lint pass (the periodic whole-dictionary check) is due in two more cycles.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.
