## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries written from the queue of words the dictionary still needs. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job) chose it because building was again furthest behind its share of the work. With the band 1 adjectives finished in cycle 1, the queue now hands out band 1 adverbs (band 1 is the most common words), and this cycle took the first twelve: *abroad*, *absolutely*, *across*, *after*, *again*, *all*, *almost*, *alone*, *already*, *also*, *always*, and *anywhere*. Only fourteen adverbs existed before.

Several of these words already had entries under another part of speech (*after* as a preposition and a conjunction, *across* as a preposition, *all* as a determiner and a pronoun, *alone* as an adjective). Each new adverb entry says in one sentence where the other uses live, and the existing entries were linked back to them. Some points worth knowing:

- *all* (adverb) holds the fixed phrases that the *all* determiner entry already pointed to: *at all*, *all in all*, *all along*, *all over*, and *all but*, plus the score use (*three all*) and *all the better*.
- *already*, *yet*, and *still* are set side by side in a short note on choosing between them, and the entry notes that American English often says *I already ate* where British English says *I've already eaten*.
- *almost* and *nearly* get the same kind of note, and *also*, *too*, and *as well* another, mostly about where each goes in the sentence.
- *again* records the two common pronunciations (rhyming with *ten*, or with *rain*).

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two reviewer models from other companies read every field of every entry. They raised 44 issues, 35 of them marked blocking (meaning the entry must not be published until each is settled). I accepted 38 and rejected 6. None needed the owner. *absolutely* and *always* came back with no issues.

The main lesson of this batch: function words invite rules that are too absolute, and the first reviewer caught most of them.

- *alone* had a second sense, *only* (*You alone can decide*, *The ticket alone cost fifty dollars*). The reviewer said this is the adjective. I first thought it was a focusing adverb, like *only* itself, but the existing *alone* adjective entry already has exactly this sense (*Sara alone knew where the key was*). Two entries holding the same sense would contradict each other, so I removed it from the adverb and pointed to the adjective.
- *almost* said that only *almost* can come before *no* and *never*, and only *nearly* after *not*. Both are tendencies, not rules; the note now says *much more usual* and *the usual word*. Its explanation also said that *almost* before a verb means something did not happen, which fails for *I've almost finished*; that is fixed too.
- *again* treated *I again called her* as an error. It is formal, not wrong; the error was removed and the usage note now says so.
- *already* had a similar doubtful error; it was replaced by a common real one (*I have finished my homework yet*).
- *anywhere* called the range use (*anywhere from two to four hours*) American and informal; it is neither, and both claims were removed.
- The second reviewer said *also* was defined by a list of synonyms (*as well; too*); it is now *as well as that; too*.

Rejected, among others: a reviewer wanted *across from* (meaning *opposite*) shown as general English; the project's region labels mean *mainly used there*, and British English mainly says *opposite*. Another wanted *already* to stop pointing to *all*; the *all* entry points to *already* (about *all ready* and *already*), and removing one side would be re-created by the next consistency pass.
