## What the reviewers found and what I decided

reviewer-a raised 29 issues; reviewer-b raised 1. I applied 22 and rejected 8. No question needed the owner. *Partly* drew no issues at all.

Applied, among others:

- ***personally***: sense 2 (*done by you yourself*) was defined only for *you*, and it held *You will be personally responsible*, which does not mean doing it yourself. Sense 2 now says *done by a particular person themselves*, and sense 3 now means *as one person, or about a person's own feelings or life, not as part of a group or as someone in a job*. It takes *personally responsible* and *personally involved*, as well as *know someone personally* and *take something personally*.
- ***perfectly***: sense 2 said *completely, or very*. It is used only with some adjectives (*perfectly safe*, *perfectly clear*), so *very* was too broad. It now says *completely, or in every way*.
- ***properly***: the informal British use (*I was properly scared*) means *very*, not *completely*. The definition, the opening summary, and the collocations were changed to match.
- ***probably***: a learner-error note stated a word-order rule and then gave an example that broke it. It now gives both correct orders: *He probably won't come* and *He will probably not come*.
- ***perhaps***: sense 3 (*There were perhaps fifty people there*) now reads *used before a number to show that it is only a guess*. Both reviewers flagged the old wording. The comparison box no longer says that *possibly* always means *less likely*.
- ***rather***: the example *She walked rather than take the bus* became *She decided to walk rather than take the bus*, where the plain verb form fits better.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- reviewer-a said that in British English, saying *quite* with stress makes it stronger. In fact, *it's QUITE good*, said with the voice falling and rising on *quite*, means *only fairly good*. The entry keeps its note.
- *More rarely* and *most rarely* are normal English, so *rarely* keeps the code for comparatives with *more*.
- *Rather* meaning *fairly* gets no region label: it is more common in British English but is also used in American English, and a label here would mean *mainly British*.
- *He will not probably come* stays as a learner error: learners produce it, and native speakers would find it odd.
