# Run report, 2026-10-25 (fourth run of the day)

## Cycle 1: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector, the tool that picks each cycle's job, chose *review*: rereading entries that already exist rather than writing new ones. Its reason was that review had the largest backlog among the jobs allowed today. No entries were waiting as drafts, so I took the next items from `NEXT.md`, the file that hands work from one run to the next.

The first was a small fix left by an earlier run. The entry for *centimeter* gave a conversion fact ("there are 100 centimeters in a meter") inside its definition, after a semicolon. A definition should be a phrase that could replace the word in a sentence. It now reads "a small unit for measuring length, one of the 100 equal parts of a meter". The usage note now says that the word is written *cm* after a number.

The rest of the cycle continued the coverage pass. This pass rereads the most common words (band 1, the dictionary's top frequency group) to check that no current meaning is missing. It goes through the nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs with the most senses first. This cycle took the six that the previous review named: *strong*, *time*, *stand*, *sharp*, *set* and *keep*.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** Two reviewer models read each entry field by field. Reviewer A is a Claude model and reviewer B is a Google model. They raised 102 points on the seven entries, 20 of them "blocking", meaning the entry should not stand until the point is settled. I accepted 89 and rejected 13. Nothing needed to go to the owner.

Missing meanings were added:
- *strong* now covers rude language (*strong language*) and likely chances (*a strong chance of rain*).
- *stand* now covers being strong enough not to be damaged (*these plates can stand high heat*) and the collocation *stand trial*.
- *sharp* now covers short, loud sounds (*a sharp knock*) and an informal sense meaning stylish (*a sharp suit*).
- *set* now covers *set a trap*.
- *keep* now covers keeping a quality or control (*keep your balance*), saving something for someone (*keep a seat for me*), not telling someone something (*kept the news from her parents*), and following a rule or path (*keep to the schedule*).
- *time* gained the phrase *most of the time*.

Several fixes were about quality, not coverage. *Strong* had one sense that mixed "good at", "successful" and "likely to win". It is now narrower, and the "likely" meaning has its own subsense. In *stand*, the very common *can't stand* sense now comes before the rarer "put something upright" sense. Its definition now says "dislike" as well as "accept". *Time* claimed that *the last time* takes the present perfect, as *the first time* does. That is wrong (*It was the last time I saw him*), so the claim was removed.

Three learner errors broke the house rule against invented mistakes. Two were made-up regular past tenses (*standed*, *setted*). The third was a generic grammar slip (*a sharp increasing*) that has nothing to do with the word itself. All three were removed, along with one more on *time* that the reviewer judged invented. Three translator notes said only "translate each sense on its own", which the rules forbid. Each was replaced with a specific trap. For *sharp*, for example, the note now says that in some languages the word for "sharp" also means spicy, but an English *sharp* taste is sour, not hot. Many facts were repeated in two or three fields, and the extra copies were cut.

Most of the rejections followed the keyword rule: an idiom is filed under its first noun, or under its first verb if it has no noun. *Stand a chance*, *stand your ground*, *keep in touch*, *keep someone company* and *strong point* are already under *chance*, *ground*, *touch*, *company* and *point*. *Going strong* and *look sharp* belong under *go* and *look*, but those entries do not have them yet. `NEXT.md` now asks the next reading of those verbs to add them.

## Cycle 2: lint

**What this cycle did.** The selector forced a *lint* pass, because ten runs had passed since the last one. A lint pass is the periodic whole-dictionary check. Scripts look for broken links, size limits and one-sided cross-references, and I read the project's own notes for contradictions and loose ends. This was the eighty-seventh pass.

**The mechanical checks.** Every size cap held, and every link inside the knowledge base resolves. The wiki, the project's notes, is close to its 30,000-word cap, so I condensed two older paragraphs of the reviewer-precision note before adding this pass's figures.

The cross-reference tool keeps synonyms, antonyms and "compare" links two-way. When entry A names B, B should name A back in the matching sense. It added five such back-links by itself. It placed ten more on no sense, because it could not tell which sense of the target matched, so I placed them by hand. For example, *put on*, *put together* and *set up* all list *organize* as a synonym, and each now points back from *organize* sense 1 ("plan and arrange an event"). In cycle 1 I had removed *healthy* as a synonym of one sense of *strong*. The rule is that a review that removes a link also removes its mirror, so *strong* came out of *healthy* as well.

The tool that finds "promised" entries reported seven. These are notes that say "this has its own entry" when no entry exists. All seven were false alarms: a queue row already exists under a slightly different name, or the promised word is a second, unrelated word with the same spelling, which the queue cannot name yet. The list of sense pairs for the owner's cross-reference audit (ruling C1) now has 694 items.

**The band vote.** Sixty-three queued words had no frequency band yet. A band is the dictionary's own rating of how common a word is, from 1 (most common) to 5. Three cheap models voted on them for less than one US cent. Only one word split widely: *in.*, the written short form of *inch*, got votes of 1, 3 and 1. I set it at band 2, the same as *inch*, since the short form is not more common than the word. One tool quirk is worth noting: the vote's follow-up steps must be told the file name (`votes-unbanded.tsv`) or they fail.

**Reviewer precision.** Precision is the share of a reviewer's points that I accepted. This was the first lint with real figures for the new reviewer A, the Claude model in place since the owner's ruling of 2026-10-25. It scored 0.86 over 316 decisions. Its weakest areas, coverage (0.60) and grammar codes (0.68), are well above the 30 percent line at which an area is switched off. Reviewer B stays at 0.50 overall. Nothing was switched off or on.

**The reviewer-B test.** The owner's ruling E1 said that after two lint passes under the new house-rules prompt, reviewer B should be compared with another model if it still found fewer than about 0.5 issues per entry. This is the second such pass. Since the prompt changed, reviewer B has raised 1.09 issues per entry over 171 reviews, more than twice the line, so no trial is needed. The status table of the owner's rulings and `NEXT.md` now say so.

**Housekeeping.** Three lines in the curator queue (`reviews/needs_curator.txt`, the list of questions for the owner) asked for old branches to be deleted. The repository now has only `main`, so those lines are marked resolved.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue, the ordered list of words waiting for entries. Under the owner's ruling A2, milestone 1 comes first. Milestone 1 is the set of words used in the dictionary's own definitions. The claim tool gave me the next twelve nouns in queue order, and I wrote all twelve: *jungle*, *jury*, *kidney*, *kindness*, *laboratory*, *laundry*, *lightning*, *liquid*, *makeup*, *mess*, *nest* and *pharmacy*.

Each entry went through the full pipeline. The checks confirmed the plural forms. A panel of models compared the American and British transcriptions, and the American ones were also checked against an open pronouncing dictionary, downloaded for the check and not kept. All 24 transcriptions were confirmed at the first try. Then both reviewers read every field.

Some choices I made while drafting:
- *Laboratory* has a translator note on stress. American English stresses the first part (*LAB-ruh-tor-ee*) and British English the second (*luh-BOR-uh-tree*).
- *Lightning* carries a learner error: the common misspelling *lightening*, which is a different word meaning "making lighter".
- *Pharmacy* has a short note on choosing between *pharmacy*, the American *drugstore*, and the British *chemist's*.
- *Jury* has a culture note for translators. In many countries judges alone decide cases, so the idea of ordinary citizens deciding guilt may need explaining, not just translating.
- *Mess* includes the polite use for animal waste and the military dining room.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 36 points, only 2 of them blocking. I accepted 33 and rejected 3.

Both blocking points came from reviewer B about *lightning* used before a noun, as in *at lightning speed*. That use behaves like an adjective. The house rule is that an entry holds only one part of speech. So the noun entry now just points to a separate adjective entry, and *lightning* (adjective) has been added to the queue.

Reviewer A's suggestions added several things:
- two senses of *nest*: a hiding place for bad people (*a nest of thieves*) and a set of things that fit inside each other (*a nest of tables*)
- the phrases *like lightning*, *a laundry list* (American: a long list of complaints or wants), *make a mess of something*, and, from reviewer B, *kill someone with kindness*

Many small points corrected collocation types. For example, *a hospital laboratory* had been filed as "adjective + noun", but *hospital* is a noun.

The three rejections:
- *Law of the jungle* belongs under *law*, and *stir up a hornets' nest* under *hornet*, by the keyword rule.
- Reviewer A suggested folding the animal-waste sense of *mess* into the "untidy" sense. I kept it separate: it carries a different label, and many languages need a different word for it.

## Cycle 4: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again, so I took the next twelve milestone-1 words from the queue. Milestone 1 is the words the dictionary's own definitions are written in. The twelve were *phrase*, *printer*, *pronunciation*, *quantity*, *relation*, *relative*, *republic*, *retirement*, *scar*, *scent*, *shampoo* and *sickness*, all nouns. Each went through the full pipeline.

Three of the new entries have a short note on choosing between near-synonyms. Learners often get these choices wrong:
- *amount*, *number* or *quantity*: *amount* goes with uncountable nouns, *number* with plurals, and *quantity* with both.
- *relation* or *relationship*: *relationship* for two people, *relations* for groups and countries.
- *illness*, *sickness* or *disease*: *illness* is the general word, *disease* is a named and often serious illness, and *sickness* is usual in fixed names such as *altitude sickness*.

*Pronunciation* records a learner error that is both real and very common: the spelling *pronounciation*, copied from the verb *pronounce*. *Scent* and *shampoo* each have a short pronunciation note for translators. *Scent* sounds exactly like *cent* and *sent*, and *shampoo* is stressed on the second part.

**Pronunciation.** Twenty-three of the 24 transcriptions were confirmed at once. The American *retirement* was disputed. I had written the *tire* part as one syllable, while the open pronouncing dictionary and the house rule both treat it as two (as in *fire*). I rewrote it in the house form, and the check then confirmed it.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 37 points, 7 of them blocking. I accepted 35 and rejected 2.

Four of the blocking points were the same problem, flagged by both reviewers on two fields. One *sickness* example said *we all felt sickness*, which no one says; people say *felt sick*. I replaced the example. The other three blocking points:
- *Quantity* had a second sense for "quality, not quantity", but that is just the ordinary meaning "how much" used in a contrast. It is now a collocation of the main sense.
- The second sense of *relative* (an animal, plant or language that developed from the same early kind as another) was vague and has been redefined.
- A phrase definition in *scent* had a "usually by ..." clause; that clause moved to the explanation field, which is where the house style puts it.

The reviewers also caught things I had missed:
- *3D printer*, now a subsense of *printer*
- the formal *sexual relations* under *relation*
- the uses *the scent of victory* and *on the scent of something*
- an example claiming that in a republic "the head of state is chosen by the people", which is not true of every republic

Both rejections were the same idiom, *turn of phrase*. By the keyword rule it belongs under *turn*, its first noun.

The cross-reference step left four back-links for me to place by hand, on *quality*, *relationship*, *smell* and *health*. It also found that *sickness* listed *disease* as a synonym while *disease* listed *sickness* under "compare". I made them match, using "compare", since the two words are near-synonyms that learners have to choose between.
