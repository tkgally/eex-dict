# 2026-10-21, cycle 3 of the 12:44 UTC run: eight phrasal verbs, *take away* to *try on*

## What this cycle did

This was a **build** cycle: new entries taken from the queue (the ordered list of words still to be written). The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) chose it and allowed up to twelve. The queue is still in the most common phrasal verbs (a verb plus a small word such as *up* or *off*, with a meaning of its own). I claimed twelve and wrote eight:

- *take away* (remove; make a pain stop; subtract; British *to take away* for food; *take away from*, make something seem less good)
- *take back* (return something to a store; accept someone back; say your words were wrong; remind you of the past)
- *take off* (clothes; a plane leaving the ground; become successful; time off work; leave suddenly; an amount off a price)
- *take out* (remove from inside; take someone to dinner; get money or a loan; *take it out on someone*)
- *take over* (get control of; do someone's job after them; fill a place or a mind)
- *take up* (start a hobby; use space or time; accept an offer; deal with a subject; make clothes shorter)
- *throw away* (get rid of; waste a chance)
- *try on* (clothes; British informal *try it on*, behave badly to see what you can do)

Four claimed words were not written, because the dictionary already covers them:

- *Take place* is an idiom, and by the house keyword rule it already sits under *place* (noun) as a phrase.
- *Talk to*, *think about*, and *think of* are verb + preposition patterns already covered by senses of *talk* and *think*. A settled house rule says such a pattern does not get its own phrasal-verb entry.

All four are marked as duplicates in the queue.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two outside reviewer models raised 33 issues. I made 33 decisions: 23 applied and 10 rejected. *Throw away* drew no issues. The main changes:

- *take away*: the food sense (*two burgers to take away*) was marked as taking an object, but both reviewers pointed out that it is usually used without one. It is now "transitive or intransitive", and its definition no longer says the food is bought. The note on British **takeaway** and American **takeout** now says which of them can mean the restaurant.
- *take up*: reviewer-b caught a real error. The opposite of taking up a skirt (making it shorter) is *letting it down*, not *letting it out*, which means making it wider. The link now points to *let down*. The sense for accepting an offer now covers invitations and challenges, and the summary sentence covers all five senses.
- *take back*, *take off*: the summary sentences were rewritten to fit every sense. *Take off* sense 4 now says you *arrange* time away from work.
- *take over* sense 3 now covers a feeling that takes control (*his fear took over*), not only weeds filling a garden.

I rejected, among others: reviewer-b's grammar patterns that are not in our closed list (a settled house rule); its view that the notes on the nouns *takeaway* and *takeout* do not belong in the verb entries (house practice is a one-clause pointer); and reviewer-a's objection to comparing *take out* with *take off*. Learners do confuse the two for clothes, which is exactly what a comparison is for.
