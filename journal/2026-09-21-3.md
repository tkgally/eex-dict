## Splitting entries that mixed two grammatical roles

The most interesting finding: both reviewers, independently, flagged that the entry for *one* mixed the number ("I have one brother") with three uses that are really a different part of speech, a pronoun ("do you have a red one?", "one should always be polite," "the little ones"). The project's rules say one entry holds one part of speech only. I split it: *one* the number keeps its own entry, and a new entry, *one* the pronoun, holds the other three senses, complete with its own pronunciation check, dictionary-form generation, and a full second editorial pass of its own. The same pattern showed up in *because*: a newer, internet-born use ("we stayed up all night, because movies") turned out to work grammatically like a preposition, not the conjunction the rest of the entry covers, so I removed it and added it to the list of words still waiting to be written, this time as its own entry. And the pronoun entry for *it* had picked up a children's-game sense ("tag, you're it!") that is really a noun, not a pronoun; rather than open a third new entry for one narrow use, I moved it into *it*'s list of fixed phrases instead, following the same pattern the project already uses for greetings and sayings that don't share their host entry's grammar.

## Reviewers getting it wrong

Not every flag was right. One reviewer, five separate times on the entry for *see*, insisted that phrases like "see you" and "I see" needed a different kind of definition than the one the project has used successfully in over forty other entries; I rejected all five with that precedent cited. The same reviewer also twice claimed a pronunciation note about the spelling of *one* was false, apparently reading the claim as being about the look-alike word *won* instead — I rejected both and wrote the pattern up for future reference. Elsewhere I found and fixed several notes that used raw phonetic symbols instead of the project's required plain-English pronunciation spelling, and a few notes that overclaimed "in most languages" when the house rule requires a softer "often" or "many."
