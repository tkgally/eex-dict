# Run report, 2026-11-01 (second run)

## Cycle 1: build

**What the cycle did.** The mode selector (`tools/next_mode.py`, the script that picks each cycle's job from what is most overdue) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words still to be added. It allowed up to twelve new entries and US$3 of paid model calls. Nothing was stranded from earlier runs: there were no open pull requests and no leftover `claude/*` branches, the inbox (the folder where the owner leaves instructions) was empty, and there were no reader reports of problems with entries.

The cycle wrote twelve band-2 verbs, the next ones in queue order: *scatter*, *schedule*, *scrape*, *scream*, *seat*, *seek*, *seize*, *select*, *separate*, *settle*, *shift*, and *shiver*. ("Band 2" is the second of five frequency levels the project assigns by model vote; band 1 is the most common words.) Each entry has every sense a learner needs, signposts, grammar codes, two to four examples per sense, collocations, cross-references, and notes only where a learner would otherwise go wrong. *Settle* is the largest, with seven senses (end an argument, decide, go to live, get comfortable, become calm, come to rest, pay). *Schedule* records both the American *sk* and the British *sh* pronunciations, in the same way as the existing noun entry. *Seat* warns against the common mistake *seat down*, and *seek* warns against *seek for*.

**Checks.** Every inflected form came from the rules or the exceptions table (*sought* for *seek*). All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each verb) passed the agreement check: the pronunciation panel of models agreed with our transcription, and the open CMU pronouncing dictionary agreed for American English.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models (reviewer A, a Claude model, and reviewer B, a Google model) raised 85 issues, 14 of them blocking, meaning the entry could not be marked reviewed until each was decided. I applied 71 and rejected 14, with a logged reason for each; none needed the owner. The main fixes:

- *scatter*: the third sense (*farms are scattered across the hills*) was coded as a passive verb use but defined as a state (*be in many places*). Both reviewers caught the mismatch; it is now defined as an action, *spread things over a wide area*, used mostly in the passive.
- *settle*: three examples did not fit their senses. One was transitive (*settled the valley*) in a sense marked intransitive; one used the phrasal verb *settle up* inside an ordinary sense; and one (*ginger can settle your stomach*) made a health claim, which the example rules forbid. All three were replaced. *Settle up* is now linked as a phrasal verb to be written later.
- *shift*: a catch-all sense joined two meanings (*shift a stain* and *shift old stock*), so it was split into two British informal senses. The figurative American *shift gears* (*let's shift gears and talk about next week*) was added as a subsense.
- *seat*: the formal *please be seated* was a separate sense defined as *sit down*, which clashed with its transitive grammar and with *remain seated*. It is now a subsense of the first sense, defined *sit down, or be sitting*.
- *scream* gained an informal sense, *show clearly* (*that house screams money*). *Scrape* in *scrape a pass* is now labeled British.

Rejected objections: reviewer B asked for hand marks on *sought* (the site marks listed forms itself); both reviewers asked for idioms whose keyword is another word (*settle a score*, *when the dust settles*, *scrape the bottom of the barrel*), which by house rule live under their first noun; and reviewer B objected to a closed grammar label. Eighteen links back from older entries (for example from *pick* to *select*, and from *pay* to *settle*) could not be placed by the cross-reference tool, so I placed them by hand in 17 existing entries.

**Spend.** US$0.42 for this cycle; US$1.39 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. Two reviewer calls hit a network hiccup and were retried automatically.

**What is next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order. Lint (the periodic housekeeping pass) is due in six runs.

**For the owner.** Nothing new. The open items in `NEXT.md` ("For the owner") are unchanged.

## Cycle 2: review

**What the cycle did.** After the first cycle merged, the mode selector chose *review*. This is a re-reading of existing entries to find what is missing or wrong. No entries were waiting in `draft` (unreviewed) and there were no reader reports, so the cycle went on with the *coverage pass*. The owner ordered this pass on 2026-10-24: it re-reads the most important band-1 entries, those with the most senses first, and asks whether each entry covers everything a learner needs. This cycle read the three six-sense adjectives that no one had read since that ruling: *bad*, *cool*, and *heavy*. That brings the pass to 133 entries.

**What I added.**
- *bad*: a new sense, *not suitable or convenient* (*Is this a bad time to call?*, *a bad idea*). A subsense for a body part that is injured or painful (*my bad knee*, *a bad back*).
- *cool*: an informal sense for money (*He made a cool million*).
- *heavy*: a subsense for a person, a softer word than *fat* (*He's gotten a little heavy*), and a sense *heavy with* (*branches heavy with snow*). An example of *heavy on* (*a bit heavy on salt*).

I also removed two notes that only repeated other fields. *Bad*'s usage note repeated the forms *worse* and *worst*, which the inflections already show. *Cool*'s translation note repeated the cool/cold comparison and added only generic advice.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The two reviewer models raised 26 issues, one of them blocking. I applied 20 and rejected 6.

The blocking issue was right. The *bad* subsense for spoiled food was coded "used only after a verb", but people say *bad eggs* and *a bad apple*. The code was removed, and the definition now covers drink as well as food.

The reviewers' other points were fixed as follows:
- *Too bad* now also covers the unkind use (*Too bad. You'll have to wait.*).
- *Bad language* was added as a collocation.
- *Cool*'s money sense was redefined as a plain adjective phrase, *surprisingly large*.
- *Heavy*'s *thick, strong, or solid* became *made of thick, strong material*.
- *Heavy*'s learner-error note was softened. *Big rain* does occur, but *heavy* is the usual word.

Two cross-references were questioned: *ill* as a synonym of *bad*, and *slim* as an opposite of *heavy*. Each is also mirrored in the other entry, so I kept them and added a short note saying where each one applies.

I rejected these:
- Requests to add *with a heavy heart* and *heavy going*. The house rule files an idiom under its first noun.
- The claim that *heavy machinery* is not about weight.
- The claim that *bad*'s learner error (*more bad* instead of *worse*) repeats the inflections. The error warns about a mistake that the bare forms do not.

**Spend.** US$0.16 for this cycle; US$1.55 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** One phrase definition used *sympathy*, which is outside the words definitions may use, and the vocabulary check caught it. I rewrote it.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with *feeling* and then the five-sense entries.

**For the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *build* again. I took eight verbs from the queue instead of the allowed twelve. That left room in this run for review and wrap-up without making the entries thinner. The eight verbs are *shower*, *signal*, *sink*, *slice*, *slip*, *snap*, *sniff*, and *soak*. All 16 pronunciations passed the agreement check. The irregular forms of *sink* (*sank*, *sunk*) came from the project's exceptions table. The rules gave *signal* the American forms *signaled* and *signaling*, with a note that British English doubles the **l**.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 42 issues, 9 of them blocking. I applied 34 and rejected 8.

Blocking issues I accepted:
- *Shower* was defined with its own noun (*wash under a shower*). That is circular, so it now reads *wash yourself while standing under water that falls from above*.
- *Sink* was missing the common figurative use *the scandal sank his campaign*. It is now an informal sense, *ruin*.
- The *snap* example *He snapped his fingers* did not fit the sense *move into a position with a sharp sound*. Finger-snapping now has its own subsense.

Other additions:
- The reviewers' coverage checks led to *sink or swim*, a sport sense of *slice* (*he sliced the ball into the trees*), and *slip into bad habits*.
- *Sniff* gained *she sniffed at the offer* and the phrase *not to be sniffed at*.
- One *sink* sense joined *sink your teeth into* and *sink a well*. They are now two senses.

Rejected:
- Four blocking requests for idioms whose keyword is another word: *your heart sinks*, *it slipped my mind*, *let something slip*. By house rule an idiom is filed under its first noun (here *heart* or *mind*), or, if it has no noun, under its first verb (*let*). *Slip your mind* is already in the *mind* entry, so *slip* now points to it. *Let something slip* is added to the list in `NEXT.md` of phrases for *let* when that entry is next reviewed.
- Reviewer B again objected to `usually passive` as a pattern. That value is in the project's closed list of patterns.
- Reviewer B also objected to the format of the inflection note on *signal*. A script writes that note, not the drafter.

Ten links back from existing entries (for example from *break* to *snap* and from *cut* to *slice*) were placed by hand.

**Spend.** US$0.29 for this cycle; US$1.84 of the US$15 daily cap so far today.

**What did not work.** One new definition used *gradually*, which is outside the words definitions may use. I replaced it with *slowly*. `NEXT.md` went one word over its cap and was trimmed.

**What is next.** The next band-2 verbs in queue order, or whatever the selector picks.

**For the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: closure

**What the cycle did.** The selector chose *closure*. This mode builds entries for words that the dictionary already uses but has no entry for yet. There are three sources:
- words used in definitions (the *closure* source, which is empty at present);
- members of a word family named in an existing entry (the *family* source), for example *attacker* listed under *attack*;
- words named in cross-references (the *crossref* source).

The owner's ruling limits closure to bands 1 and 2 for now. I took six band-2 nouns from the family source, in queue order: *actress*, *attachment*, *attacker*, *attendance*, *attraction*, and *auntie*. *Auntie* records *aunty* as a spelling variant. *Attendance* has a translator's note about a false friend: in some languages a word like *assistance* means being present at an event, while in English it means *help*. All 12 pronunciations passed the agreement check.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 11 issues, 5 of them blocking. I applied all 11.
- *Attacker* was defined as *a person who attacks someone*, which uses the headword's own verb. It now reads *a person who uses violence to try to hurt someone*.
- Two examples for the physics sense of *attraction* stated science facts, which the example rules forbid. They were replaced with classroom examples (*The teacher used two magnets to show attraction*).
- *Auntie* stated its regional use, as a polite word for any older woman, twice in two different wordings. The second copy was removed.
- For coverage, *attachment* gained a formal sense for the act of joining one thing to another, and *attacker* gained a computing subsense for someone who breaks into computer systems.
- Some collocation types were corrected (*school attendance* is noun + noun, not adjective + noun).

Two links back from existing entries were placed by hand, from *actor* to *actress* and from *victim* to *attacker*.

**Spend.** US$0.18 for this cycle; US$2.02 of the US$15 daily cap for the day.

**What did not work.** Reviewer B's first reply for *auntie* could not be read: the tool found no list of verdicts in it. I ran that reviewer again on the entry alone and got a clean reply. One definition used *sexual*, which is outside the words definitions may use, and I reworded it.

**What is next.** This was the run's fourth and last cycle. The next run continues with whatever the selector picks: band-2 verbs, more family-source nouns, or the coverage pass at *feeling*.

**For the owner.** Nothing new this run. The standing items in `NEXT.md` ("For the owner") are unchanged.
