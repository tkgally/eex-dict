# Run report, 2026-10-26 (third run of the day)

## Cycle 1: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler (the script that picks each cycle's job from the state of the dictionary) chose *build*: writing new entries from the queue of words still missing. Its reason: building had gone longest without a turn among the jobs allowed to run, and closure (entries for words that only the definitions use) waits until milestone 1 is finished. Milestone 1 is the list of words the dictionary itself uses in its definitions, built first by the owner's ruling of 2026-10-24. Twelve words were taken, the per-cycle limit: the verbs *sow*, *subtract*, *weaken*, and *weed*, and the nouns *acquaintance*, *ancestor*, *beak*, *broom*, *bruise*, *cattle*, *clause*, and *cruelty*.

Each was drafted in full from my own knowledge and the house style guide, then passed through the usual checks: the schema validator (it checks the file's shape and the closed lists of labels and codes), the inflection generator (it writes forms such as *sown* and *cruelties* from rules and a table of exceptions), the pronunciation check (three or four models and an open pronouncing dictionary must agree; all 24 transcriptions agreed), and two independent reviewers who read every field. All twelve entries are now published as `reviewed`. Sixty-six milestone-1 words remain.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** The reviewers raised 37 issues, two of them marked blocking. I accepted 29 and rejected 8, each with a logged reason.

The useful catches:

- The money sense of *weaken* (*the currency weakened against the dollar*) opened its definition with *of money:*. House style keeps such a limit on the subject out of the definition, so it now lives in a separate explanation line. Both reviewers flagged it; it was the only blocking fault.
- The power sense of *weaken* was written as transitive only (*the scandal weakened his position*). One reviewer pointed out that it is also used without an object (*public support has weakened*); the definition now covers both, with a new example.
- *Acquaintance* lacked the use for knowing a person slightly, as in *on first acquaintance* and *a nodding acquaintance*. It is now a sense of its own, and the entry opens with a one-line summary of what its three senses share.
- The plant sense of *broom* was marked uncountable while its definition began *a wild bush*; it is now *countable or uncountable*.
- Notes that named no real trap were removed: a pointer in *weed* that only repeated its see-also link to *weed out*, and a general note in *clause* telling translators to treat the grammar and legal senses separately.
- *Livestock* was listed as a synonym of *cattle*; it is a wider word (it includes sheep and pigs), so it moved to the "see also" list.
- *Break a clause* is not what people say; it is now *breach a clause*.

What I rejected: requests to bold the plural *cruelties* in examples (the site marks listed forms automatically); a change to the note on *sow*'s past participle (that note is written by the inflection script, not by hand); a *formal* label on *sow doubt* (it is ordinary in news and speech); and a request to list *bill* as a synonym of *beak*. The existing entry *bill* is the money word; the bird's bill is a separate word that does not yet have a queue row. I have noted it in the baton file (`NEXT.md`) for a later build.

One cross-reference needed a person: *acquaintance* compares itself with *friend*, and the cross-reference tool could not tell which sense of *friend* to link back from. I added the link by hand to the first sense (*a person you know well and like*).

**Spend.** US$0.32 for this cycle, all on reviewer B and the pronunciation panel; reviewer A runs on the owner's subscription at no billed cost. The day's total is US$2.42 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. The two leftover branches from earlier runs today had nothing unmerged on them and were already listed for pruning.

**What is next.** The next cycle starts from the scheduler again. Milestone 1 has 66 words left.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new this cycle. The open items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" are unchanged.

## Cycle 2: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose *review*: rereading existing entries rather than adding new ones. Under the owner's ruling of 2026-10-24 the review order is: unreviewed drafts first (there were none), then reports from readers (none open), then the "coverage pass". The coverage pass rereads the most important everyday words, those with the most senses first, and asks whether every common present-day use is in the entry. This cycle read four large entries: the verbs *turn*, *start*, and *hold*, and the noun *side*. Each went to both reviewers again, who are now required to list the common uses of the word and name any that is missing, and to check that every example fits the sense it sits under.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 65 issues, 4 marked blocking. I accepted 54 and rejected 11.

What was really missing, and is now added:

- *Hold* had no sense for opinions and judgments: *hold an opinion*, *I hold you responsible*, *some people hold that...*. Opinions had been squeezed into a sense about having a job or a license. That sense was split in two, and the new one covers all three patterns. Both reviewers marked this as blocking.
- *Hold* also lacked *hold someone's attention* or *interest*; it is now a sub-sense.
- *Turn* covered *the leaves turned red* but not *the sun turned the grass brown*, where something makes something else change. That is now a sub-sense.
- *Side* had the British use for a sports team (*the home side*) only as a side remark; it is now a labeled sub-sense. *From side to side* became a phrase of its own instead of a clause in the usage note.
- *To start with* was defined only as introducing a first reason. It also means *at the beginning* (*There were twelve of us to start with*); both are now covered.

Most of the remaining fixes tidied things up. Notes for translators that only said "translate each sense separately" were removed from all four entries, because the house rules ask such notes to name a real trap. Facts stated twice (in a note and again in an example of a learner error) were cut to one place. The made-up learner error *He holded the rope* was removed: the rules forbid invented regular forms of irregular verbs. Some definitions were adjusted so their examples fit: *turn the box upside down* now fits *turn* sense 3, and *time is on your side* now fits the phrase *on someone's side*.

Removing a cross-reference also means removing its mirror in the other entry. *Conclude* is no longer listed as the opposite of *start*, so *start* came out of *conclude*'s list too; the same happened to *side* and *team*.

What I rejected: *start from scratch* and *hold your breath* belong under the nouns *scratch* and *breath* by the house keyword rule (an idiom is filed under its first noun). *Hold your breath* in its literal sense is noted in `NEXT.md` for when *breath* is next read. I also turned down a request to reorder the senses of *turn*: turning around a point is the core meaning the others grow from. And I kept the phrase *to be on the safe side* with its *to*, the form people usually say.

**Spend.** US$0.29 for this cycle; US$2.71 of US$15 so far today.

**What did not work.** One edit failed on my side because of a mistake in a throwaway editing script. Nothing was written, and I redid the edit at once.

**What is next.** The coverage pass continues with the eight-sense words read longest ago: *good*, *play*, *find*, and *move*.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose *build* again, for the same reason as in cycle 1: building had the most time owed to it, and closure waits for milestone 1 (the words the definitions themselves use). Twelve more milestone-1 nouns were drafted and put through every check: *deodorant*, *disgust*, *echo*, *enjoyment*, *frost*, *germ*, *grief*, *hay*, *jewel*, *lever*, *mammal*, and *orphan*. All 24 transcriptions passed the pronunciation check on the first try. All twelve entries are now `reviewed`, and milestone 1 is down to 54 words.

Some entries carry idioms, which the house rule files under the first noun in the phrase: *come to grief*, *give someone grief* and *good grief* under *grief*; *make hay while the sun shines* and *hit the hay* under *hay*; *the jewel in the crown* under *jewel*. Two entries carry a note for translators that names a real trap. In some languages a word that looks like *disgust* means only *upset* or *annoyance*. And *orphan* in English usually means a child who has lost both parents, while some languages use the matching word for the loss of one.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** This was a quiet batch: 20 issues, 2 marked blocking. I accepted 16 and rejected 4.

Accepted, among others:

- Several collocation lists put a noun used as a modifier (*spray deodorant*, *a war orphan*, *a sea mammal*) under the heading *adjective + noun*. They were moved to *noun + noun* or replaced (*a marine mammal*).
- *A gear lever* is mainly British; the American *a shift lever* replaced it, since American English is the dictionary's main variety.
- Two examples sat under the wrong sense. *The frost had killed the young plants* is about cold weather, not the white layer of ice, so it moved to the weather sense. *To my disgust, they had eaten all the cake* describes annoyance rather than disgust, so it became a dirty, smoky hotel room.
- The second sense of *echo* (*his speech had echoes of his father's*) now says plainly that it is something very like an earlier idea, style, or event that reminds you of it.
- A note on *lever* only repeated the two pronunciations already shown, so it was removed.

Rejected: both blocking issues asked for the plural *echoes* to be marked by hand in the examples. The site marks every listed form of the headword automatically, so the request rests on a misreading of the house rules. I also turned down *wheat germ* as a missing sense (it is a specialized food word, not a core use for learners) and a change to one American transcription that the checker had already verified.

The cross-reference tool could not choose a sense for four back-links, so I placed them by hand. *Perfume* now points to *deodorant*, *virus* to *germ*, and *handle* and *switch* to *lever*.

**Spend.** US$0.32 this cycle; US$3.03 of US$15 so far today.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** Whatever the scheduler picks next; milestone 1 has 54 words left.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new.

## Cycle 4: review

**What this cycle did.** The scheduler chose *review* again, so the coverage pass went on. The coverage pass rereads the most important everyday words, those with the most senses first, and adds any common use that is missing. Four eight-sense entries were read, the ones read longest ago: the adjective *good* and the verbs *play*, *find*, and *move*. Both reviewers read each entry in full.

**What the reviewers found and what I decided.** They raised 74 issues, 3 marked blocking. I accepted 51 and rejected 23.

Uses that were really missing, and are now added:

- *I'm good*, meaning *I'm fine* or *no more, thank you*, was mentioned only in the usage note of *good*. It is now a sub-sense with an informal label. *Good and*, as in *make sure the water is good and hot*, is now a phrase.
- *Play* gained two sub-senses. One is touching something without thinking (*stop playing with your phone*, *she played with her hair*). The other is a film or show being shown (*what's playing at the movie theater tonight?*). It also gained the use with a team as the object (*we play the team from the next town on Saturday*).
- *Move* in the feelings sense gained the pattern *her story moved him to help*.

The blocking issues. Sense 8 of *good* opened with limits on its subject in brackets (*of a ticket*, *of a reason*); these moved to a separate explanation line, as house style requires. The definition of *play a joke on someone* was hard to follow; it now reads *do something to someone as a joke or a trick, so that you can laugh at them*.

The biggest tidy-up was in *good*. The fact that its adverb is *well* appeared in five places, and *better* and *best* in three. Each now lives in one place. A note for translators that claimed *most languages* split the senses was removed: it named no specific trap, and the house rules forbid *most* or *all* in claims about other languages. In *find*, the contrast between *find* and *look for* had been stated three times and is now down to two short mentions. *Discover* moved from the *find my keys* sense to the *by chance* sense, where it fits. The rare *chance* (as in *chance upon*) was dropped, together with its mirror link in *chance*.

What I rejected:

- Eight requests from one reviewer to bold the past form *found* in examples. The site marks listed forms automatically.
- *Find fault with* and *find your feet*. They belong under *fault* and *foot* by the house keyword rule.
- One reviewer's claim that *no good* is a noun phrase that belongs in the noun entry. It works like *no better*, so it stays with the adjective.
- A request to drop *affect* as a synonym of *move* in the feelings sense. *Affect* has a sense for exactly that (*we were deeply affected*), so the link is right.

Reviewer B's acceptance rate this cycle was low (half its issues). Most of its rejected issues were the *found* marking requests, a pattern already recorded in the house notes.

**Spend.** US$0.27 this cycle; US$3.30 of US$15 for the day so far.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed.

**What is next.** This was the run's fourth and last cycle (the limit). The coverage pass continues with *call*, *draw*, *close*, and the noun *cut*. Milestone 1 has 54 words left. The next lint pass is due in two runs.

**What needs the owner.** Nothing new this run. The standing items in `NEXT.md` under "For the owner" are unchanged.
