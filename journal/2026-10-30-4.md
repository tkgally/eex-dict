## Cycle 1: review

**What this cycle did.** The selector is the script that picks each cycle's kind of work from the project's state. It chose **review**: reading entries that already exist and improving them. It said this kind of work had fallen furthest behind, and it allowed up to 20 entries and US$3 of paid model calls. No entries were waiting as drafts and no reader had filed a problem report, so the cycle went on with the coverage pass. That is the reading the owner asked for on 2026-10-24: the most important common ("band 1") words, taking the entries with the most senses first, checked for any current meaning, grammar, or fixed phrase that is missing. I read four entries with seven senses each: **level** (noun), **life** (noun), **body** (noun), and **bit** (noun). That brings the pass to 113 entries.

**What I added.** For **level**, a new sense for one way of looking at something (*on a personal level*, *the film works on many levels*) and the phrase *take something to the next level*. For **life**, a sub-sense for life in general (*Life is full of surprises*) and three phrases: *the life of the party*, *larger than life*, and *for dear life*. For **body**, a sense for an object in space (*a celestial body*). For **bit**, a sub-sense for a typical set of activities said without much respect (*the whole big wedding bit*) and the phrase *a bit much*. I also replaced two examples in **body** that stated science facts, which the house style forbids in examples (*The human body is mostly water*, and an example about smoking).

**What the reviewers found.** Every changed entry goes to two reviewing models from different companies (reviewer A and reviewer B), which mark every field. They raised 48 issues, 7 of them "blocking" (an issue that must be settled before the entry counts as reviewed). I read each one and decided: 42 applied, 6 rejected, none sent to the owner.

The blocking issues:
- The pronunciation note on **level** said both **e** sounds are like the **e** in *bed*. The second vowel is weak, as the respelling *LEV-ul* shows. The note now speaks only of the first **e**.
- The amount sense of **level** was marked as usable without *a* or *the*. It is not, so it is now countable only. In the other direction, the height and rank senses were changed to "countable or uncountable" because of *at eye level* and *at government level*.
- **Life** in *battery life* has no article, so that sense is now "countable or uncountable".
- Reviewer B pointed out that **life** had no sense for life in general. This was the cycle's most useful catch, and it is the sub-sense listed above.
- Both reviewers asked for the computing **bit** (a unit of data). I rejected this for the **bit** entry, not on its merits: the computing word comes from a different origin, so by house rule it gets its own entry, `bit-n-2`. It is listed in `NEXT.md` to be claimed by hand.

Many of the minor fixes enforced the rule that one fact lives in one field. The usage notes on **life** and **bit** said again what a learner error and a phrase explanation already said, so both notes were removed. The note on **body** about *somebody* and *everybody* became a learner error (*Every body was there*). The translator notes on **level**, **body**, and **bit** now name a specific trap instead of telling the translator to "translate by sense". On reviewer A's advice, the "upper body" sense of **body** moved below the more common senses. The other rejections: *have the time of your life* belongs under **time**, the first noun in the phrase; the height sense of **level** stays before the rank sense because the other senses build on it; and I kept two parts of **bit** that reviewer A wanted changed: the explanation of *a bit of* stays because it names the real trap, and the *wedding bit* sub-sense stays under the performance sense because it shares that sense's idea of a routine.

## Cycle 2: closure

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose **closure**: writing entries for words the dictionary already uses but does not yet define. It allowed up to 12 entries and US$3. The "closure" list proper is empty, so the words came from the next list in order, "family" rows. These are related forms of words that already have entries, such as the verb **text** beside the noun **text**. I took eight, fewer than allowed, so each could be full: the verbs **stuff**, **text**, **time**, **top**, **trip**, **undertake**, and **undo**, and the noun **accountant**. Each was written from my own knowledge under the style guide and went through the full pipeline: the validator, the inflection tool (the past forms *undid*, *undone*, *undertook* come from the project's table of irregular forms), the pronunciation check (all 16 transcriptions agreed with the panel of models), and the two reviewers.

**What the reviewers found.** 58 issues, 10 blocking. I applied 47 and rejected 11.

The blocking issues were all real:
- In **stuff**, the example *The museum had a stuffed bear near the door* uses **stuffed** as an adjective, not the verb. It became *He had his old dog stuffed after it died*. A usage note about *stuffed animal* toys also belonged to the adjective, so I removed it.
- In **top**, the example *A small tower topped the old church* means "be on top of", but the definition only covered putting something on top. That use is now a sub-sense of its own. *Top a record* is not a natural collocation, so I removed it.
- In **trip**, I had defined the switch sense as "start working". A tripped switch usually cuts the power, as my own example showed. The definition now says *turn on or off suddenly*.

The reviewers' coverage checks also added an informal drug sense to **trip** (*he was tripping*), the formal *be undone by* to **undo**, and the very informal phrase *stuff it* to **stuff**. Ten of the rejections were one flag that both reviewers raised five times each. They asked for hand marks on *undid* and *undone* in examples, but the house style says the site marks every listed form itself. The other rejection was a request to move *usually passive* out of the verb patterns, where the closed list puts it.

The cross-reference tool adds the mirror link on the other entry when an entry names a synonym or an opposite. It could not tell which sense of the other entry to use for five links, so I placed them by hand: **fill** to **stuff**, **beat** to **top**, **promise** to **undertake**, and **fasten** and **tie** to **undo** as opposites.

**Spend.** US$0.25 for this cycle. The day's total is US$3.60 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** Nothing failed. My first draft put a word that is not a status value in the pronunciation status fields. The validator caught it before anything else ran.

**What is next.** More family rows in queue order, and the hand-claimed homographs, now including the computing **bit**.

## Cycle 3: build

**What this cycle did.** The selector chose **build**: new entries from the main queue, in queue order. It allowed up to 12 entries and US$3. I took the next eight common ("band 2") verbs, fewer than allowed, so each could be full: **illustrate**, **imply**, **indicate**, **influence**, **inherit**, **insert**, **insist**, and **insure**. All went through the full pipeline. The inflections all come from the regular rules. All 16 transcriptions were verified. For the British **insure**, I gave `ɪnˈʃʊə` (rhyming with *poor*), which two of the three panel models gave, and listed `ɪnˈʃɔː` (rhyming with *more*) as a variant. That is the reverse of the choice made for **ensure** earlier today, which is still flagged and waiting for the owner in the curator file.

I wrote the notes only where a learner really goes wrong:
- **imply** got a learner error for the listener's mistake (*I implied that she was angry* for *inferred*).
- **influence** got one for *influenced on me*, and a note that it is stressed on the first part, like the noun.
- **insist** got one for *insisted to pay*, and a usage note on the base form after *insist that* (*They insisted that he leave*).
- **insert** got a translator note that the verb and the noun are stressed on different parts.
- **insure** got a short paragraph on choosing between **insure**, **ensure**, and **assure**.

**What the reviewers found.** Only 20 issues, 1 of them blocking, and every one was right, so I applied all 20. The blocking one was in **imply**. Its second sense said "show that something is likely". The real meaning is "have something as a necessary part or result" (*Being a manager implies working long hours*), and it is fixed.

The other fixes:
- **illustrate**: the use with a thing as subject (*This case illustrates how dangerous the road can be*) is now its own sub-sense, separate from a person giving examples.
- **indicate**: a sub-sense for instruments (*The gauge indicated that the tank was almost empty*).
- **inherit**: now also used with no object (*her two sons will inherit*).
- **insist**: the *insist on* sense moved first, because learners meet it more.
- **insure**: an American sense meaning *make sure*.
- *Seem to imply* and *seem to indicate* were moved out of the "adverb + verb" collocation group, where they did not belong.

When an entry names a synonym or an opposite, the cross-reference tool adds the mirror link on the other entry. It could not choose the sense for nine links, so I placed them by hand on **show** (two links), **demonstrate**, **mean**, **point**, **remove**, **add**, **demand**, and **ensure**.

**Spend.** US$0.24 for this cycle. The day's total is US$3.84 of the US$15 cap.

**What did not work.** One small trap in a tool: the cross-reference tool lists the links it cannot place only when it is run with its writing switch (`--apply`). A check without the switch reports none, which can make it look as if the work is done when it is not. This cycle caught it, and the note is in the log for later runs.

**What is next.** More band-2 verbs in queue order, from `interpret-v` on.
