## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The reviewers raised 33 issues, 30 of them marked blocking. I applied 21 and rejected 12. *fear*, *feather*, *ignore*, *document*, and *planet* drew no issues.

Applied, among others:

- *fail*: the test meaning mixed "not pass a test" with "decide that someone has not passed". The second is now a sub-meaning with its own examples (*The teacher failed three students*). *fold* (used mainly of businesses) was dropped as a synonym. *work* moved from the general "not succeed" meaning to the "stop working" meaning, where it is the true opposite. The summary line was reworded.
- *cheat on someone*: the definition said the relationship was secret. It now means to be unfaithful to your husband, wife, or partner with someone else; secrecy is not part of the meaning. The usage note now matches. *Cheat someone* is now "trick someone in order to get money or something else from them", rather than any dishonest taking.
- *fat*, the food meaning: it had treated butter and oil as kinds of fat. It now says "a substance found in foods such as butter, oil, meat, and cheese", and *oil* is no longer listed as a synonym. The example for *the fat is in the fire* was in the future tense, which the idiom rarely takes; it is now in the present.
- *far*: the political meaning (*the far left*, *the far right*) now speaks of the strongest opinions on the left or the right. The grammar notes now allow linking verbs other than *be* (*it seems far*).
- *father*: an awkward example (*the father of the whole project*) was replaced.

Rejected:

- The second reviewer called *feel cheated* an adjective three times. It is the verb's past participle, and the house rule files such a phrase as a collocation of the verb.
- A claim that *the father of something* wrongly excludes women. For a woman the usual phrase is *the mother of*.
- *Pass* stays the opposite of the test meaning of *fail*, because *pass* also means deciding that someone has passed.
- The learner error *My house is far* stays. Using *a long way* in positive sentences is the natural form and a common learner trap.
