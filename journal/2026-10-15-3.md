Several of these are small grammar words whose meaning is easy but whose use is hard, so most of the work went into the explanation fields and the learner-error boxes:

- *either* (adverb) is the negative partner of *too*: *I don't like it either*, not *I don't like it too*. The entry compares it with *too*, *also*, and *neither*, and its learner errors cover both *don't ... too* and *don't ... neither*.
- *else* comes after the word it changes (*someone else*, *what else*) and takes the possessive itself (*someone else's bag*). It holds the phrases *or else* and *if nothing else*.
- *enough* as an adverb follows its adjective (*big enough*, never *enough big*); it also has a "fairly" sense (*nice enough*) and the sentence use *strangely enough*.
- *even* has a "surprising" sense and a "stronger comparison" sense (*even better*), the phrase *even so*, and a learner error for *Even he is rich, ...* (it must be *even though*).
- *ever* is limited to questions, negatives, comparisons, and *if* (*Have you ever ...?*), with a learner error for *I have ever eaten sushi*.
- *effectively* and *eventually* carry false-friend warnings for translators: in several European languages, look-alike words mean "indeed" and "possibly".

All 24 pronunciations (American and British for each word) were confirmed by the pronunciation panel on the first try, and the comparative *earlier*, *earliest* came from the project's exceptions table.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

The two model reviewers raised 28 issues, 21 of them blocking. I accepted 20 and rejected 8; none had to go to the owner.

Accepted, among others:

- *easily*: I had defined the sense in *He gets angry easily* as "very quickly". The reviewer was right that it means "when only a small thing makes it happen"; the definition and its short label were changed. I had also called *I can do it easy* simply wrong; *easy* after a verb is common informal American English, so the note now says it is informal rather than wrong.
- *enough*: the sense in *The hotel was comfortable enough* was defined as "to a fair degree, but not very". It means an acceptable degree, so it now reads "fairly, or to a degree that is acceptable but not high".
- *either*: the explanation said the word "adds a second negative to a first one", which could be misread as a double negative. It now says the clause agrees with a negative statement just made.
- *else*: the definition is now in the house pattern for grammar words ("used after words such as ... to mean *other*, *different*, or *more*").
- *even*: *still* was listed as a synonym of *even* in *even better*; it is now a word to compare, with a note on *better still*.
- *everywhere*: the claim that no preposition ever comes before it was too strong (*from everywhere* is fine); the rule now covers *look*, *search*, and verbs of movement.

Rejected, with reasons logged:

- *everywhere* can mean "in a lot of places" (*There were flowers everywhere*); that use stays in the definition.
- *the downstairs* (*the downstairs needs painting*) is a real noun, so it stays in the word family of *downstairs*.
- The note that *or else!* standing alone is informal is in the phrase's explanation; a label on the whole phrase would be wrong.

One reviewer reply (reviewer-b on *ever*) came back empty and was re-run; the second reply found no issues.

The mirror-link tool added *ever* as a synonym on *always* ("at all times"), which is the right sense.
