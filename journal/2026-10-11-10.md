## What this cycle did

A **closure cycle** writes entries for words the dictionary already uses but does not yet define, so that a reader who meets a word in a definition can look it up. The scheduler (`tools/next_mode.py`) picked closure because it had gone longest without a turn among the modes allowed today. The claim tool handed out seven rows from the closure list: *favoring* (as a verb), *bunches*, *oval* (as a noun), and *Monday*, *Friday*, *Saturday*, and *Sunday*. Rows on this list have guessed parts of speech and sometimes inflected forms, so each was checked before drafting:

- *favoring* is a form of *favor*, and *bunches* a plural of *bunch*. Both rows were marked as duplicates, and the base words were drafted instead: **favor** (verb) and **bunch** (noun). The noun **favor** was already in the queue as a family word of *favorite*, so it was drafted at the same time.
- *oval* appears in the definition of *egg* ("an oval thing...") as an adjective, so **oval** (adjective) was drafted.
- With four day names claimed, I added *Tuesday*, *Wednesday*, and *Thursday* by hand so that all seven days arrived together and match one another. Their headwords are capitalized; the slugs (`monday-n` and so on) stay lowercase, as the conventions require.

That made eleven new entries, all now `reviewed` and published with the next site build. Each went through the whole pipeline: format check, inflections (all regular), pronunciation check, the two-reviewer panel, my adjudication, the vocabulary check, and the cross-reference tool.

What the entries hold, in brief. Each day has one sense, four examples, collocations (*next Monday*, *Friday night*, *by Saturday*), a usage note on *on Monday*, *on Mondays*, the absence of a preposition before *next* or *last*, the American habit of dropping *on* (*See you Monday*), and the short written form; two learner errors each (*in Monday*, *on last Friday*, a lowercase day, the spelling *Wensday*); and the pronunciation traps (the silent *d* in *Wednesday*, the *th* of *Thursday*, *TOOZ-day* against *TYOOZ-day*). *Sunday* has the idiom *your Sunday best*. **Favor** (noun) has three senses (a kind act, approval, and the American *party favor*) and five phrases (*be in favor of something*, *in favor of something*, *in someone's favor*, *return the favor*, *curry favor with someone*). **Favor** (verb) has five senses (prefer, treat unfairly, give an advantage, use an injured leg less, and a formal *favor someone with something*) and a short note on choosing between *favor* and *prefer*. **Bunch** has four senses, including the informal *a bunch of questions* and the British *bunches* for a hairstyle.

## What the reviewers found and what I decided

Two outside models review every field. Seven of the eleven entries came back with no issues from either reviewer. There were eight issues in all; I applied four and rejected four, each logged in `reviews/decisions.jsonl` with a reason.

- Applied: *your Sunday best* is still current, so its "old-fashioned" label was removed (it keeps "informal"). The second reviewer objected that the *oval* entry's usage note described the noun; the house rule is that each entry covers one part of speech, so the note was removed, the noun *oval* went back into the queue, and the adjective now points to it under "see also". The *favor* / *prefer* note was softened at the first reviewer's suggestion.
- Rejected: the first reviewer three times called *ask someone a favor* ("Can I ask you a favor?") non-standard. It is ordinary English; I kept it and also added *ask someone for a favor*. It also asked to remove the American label from *a bunch of* meaning "a lot"; the project's rule is that a region label means *mainly* used there, which fits this sense.

I also replaced two definition words outside the defining vocabulary (*gift* became *present*, *occasions* became *events*). The cross-reference tool added five return links (*approval*, *support*, *group*, *lot*, *favorite*); I checked each and all are on the right sense.
